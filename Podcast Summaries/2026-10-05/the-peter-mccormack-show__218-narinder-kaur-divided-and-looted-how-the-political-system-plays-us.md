# #218 - Narinder Kaur - Divided & Looted: How the Political System Plays Us — The Peter McCormack Show

## TL;DR
Peter McCormack and Narinder Kaur, a left-leaning commentator and daughter of Indian immigrants, explore how political and media systems fuel division while the state enriches the wealthy. They find common ground on economics—agreeing that endless government borrowing and inflated currency hollow out the middle class—but clash on immigration, minimum wage, and the rise of right-wing figures like Rupert Lowe. The conversation argues for cross-ideological dialogue and identifies the state itself as the primary obstacle to prosperity.

## Key points
- Peter and Narinder agree that social media algorithms reward rage and controversy, leading to monetized clickbait and political polarization; both admit feeling complicit at times, and Narinder says rage-baiting accusations against her anti-racism activism are weaponized.
- Narinder shares her lived experience as the only brown child at George Simpson's High School in Newcastle during the 1980s–90s, enduring severe racism and bullying, which shaped her lifelong fight against racism.
- Peter mentions his son's white male friends feel discriminated against by institutional 'box-ticking' and shame, but Narinder counters that statistical institutional racism still disadvantages minorities, citing that a Black or brown applicant with a non-white name must work '10 times as hard.'
- As a matchmaker, Narinder reports unrealistic dating standards across all ages: women often demand a man who is 6'4", wealthy, with hair, while men prioritize looks; she questions the entitlement and warns that high-value men won't choose women who don't bring equivalent qualities.
- Narinder advises that if a woman wants children, the most important action is to find a good partner in her 20s, because female fertility peaks in the 20s and declines significantly by mid-30s; she supports choice but wants realistic education.
- On immigration, Narinder states that UK net migration has averaged 600,000–800,000 per year over the past decade, reaching around 1 million under Boris Johnson, but argues immigration is not new and the country needs workers; she says the real issue is lack of safe routes and processing for asylum seekers.
- Narinder challenges the framing of small-boat arrivals as illegal, pointing out that Ukrainians were given visas and safe routes while brown and black asylum seekers are not; she calls for faster processing, more safe routes, and returns agreements for rejected claims.
- Peter explains the Cantillon effect: when the government or banks create money, the wealthy receive it first and buy assets, causing prices to rise while wages lag, which he says is 'hollowing out' the country and making life unaffordable.
- Peter argues that the UK is not overtaxed but has a spending problem; he notes interest on national debt is £140 billion per year, more than the £80 billion education budget, and says every government buys votes with unfunded promises.
- Peter compares house prices: his father bought a three-bed house in Kempston, Bedford, for £12,000 on a £3,500 salary (3.5x income) in the 1970s; the equivalent house now costs £380,000 while equivalent salary is £35,000 (10x), showing how homeownership has moved out of reach.
- On minimum wage, Peter says if £15/hour forced his coffee shop into loss, he wouldn't run it at a loss and might close; Narinder counters that business owners with luxury cars and private school fees claim they can't pay staff more while still profiting, and that government overheads, not wages alone, make businesses unviable.
- Narinder argues that Indian immigrant businesses succeeded by using unpaid family labor, not by underpaying employees; she says no business should rely on paying workers below a living wage and that employers should share profits with staff.
- Peter admits he has not voted in the last three UK general elections because he sees all major parties as variants of decline; he would only vote for a leader who stops deficit spending and shrinks the state, citing Javier Milei's Argentina as a model.
- Narinder describes herself as politically homeless, having left Labour over Shabana Mahmood's immigration policy; she admires Zac Goldsmith's honesty and would support Green policies but fears their economics, while acknowledging Modi's economic success in India despite right-wing associations.
- On women's safety, Narinder argues that women and girls are overwhelmingly at risk from partners or men they know, not immigrants; she criticizes those who use immigrant sex crimes to distract from the broader epidemic of violence against women.
- Narinder says she cannot safely attend a Tommy Robinson march as a brown woman, and that such movements enable racism even if leaders aren't personally racist; Peter counters that there were black attendees and Tommy condemned troublemakers, but agrees fringe elements cause problems.
- Peter and Narinder agree that the true enemy is the state itself, which tells voters what they want to hear and then enriches the wealthy; Narinder says a public revolution is getting nearer as middle-class people unite, and they plan future conversations to build common ground.
- In a brief Bitcoin explanation, Peter argues that technology is deflationary but prices keep rising because money is printed; he says Bitcoin's fixed supply of 21 million means assets priced in Bitcoin have fallen over time, offering an alternative to inflation.
