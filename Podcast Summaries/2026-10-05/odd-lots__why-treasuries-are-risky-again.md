# Why Treasuries Are 'Risky' Again — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal speak with University of Chicago economist Carolin Pflueger about her research measuring the market's perceived Fed policy reaction function. The episode pairs that framework with her work on Treasury bond risk, arguing that bonds have swung back toward the risky, stock-correlated behaviour they showed before 2000 — meaning a 60/40 portfolio has 'nowhere to hide'.

## Key points

- The setup: the 30-year yield sits around 5.59%, the highest since 2002, and has kept rising even after a Fed rate hike — raising doubts about whether policy is tightening financial conditions as intended.
- Pflueger defines the 'policy reaction function' as what markets or observers expect the Fed to do in response to economic conditions; if it is well understood, markets move rates in the direction the Fed intends, effectively doing the Fed's work before the next FOMC meeting.
- Her joint work with Michael Bauer (San Francisco Fed) and Adi Sundaram (Harvard Business School) measures the perceived reaction function two ways: regressing Blue Chip forecaster expectations of the funds rate on their inflation and output assumptions, and event-study regressions of yield moves on inflation surprises.
- The two methodologies usually agree, which she treats as strong validation.
- The perceived reaction function is not stable over time: after late-2011 date-based forward guidance, funds-rate forecasts collapsed to zero with essentially no dispersion across forecasters.
- In 2020-2021 the perceived reaction function was extremely flat. In May 2021 inflation surprised by about half a percentage point — roughly 6% annualised — and the two-year yield did not move at all, even among forecasters who expected higher inflation.
- Perceptions only caught up after the Fed acted: the perceived inflation response rose from roughly zero to one between early 2022 and late 2023. She calls this the 'learning from actions' channel.
- Perceived response to output tends to be steeper during tightening cycles and flatter during easings, which are typically sudden with little expected follow-through.
- On bond risk: Treasury bonds were not always safe. In the 1970s-1990s they behaved more like stocks (positive correlation, the era of bond-market vigilantes and a large inflation risk premium). Post-2000 they were safe, negatively correlated with equities. In the most recent period that bond risk has risen again.
- The practical implication is that when bonds and stocks are positively correlated there is nowhere to hide in a mixed portfolio — part of why 2022 was so painful for 60/40 investors.
- She links the shift in bond risk to the nature of shocks and to monetary policy, and asks whether it reflects luck or policy behaviour, in work with John Campbell and Luis Becerra at Harvard.
