## Key Points

- Solomon pushes back on the "AI job apocalypse" narrative, arguing technology historically creates new opportunities even as it eliminates specific roles — "it's never different this time."
- Goldman is shifting junior analysts away from PowerPoint production toward client-facing work, using AI to handle routine tasks, but worries the apprenticeship model for developing foundational knowledge is at risk.
- Goldman has approximately 2,400–2,500 summer interns plus a comparable number of permanent entry-level hires annually; entry-level hiring in certain industries is down ~16% per Stanford data.
- Human relationships remain the firm's core competitive advantage — Goldman won the SpaceX IPO lead role through 20 years of consistent engagement, not a recent pitch.
- AI data quality is a critical limitation: Solomon recounted AI models giving wrong answers about Masters golf winners when trained on unreliable internet data, illustrating the "garbage in, garbage out" problem.
- On Alphabet's ~$90 billion equity raise, Solomon says massive AI infrastructure capital needs justify equity issuance; companies must think about debt vs. equity over 5–10 year horizons.
- Solomon defends the trend of companies staying private longer — it isn't "capitalism-breaking" — but advocates for regulatory reforms to make public markets more attractive.
- Interpersonal and emotional intelligence skills will become more valuable, not less, as automation advances and routine cognitive work is automated away.

## Overview

Goldman Sachs Chairman and CEO David Solomon joins Odd Lots hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway to discuss how AI is reshaping banking — from junior analyst workflows to capital markets. Solomon argues against predictions of mass white-collar unemployment, instead emphasizing that Goldman is redeploying junior talent toward higher-value client interaction while using AI to automate rote tasks. He frames human relationship-building as Goldman's irreplaceable competitive moat, citing the SpaceX IPO relationship as a 20-year proof point. The conversation spans AI data quality challenges, the logic behind large equity raises for AI infrastructure investment, and the structural dynamics keeping companies private longer.

## Implications

AI is accelerating a shift in what entry-level finance workers are paid to do — from document production to relationship cultivation — which changes both hiring criteria and training models for major banks. The "garbage in, garbage out" problem Solomon highlights suggests that firms with proprietary, clean datasets will have a durable AI advantage over those relying on public internet data. The sustained trend of companies staying private longer, combined with massive private capital deployment for AI infrastructure, is reshaping where and how capital formation happens — with implications for public market health and retail investor access.

## Notable Quotes

- "It's never different this time." — Solomon on why he rejects AI job apocalypse predictions, invoking the historical pattern that technology disrupts but ultimately creates new roles.
- "The telephone is one of the greatest pieces of technology in the world." — Solomon emphasizing that a personal phone call still carries far more impact than email or text in relationship-driven businesses.
- "Building trusting relationships over time" — Solomon's description of how Goldman won the SpaceX IPO mandate, not through recent pitching but through two decades of consistent engagement.
- "Garbage in, garbage out." — Solomon on AI model reliability when trained on unstructured or unreliable internet sources, illustrated by wrong answers about Masters golf winners.
- "It's not capitalism-breaking." — Solomon's characterization of the trend of large companies staying private longer, framing it as a rational market structure choice rather than a systemic failure.
