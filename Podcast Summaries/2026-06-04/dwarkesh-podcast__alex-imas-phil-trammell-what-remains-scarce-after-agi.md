## Key Points

- The **relational sector** — goods and services where human involvement is intrinsically part of the value (e.g., a human barista, a doctor who listens, a live performer) — is the most plausible candidate for what remains scarce after broad automation.
- Labor's share of GDP has remained remarkably stable at ~60-65% across centuries of automation (a "Kaldor fact"), though whether this holds under AGI-scale automation is the central open question.
- Economists consistently underestimate how new demand and new varieties of goods emerge after automation; Ricardo's Industrial Revolution predictions of mass unemployment proved wrong as new sectors absorbed labor.
- The "messy middle" scenario — where automation displaces jobs faster than wealth is created to redistribute — is considered unlikely because automating enough white-collar work to cause political disruption would necessarily generate enormous surplus, making redistribution feasible.
- **Universal basic capital** (distributing ownership stakes) is preferred over pure UBI because it gives people property rights rather than dependence on a political check, though indexing challenges (what exactly to put in the portfolio) complicate this.
- A key underexplored risk: wealthy individuals with **insatiating preferences for capital accumulation** (e.g., Elon Musk reinvesting in data centers rather than consuming) could, especially if longevity is achieved, compound their wealth to dominate the economy, driving capital share toward 100%.
- For developing nations, the recommended strategy is to **buy a broad index** of the global economy rather than attempt targeted retraining or narrow bets on AI production chains — "Nigeria just needs to buy the index."
- Whether AI follows the **electricity model** (gains broadly distributed to users) vs. the **social media model** (rents captured by platforms) is the pivotal structural question for wealth distribution.
- Current empirical data shows little evidence of mass white-collar automation — any signal (e.g., fewer junior developer hires) is below trend growth, not an absolute level shift.
- We lack critical data: consumer demand elasticities for automated vs. human-produced goods, real-time job creation/destruction tracking, and conjoint analyses of willingness to pay for human-in-the-loop services.

## Overview

Economists Alex Imas (Director of AGI Economics at Google DeepMind, University of Chicago) and Phil Trammell (Head of Economics at Epoch, Stanford) join Dwarkesh Patel to work through the core economic questions of an AGI transition: what will be scarce, what happens to wages and labor share, and how should societies redistribute AI-generated wealth. The conversation is explicitly scenario-focused rather than predictive, emphasizing how much foundational data is missing and how historically poor economists have been at forecasting labor market transitions.

The guests identify the "relational sector" as the most durable candidate for human scarcity — not because humans are scarce inputs like horses, but because some consumers intrinsically value human involvement. However, they stress this only matters if enough sectors exhibit strong enough preferences for human presence, and that data on this is almost entirely absent. The broader arc of the discussion maps out a spectrum from "labor share holds" to "greedy capital optimizers dominate," with developing countries and redistribution mechanisms analyzed across these scenarios.

## Implications

The stability of labor share through centuries of automation does not guarantee it survives AGI-scale change — the qualitative shift of fully automating entire supply chains (network-adjusted capital share going to 1) would be unprecedented. The practical implication is that redistribution design matters enormously now, before the transition accelerates: property-based approaches (universal basic capital, consumption-tax-funded sovereign stock purchases) are structurally more robust than income transfers. For developing countries, the window to acquire indexed stakes in the global economy is open but narrowing, especially as private company dominance makes indexing harder and open models (the main democratizing force) may be only months behind frontier rather than permanently accessible.

## Notable Quotes

- "Services and goods where the fact that a human was in the loop is actually part of the value of that product... because humans are naturally scarce, if we have automation where a lot of other things stop being scarce, we will still have scarcity in things that humans are involved in and in the loop for." — Alex Imas

- "If we have the technology to automate so many jobs that it becomes a new kind of political problem, then the pie will also be growing really fast." — Phil Trammell

- "The pessimistic framing of Moore's law is every 18 months, the value of computation has halved. We're just running out of uses for computation so fast that it's sustaining Moore's law." — Dwarkesh Patel

- "Nigeria just needs to buy the index. And Nigeria has access to AGI because of the open models." — Phil Trammell

- "When that [labor income] is no longer the case, and we are now at the mercy of the elected official for basic needs — that feels like a power sharing arrangement that's really dangerous." — Alex Imas (on UBI's political vulnerability)

- "We don't have any data. I've been saying we need a Manhattan project for data. We don't have data on basically consumer demand elasticities." — Alex Imas

- "The more you think AGI is going to be something our economy runs on the way our economy currently runs on electricity, the more it looks like electricity, and the more it's like every company in the S&P of the future has leveraged AI — and then you're indexed again." — Alex Imas

- "It's very easy for somebody to go on a podcast and say these jobs that you like, they're going away. It's harder for somebody to spin up a utopia, which doesn't exist yet." — Phil Trammell
