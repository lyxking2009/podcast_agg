## Overview

Helima Croft and John Soughan examine why oil prices remain surprisingly subdued despite what Croft calls "the largest physical energy supply disruption in history." Three months into the U.S.-Iran conflict, over one billion barrels of cumulative supply have been lost, yet Brent crude sits below levels seen during the Russia-Ukraine crisis. The episode unpacks the mechanics behind this disconnect and warns that the defenses buffering prices are running out.

## Key Points

- The conflict has removed 10–12.5 million barrels per day from markets, exceeding the severity of the Russia-Ukraine supply shock, yet prices have not reflected this magnitude.
- Pre-war inventory builds and the largest-ever coordinated IEA stockpile release provided a significant "barrel buffer," but those reserves are now rapidly depleting.
- Since May 6, markets have repeatedly sold off on rumors of imminent peace agreements that have never materialized — a pattern Croft calls the "memento mindset" of expecting quick resolution.
- Core negotiation stalemates remain unresolved: uranium enrichment capabilities, stockpile levels, sanctions relief scope, and Iran's demand for operational control of the Strait of Hormuz.
- Congressional mandates govern key Iran sanctions, limiting the President's unilateral authority to offer relief — unlike the more flexible executive-branch sanctions applied to Russia and Venezuela.
- Iran's IRGC may strategically benefit from prolonged negotiations, collecting transit fees and restoring military capabilities during ceasefire pauses.
- ADNOC CEO Sultan Al Jaber stated that returning to 80% of pre-war production levels requires a minimum of four months after conflict resolution — a timeline markets have largely dismissed.
- Global crude inventories are on track to hit historically low levels by October 2026, potentially falling to 30–40 days of cover, threatening normal refinery operations.

## Implications

The repeated market sell-offs triggered by unsubstantiated deal headlines suggest that optimism is priced in while the physical supply deterioration is not. As inventories drain through summer, the disconnect between paper prices and physical reality could compress sharply — the June–July–August period will "seriously stress-test" markets. Any lasting deal faces structural obstacles in Congress and unresolved Iranian demands over Strait sovereignty, making a swift resolution far less likely than current price signals imply.
