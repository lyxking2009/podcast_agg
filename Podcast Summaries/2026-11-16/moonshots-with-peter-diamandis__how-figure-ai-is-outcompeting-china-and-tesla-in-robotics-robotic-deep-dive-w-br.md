# How Figure AI Is Outcompeting China and Tesla in Robotics | Robotic Deep-Dive w/ Brett Adcock — Moonshots with Peter Diamandis

## TL;DR
Brett Adcock, CEO of Figure AI, details the company's push to ship humanoid robots into homes and commercial settings, arguing Figure is ahead of Tesla and China because its Helix neural-network stack does end-to-end locomotion and manipulation rather than teleoperated demos. Figure has over 1,500 robots on campus, is testing zero-shot home tasks, and plans to launch Figure 4 as an 'iPhone moment' with near-human hands sometime soon, targeting $300–$500 monthly leases. Key milestones include the K-cup neural-net demo that unlocked ~$700M in funding, Helix 2.5 scaling laws, and a 2026-2027 timeline for long-horizon autonomous home work.

## Key points
- Brett Adcock frames the humanoid race in four chapters: build functional hardware, build an AI recipe, scale intelligence, and scale robots. Figure is now focused on chapters three and four.
- There are over 1,500 Figure robots on campus versus roughly 700–800 humans. Visitor bots autonomously walk, open doors, use stairs and elevators, greet people, and have a stretch goal of getting coffee and eventually letting visitors check in without interacting with a human.
- Figure 3 can already fold laundry, do dishes, unload groceries, open refrigerators and ovens, and clean up, but the main constraint is intelligence: the robot must do end-to-end zero-shot work in unseen homes for 12+ hours per day, seven days a week for years.
- Testing is underway in 20–100 new California rental homes since the launch of Helix 2.5, with robots performing tasks like making beds and unloading dishwashers through open-ended speech prompts in homes never seen before.
- Helix 3 was previewed to only a few outsiders in a secret 'Black Box' lab and is described as a 'baby AGI moment' or 'a two-year-old in an adult body.' Adcock hopes within six months to put a robot into any home and do fairly large long-horizon tasks end-to-end.
- Home launch criteria require an Apple-quality product with no teleoperation, no failures, and a magical experience. Figure is aiming for a $300–$500 per month lease (under $1 per hour) and says the average person wants at least two robots.
- Figure 4 is described as the 'iPhone 1 moment' and will make Figure 3 look like a caveman. Hardware focuses on lower cost and greater capability, especially hands: after seven iterations of tendon- and non-tendon-driven hands, a Figure 4 hand with a glove is almost indistinguishable from a human hand.
- The Keurig cup demo where Figure 1 placed a K-cup into a Keurig using a single neural net for the upper body was pivotal: it convinced the team deep learning works for robotics and led to raising just under $700 million.
- Transitioning from C++ code to neural networks took about a year. Figure 1 had upper-body neural nets, Helix 1 was upper body, and Helix 2 achieved full-body neural nets. The team learned that joint trajectories for feet, hips, hands, and fingers must be trained together as a unified experience.
- Adcock says he hasn't seen much from Tesla in two or three years. China's impressive dancing robots are trained via deep mimic reinforcement learning from motion capture, run open-loop, don't react to the environment, and their AI stack 'can be done by a senior in college' — not practical for general robotics.
- General-purpose models like GPT/Astra are not real robotics models: they run slowly on fixed camera frames, often with sped-up video, and cannot handle the high-dynamics, millisecond-level control Figure needs. All Helix inference runs on-board the robot, not on Wi-Fi or cloud.
- Figure spends nine figures a year on compute and is doubling it. A deal with Scale for 2027 includes 100,000 GPUs and $3+ billion, which Adcock estimates will be over 100x current training compute.
- Helix 2.5 demonstrated zero-shot work in 30 unseen rental homes and showed the first humanoid scaling laws for data: model losses came down log-linearly as data increased, meaning more compute and data will make robots smarter.
- Current manufacturing capacity is about 40,000 units per year across four lines. Figure 4 was designed to enable lights-out manufacturing where robots control machines to build robots. Adcock sees a path to 1 million units per year if the scaling intelligence problems are solved.
- Adcock projects the world could hold about 20 billion humanoids in a first phase: roughly 3.2 billion human workers scaling to 5–10 billion plus at least one robot per human. At about $1 per hour, robots will be deflationary and create abundance.
- Safety is handled from non-volatile memory rules up through system architecture for physical and semantic safety, with 40+ actuators that must not fail near kids or pets, plus a full cybersecurity team. Figure will not do defense work, choosing to focus entirely on civilian applications.
- Hark AI, Adcock's separate company, provides speech-to-speech AI and is Figure's voice interface. Hark Pro launched about a month ago, revenue is growing fast, and Adcock predicts Hark could be bigger than Figure within six months, possibly 90 days, by revenue. Hark has hardware in the lab and aims to replace phones/computers as the centerpiece AI device.
- OpenAI led Figure's Series B with Microsoft but never showed up to the office, progress was lopsided, and OpenAI wanted to get back into robotics. Figure ended the partnership about two years ago. Adcock believes Jony Ive is likely working on AI devices at OpenAI, not humanoids.
- On regulation, Adcock has seen only the FCC ban on Chinese humanoids in the last 90 days and no broader humanoid-specific regulation. He is focused on launching a safe, high-quality product rather than flying to Washington every week.
