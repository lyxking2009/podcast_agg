# Democrats Are Back. Is Crypto in Trouble? | Justin Slaughter — Bankless

## TL;DR
Justin Slaughter, senior advisor at Paradigm, assesses the political landscape after Democrats retook the House and Senate with a large majority. He argues the big victory is better than a narrow one for crypto because it brings younger, more moderate members less hostile to the industry, but warns that the next two years will bring broad oversight of tech and crypto, with Elizabeth Warren as Senate Banking Chair and Maxine Waters drafting a crypto bill that may be tougher on DeFi. Slaughter advises crypto to put the past behind, focus on key regulatory wins, and engage Democrats by stressing use cases like stablecoins and 24/7 trading, while recognizing that the generational shift favors crypto.

## Key points
- Democrats won a large majority in the 2026 midterms: 260 House seats, net gain of 56, giving them control of both chambers. Justin Slaughter says this broad victory is actually better for crypto than a narrow one because it brings in many young, moderate, and non-white freshmen who are less likely to hold anti-crypto views; names like James Tallarico, Scott Colum, and Troy Jackson are cited as examples of Democrats with crypto-friendly records.
- The Democratic oversight agenda will be broad, targeting tech, AI, prediction markets, agriculture, trade, China, private equity, and crypto, similar to Gary Gensler's SEC where 'everything's a priority.' This dispersion means crypto is less likely to be a sole focused target, but there will be significant oversight of some crypto actors, particularly Trump-affiliated companies, SEC-dismissed cases, token exchanges, and World Liberty.
- A key internal Democratic debate is whether Trump is a unique threat or a symptom of broader 'rot' from neoliberal policies. If Trump is seen as unique, oversight focuses on Trump and his companies; if seen as a symptom, it could expand to all companies, including crypto firms. This fight will play out over the next six months.
- Congress has limited bandwidth for oversight: staffers are not using advanced AI (the Senate only got access to Claude in October), and the goal of having CEOs testify every week is unrealistic. This reduces the likelihood of sustained, damaging hearings on crypto.
- Elizabeth Warren, now chair of the Senate Banking Committee, has her first gavel and still holds influence, though her power has waned since 2021. She remains a leading voice for economic populism and continues to argue crypto lacks positive use cases; Slaughter notes about 35 people in DC strongly dislike crypto, but they are influential in nonprofit and academic circles.
- Maxine Waters, incoming chair of House Financial Services, is preparing a crypto bill that will not be as industry-friendly as Clarity. It may weaken broker-dealer rules and be tougher on DeFi, giving the SEC more power than CFTC, but could include provisions the industry accepts, such as monthly attestation of reserves and 24-hour payouts.
- The SEC and CFTC are now led by Mike Selig and Paul Atkins, respectively, reducing Congress's ability to force agency action. However, Democrats could use funding riders to block rulemakings, and final rules can be undone via Congressional Review Act or lawsuits in the next administration.
- Crypto owners swung dramatically in 2024: data cited by Slaughter shows they were 4% more Democratic in 2020 and 13% more Republican in 2024, a 17-point swing. Some Democrats argued the 'crypto voter' is fake and the swing was due to immigration and inflation, but Slaughter warns that repeating this mistake could re-ignite anti-crypto sentiment.
- The 2026 election showed that campaign money has limits: Fairshake spending was less effective than in 2024, and a super PAC is no match for a wave election. Slaughter advises the industry not to rely solely on political spending to secure policy.
- Slaughter emphasizes that crypto is a technology with persistent use cases: faster cheaper payments, peer-to-peer transactions, novel ownership of digital goods, provenance in AI era, and 24/7 trading (citing Hyperliquid tracking oil prices during weekend bombing). He argues those who still want to destroy it are living in the past.
- To win in the next two years, Slaughter recommends crypto put the past behind (stop litigating Biden-era decisions), focus on what matters in agency rulemakings, and communicate benefits in language Democrats understand. He stresses 'only Democrats can talk to Democrats' and encourages more diverse voices within crypto advocacy.
- Democrats are also angry at corporate America broadly for engaging with Trump, including law firms that settled and companies that donated. This anger may fuel oversight of many sectors, but Slaughter doubts it will lower prices and expects it to fade if polling doesn't support it.
- The credit card interest rate cap at 10% promoted by Senate Democrats is an example of first-order popular policies with second-order consequences (reduced credit access) that many Democrats ignore. Slaughter uses this to illustrate the party's risk-averse, sometimes economically naive approach that also shapes crypto policy debates.
- The fight over crypto is fundamentally a fight about 2008: many Democratic financial regulation staffers carry scar tissue from the financial crisis and fear new financial instruments could cause another collapse. However, Slaughter argues that technologies surviving multiple bubbles (like crypto) are persistent, and regulation should channel rather than ban them, citing Europe's MiCA as an example.
- Slaughter is watching four things between now and January: leadership fights in House and Senate (weak leaders could empower anti-industry generals), Hyperliquid approvals as a milestone for crypto-TradFi merger, potential funding fights that could disrupt SEC/CFTC operations, and early presidential primary entrants (strong candidates wait until Feb/March).
- Giving a grade for the first two years under Trump, Slaughter says the industry did 'mostly okay' but made mistakes on Clarity, especially failing to secure Democratic votes early. He argues the next two years will be tougher, but the long-term goal is durable policy that doesn't swing with elections.
