# #179 - Simon Dixon - The One World Government Is Already Here

## Key Points
- Simon Dixon argues that a de facto world government already exists—not through elected bodies but through the interlocking architecture of central banks, global asset managers, AI surveillance infrastructure, and programmable money systems
- Dixon contends the world is moving rapidly toward centralised AI, social credit scores, and programmable CBDCs that enable financial control at the individual level
- The "black pill" framing refers to the despair people feel upon recognising this system; Dixon's argument is that recognising it is the prerequisite for a constructive response
- AI is fundamentally different from previous technological revolutions because it concentrates power rather than distributing it—small, well-resourced actors can automate control at scale in ways previously impossible
- Dixon identifies three strategies for individuals: build using AI tools, own assets that cannot be seized or inflated away (particularly Bitcoin in self-custody), and develop personal sovereignty
- Bitcoin self-custody is framed as the financial analogue of physical self-sufficiency—removing oneself from the programmable money system underpinning the surveillance state
- The petrodollar system is in structural decline, and the geopolitical currency competition replacing it is accelerating the push toward programmable digital money by all major powers
- Political engagement is characterised as increasingly ineffective; Dixon argues the control architecture operates above and independent of conventional democratic politics
- BlackRock's Aladdin platform is cited as a concrete example of how AI is already managing trillions of dollars in assets and effectively co-directing fiscal and monetary policy
- Algorithmic distribution controls mean freedom of speech has become "freedom of speech, not freedom of reach"—what people actually encounter is determined by platforms regardless of formal legal protections

## Overview
Peter McCormack hosts Simon Dixon for what has become a recurring series on the political economy of global power. This episode, titled "What Comes After the Black Pill?" on YouTube and "The One World Government Is Already Here" in the podcast feed, begins with Dixon's observation that the fragmented chaos visible in Western democracies—broken states, endless debt, permanent political crisis—is not disorder but structure. He argues that the real governing architecture of the modern world operates through debt creation, asset management, surveillance technology, and control of capital flows, and that this architecture is now accelerating.

Dixon's central claim is that a one-world government is not a future conspiracy but a present reality, already operating through institutions like the Bank for International Settlements, the IMF, the World Bank, and their relationships with sovereign central banks. The emergence of programmable money—CBDCs with the technical capability to expire, be restricted to certain purchases, or be withheld based on social compliance—represents the completion of that architecture at the retail level. Dixon connects this to AI developments, arguing that unlike the internet, which broadly democratised information access, AI primarily amplifies the power of entities that already control massive data sets and infrastructure.

The episode discusses Bitcoin at length, with Dixon positioning it not merely as an investment but as an opt-out mechanism. Self-custody of Bitcoin is framed as the key step: holding Bitcoin on exchanges or through custodians reintroduces the dependency on the financial system Dixon is critiquing. He discusses the broader geopolitical dimension—the decline of petrodollar supremacy, currency competition between the U.S., China, and multipolar alternatives, and how each major power is pursuing its own version of programmable state money as part of this competition. The final segment turns to practical recommendations: building income using AI tools, acquiring non-capturable assets, and reducing dependence on systems that can be used as leverage against individuals.

## Implications
Dixon's framing carries weight regardless of how one assesses the intentionality of the system he describes. The observable facts—hyperscaler concentration of AI compute, CBDC development by most major central banks, algorithmic control of information distribution, and the growing technical feasibility of individualised financial surveillance—are not contested. The debate is whether these are converging by design or by the logic of incentives, but either way the practical implications for financial privacy, individual autonomy, and the balance of power between states and citizens are significant.

For Bitcoin and broader crypto audiences, Dixon's analysis reinforces the self-custody thesis but extends it into a geopolitical argument: Bitcoin held in self-custody is positioned as one of the only assets that operates outside the programmable money architecture now being constructed by both Western and non-Western state actors. For broader audiences, the episode raises questions about whether democratic institutions are structurally capable of constraining AI-enabled governance and financial surveillance as those systems mature.
