# Memory Is A Bubble, But Nvidia Protected – Jan Van Eck On Semis Surge

## Key Points
- Jan van Eck, CEO of VanEck (225 billion dollars AUM), argues that memory chip stocks such as Micron are in a "bubblicious" frenzy disconnected from durable competitive fundamentals
- VanEck's Semiconductor ETF (SMH) has compounded at 29% annualised since its 2011 inception and now manages over 65 billion dollars, making it one of the largest thematic ETFs in the world
- Nvidia holds up to 20% of the SMH portfolio; van Eck sees the company as having a generational competitive moat anchored in its CUDA software ecosystem, describing it as "the Walmart of compute"
- Memory manufacturers (Micron, Samsung, SK Hynix) lack the durable moats of Nvidia; their earnings surges are driven by pricing power rather than innovation, and Chinese competitors could quickly erode that pricing
- Van Eck applies a "10-year macro" framework—looking back from 2036 to identify which structural trends will have mattered most—as the lens for all product and portfolio decisions
- AI infrastructure capital expenditure by hyperscalers is expected to exceed 1 trillion dollars in 2027 and potentially reach 3–4 trillion dollars annually by decade's end
- U.S. fiscal deficits remain a primary risk: van Eck warns that when the 10-year Treasury yield crosses 5–5.25% he will be alarmed, and notes the Fed loses control of the long end when markets lose confidence
- The upcoming SpaceX IPO—structured with a rolling lockup and a float of only 3–3.5%—is expected to trigger a reflexive wealth-creation and reinvestment cycle
- VanEck's own internal AI token consumption doubled in three to four months beginning in early 2026, with spend approaching 1 million dollars per year
- Agentic AI is viewed as a meaningful new risk category; VanEck restricts autonomous AI agents to sandboxed environments due to potential proprietary data exposure

## Overview
Wilfred Frost sits down with Jan van Eck, CEO and owner of VanEck, a global asset manager overseeing 225 billion dollars and home to some of the most widely used thematic ETFs in the world. The conversation opens with the flagship VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH), which has delivered a 29% annualised return since inception in 2011 and now holds more than 65 billion dollars in assets. Van Eck explains how the fund's index construction—allowing Nvidia to constitute up to 20% of holdings—has contributed significantly to its outperformance versus equal-weighted alternatives.

The central investment thesis van Eck advances is a sharp distinction between Nvidia and the broader semiconductor universe. He describes Nvidia as having built an essentially irreplaceable software ecosystem through CUDA, allowing it to consistently drive down the cost per token while competitors scramble. Memory manufacturers occupy a very different position: their current earnings boom derives from pricing power in a supply-constrained environment, but they lack proprietary technology moats and face the near-term threat of Chinese manufacturers scaling export capacity. Van Eck calls the frenzy around memory stocks "bubblicious" and predicts Micron will no longer rank among the top earnings contributors to the S&P 500 within two years.

The episode also covers macro risks—particularly U.S. fiscal policy, where van Eck notes the deficit has shrunk from above 6.5% of GDP to around the low fives but remains structurally unsound. He describes current market conditions as resembling 1997–98 rather than 1999–2000, with a potential 30–50% AI-related market correction still possible before the ultimate bubble peak. Other topics include the SpaceX IPO mechanics, gold's role as a global reserve asset, uranium and nuclear energy funds, India as a long-term investment opportunity, and the internal dynamics of AI adoption within a large asset management firm.

## Implications
The distinction van Eck draws between Nvidia's durable moat and memory manufacturers' commodity position is consequential for portfolio construction. If memory valuations have run on pricing power rather than structural advantage, normalisation of supply conditions or entry of lower-cost Chinese competitors could produce a sharp correction without any broader AI demand slowdown. Investors treating the semiconductor sector as monolithic may be conflating very different risk profiles.

The broader macro caution around the 10-year Treasury yield and the structural U.S. deficit is a recurring theme for van Eck. His willingness to frame today's conditions as late-cycle but not yet at peak froth, combined with a prediction of a significant correction before the AI bubble fully resolves, suggests a strategy of riding current momentum while maintaining awareness of exit positioning. The SpaceX IPO, if it unfolds as van Eck expects, could itself become a liquidity catalyst that accelerates both the upswing and the eventual correction.
