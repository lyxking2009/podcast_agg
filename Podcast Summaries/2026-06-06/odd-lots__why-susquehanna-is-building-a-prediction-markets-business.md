# Why Susquehanna Is Building a Prediction Markets Business

**Show:** Odd Lots (Bloomberg)
**Hosts:** Joe Wisenthal, Tracy Alloway
**Guest:** Jeremy Malletts, Head of Prediction Markets at Susquehanna International Group
**Date:** 2026-06-06 (recorded live at City Winery, NYC, May 28, 2026)

## Key Points

- Susquehanna's prediction markets desk is one of the main liquidity providers across sports, economics, and politics contracts on multiple platforms
- They were the first institution to get involved in prediction markets and see their role as "shepherding" other institutions into the ecosystem
- Their mission is twofold: provide liquidity to help the ecosystem grow, and bring other institutional players in — the latter being arguably more important
- Market makers bridge the gap between buyers and sellers across time and size — Susquehanna can provide institutional-scale liquidity because capital is not a constraint for them
- Susquehanna is willing to hold directional risk (not strictly risk-neutral), which is essential for hedging use cases where everyone needs the same side of a trade
- The next frontier is institutional hedging — e.g., airlines hedging snowfall risk — but current platform volumes (e.g., $150K) seem too small for serious economic actors
- Susquehanna argues that price discovery matters more than raw volume: even low-volume contracts can reach fair prices via super forecaster communities, enabling them to make tens of millions in risk on those contracts
- They're building multiple access channels: direct platform connections, block trades on exchanges, off-exchange swaps, and licensing exchange market data
- Main institutional barriers: (1) awareness that volume doesn't limit hedging capacity, (2) compliance/legal comfort with the new asset class
- Current volume breakdown: more than half is sports, but there's a meaningful shift toward "real things" — economic, political, and regulatory contracts
- Susquehanna's founder Jeff Yass has a culture of thinking in Bayesian probabilities, which naturally extends to prediction markets
- The ecosystem is complementary: super forecaster communities provide the intelligence layer, while firms like Susquehanna provide capital and institutional infrastructure

## Overview

Recorded live at Bloomberg's City Winery event in New York, this conversation explores how Susquehanna — one of the world's largest market-making firms — is positioning itself at the center of the prediction markets boom. Jeremy Malletts, who leads their prediction markets desk, explains that Susquehanna's role goes far beyond simply providing liquidity on sports contracts. They're actively trying to bootstrap institutional adoption by being willing to take on large directional risk positions that corporations need for hedging real economic exposures. The key insight is that prediction markets function as information markets — the price discovery from super forecaster communities is valuable even at low volumes, which allows Susquehanna to confidently offer institutional-sized hedging on contracts that appear thin on screen. The conversation covers the practical plumbing of how institutional trades would work (prime brokers, block trades, swaps), the regulatory and compliance hurdles, and the gradual shift from pure sports betting toward economically meaningful contracts.

## Implications

- Prediction markets are evolving from a retail/sports betting product into institutional hedging infrastructure — Susquehanna is the bridge
- The "volume illusion" (low on-screen volume ≠ limited capacity) is the biggest misconception institutions have — market makers can provide far more liquidity than what's visible
- New access channels (swaps, block trades, licensed data) will determine how quickly institutions adopt prediction markets for real risk management
- The complementarity between super forecaster communities and institutional market makers suggests prediction markets could become a legitimate alternative to traditional insurance and OTC derivatives for certain risk classes
- Regulatory clarity remains the gating factor for institutional adoption
