# #182 - Julian Jessop - Big Government Broke the Growth Model

**Show:** The Peter McCormack Show
**Host:** Peter McCormack
**Guest:** Julian Jessop (Independent Economist)
**Date:** 2026-06-06

## Key Points

- Britain's economic decline is rooted in the post-2008 period — productivity growth has been abysmal since the Global Financial Crisis, and economists can't agree on the key drivers
- The state has simply got too big: government borrowing, public debt, and the tax burden are all at or near postwar record highs in the UK
- COVID was a turning point — the government's massive intervention created a cultural expectation that the solution to any problem is more government, not less
- Regulatory creep, especially ESG-related mandates, has shifted corporate focus away from productivity and profits toward compliance
- The UK imports oil and gas (including from Norway's side of the North Sea) while refusing to develop its own resources — a self-inflicted energy policy wound
- Planning reform is stalled — the UK could build more houses and infrastructure if the government were serious about it
- Inequality has actually been flat for decades — the real problem isn't redistribution but the lack of incentive to create wealth and the disincentives to work
- Youth unemployment has spiked, largely because minimum wage increases and day-one employment rights make employers reluctant to hire young people
- Far too many young people go to university (~50% target) when technical apprenticeships would serve them better; giving votes to 16-17 year olds is misguided
- Popular policies (Green Party's 10:1 pay cap, Reform's overtime tax exemption) poll highly but collapse under scrutiny — politicians chase votes rather than evidence-based policy
- A "ratchet effect" exists: once a benefit is introduced (winter fuel payment, triple lock on pensions), it becomes politically impossible to remove
- The Green Party and Reform are polling >50% combined — both offering popular ideas without clear funding mechanisms
- Kemi Badenoch is restructuring the Conservatives and improving personal popularity, but it's not yet reflected in polls
- Jessop is cautiously optimistic that a mainstream party with better ideas could emerge in a few years, but the appetite for reversal isn't there right now

## Overview

Julian Jessop, an independent economist, joins Peter McCormack for a sweeping diagnosis of what went wrong with the British economy. The conversation traces the decline back to the 2008 financial crisis and argues that the fundamental problem is an oversized state. The UK's tax burden is at postwar highs, government debt has ballooned, and regulatory intervention — from labor markets to energy to housing — has made the economy less efficient. COVID accelerated this trend by normalizing massive government intervention. The discussion covers specific policy failures: importing energy while sitting on domestic reserves, minimum wage hikes that price out young workers, university expansion that funnels people into degrees rather than trades, and "vibes-based" policies (like pay ratio caps) that poll well but are economically illiterate. McCormack and Jessop also debate whether this is reversible — Jessop is pessimistic in the short term, noting that the Green Party and Reform are collectively polling over 50% with populist but unfunded platforms, while the mainstream parties struggle to capture youth or offer compelling alternatives.

## Implications

- Britain's productivity crisis is fundamentally a policy crisis, not a structural or cultural one — the remedies are known but politically difficult
- The post-COVID "big government" ratchet makes fiscal consolidation nearly impossible without a political earthquake
- Youth employment policy is backfiring — well-intentioned protections are preventing young people from entering the workforce
- The rise of Greens and Reform signals deep dissatisfaction with the establishment, but neither offers economically credible alternatives
- Energy policy self-harm (importing instead of producing) is a microcosm of the broader problem: ideology over pragmatism
