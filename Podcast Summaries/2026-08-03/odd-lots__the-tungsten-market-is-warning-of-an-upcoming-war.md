## Key Points

- Tungsten has historically acted as a kind of century-old "prediction market" for war — investment in tungsten mining spikes sharply whenever war becomes a real possibility, and mines shut down again once the conflict ends
- The Dolphin mine in Tasmania (the subject of David Fickling's reporting) has opened and closed in lockstep with WWI, WWII, the Korean War, and the Vietnam War, finally closing in 1990 at the end of the Cold War — and is now reopening amid renewed geopolitical tension
- Tungsten's extreme density (similar to gold) and extraordinarily high melting point (over 3,000°C, roughly three times hotter than lava) make it ideal for armor-piercing projectiles and cluster-munition shrapnel that hold their shape on impact instead of deforming
- About 80% of tungsten's actual use is mundane: tungsten carbide for drill bits and cutting tools; it's also used in turbine blades and as a catalyst in oil refining, with only trace, uncertain use in some AI chips
- China controls roughly 80% of global tungsten supply mainly because it has by far the best geological deposits — not purely a matter of strategic hoarding — much like South Africa/Botswana for diamonds or Australia/Brazil for iron ore
- There is no futures market for tungsten (unlike copper or oil), which makes it nearly impossible for miners to hedge future revenue or get bank financing for new projects that may take five to ten years to pay off
- Total capital invested in Australia's Dolphin tungsten mine over 20 years is only about $77 million — a trivial sum compared to defense and AI infrastructure spending (a single SM-3/SAD interceptor missile costs roughly $10 million)
- China's tungsten export controls, imposed around when President Trump returned to office, have driven speculative stockpiling and price spikes; if the controls are lifted, prices could collapse and bankrupt newly reopened Western mines
- Floor prices are the main policy tool being debated to encourage non-Chinese supply, but critics worry that broad "critical minerals" lists (like the US's proposed Project Vault reserve) risk subsidizing commodities that don't actually need it (copper, nickel, aluminum) rather than truly scarce, single-source materials like tungsten, gallium, and germanium
- Rare-earth "ionic absorption clay" deposits once thought to exist only in southern China are now being found in Brazil, Malaysia, Australia, and parts of tropical Africa as China's reliability as a supplier is increasingly questioned
- Substitution is the other classic market response to scarcity — e.g., EV battery makers shifting away from cobalt (concentrated in the DRC, with child-labor concerns) toward lithium iron phosphate chemistry

## Overview

Hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway talk with Bloomberg Opinion columnist David Fickling about his reporting on a reopening tungsten mine in Tasmania and what it reveals about the tungsten market as a barometer of geopolitical risk. They cover tungsten's military and industrial uses, why China dominates global supply, the absence of a futures market that makes financing new non-Chinese mines difficult, and the surprisingly small dollar amounts involved relative to defense and AI infrastructure spending. The conversation also touches on rare-earth "ionic absorption clay" deposits turning up outside China, the policy debate over floor prices and critical-minerals reserves, and market substitution effects (such as cobalt-free EV batteries) as a general response to scarcity.

## Implications

Tungsten's boom-bust investment cycle, tightly tied to expectations of war, functions as an informal early-warning indicator of geopolitical risk. The lack of a futures market for many critical minerals is a structural barrier to Western supply diversification — without a way to hedge future prices, banks won't finance mines with multi-year payback horizons. Policymakers face a "which minerals actually matter" problem: broad critical-minerals designations risk subsidizing industries that don't need it, diluting support for genuinely single-source materials like tungsten, gallium, and germanium. The relatively tiny dollar figures involved (tens to low hundreds of millions) compared to defense and AI capex suggest that securing non-Chinese tungsten supply is cheap insurance that has been chronically underfunded due to short investment horizons and the West's historical unwillingness to subsidize loss-making strategic production during peacetime.
