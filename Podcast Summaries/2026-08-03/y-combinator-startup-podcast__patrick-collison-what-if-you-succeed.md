## Key Points

- Collison dropped out of MIT twice to start companies — first with Harj Taggar (Auctomatic), then Stripe. Dropping out is not a trapdoor: you can return, and "as far as I can tell, nobody has ever cared."
- The "what if you succeed?" question: founders obsess over failure risk but rarely ask whether they will actually enjoy running the company for 10, 17, or 30 years (Larry Ellison and Oracle cited). For Stripe, the answer was deeply interesting: "I've never met a Stripe customer and thought that's boring."
- Knowledge in the AI era is like cache: knowing something in "cognitive L1 cache" beats round-tripping to an agent, and "for a long time to come, neuronal lookups will be much faster." Revealed preference — at Stripe and the labs — still shows an enormous premium on cognitive ability.
- He still writes everything himself and has never sent a single AI-generated writing suggestion (Gmail/WhatsApp-style prompts): writing and interpersonal communication are fundamental, and models remain deficient at reasoning in the "multi-dimensional space of reality."
- Stripe's origin: after Startup School 2009 in Berkeley, over sushi in Potrero Hill, the brothers decided to start Stripe — "we might as well because it probably won't be that hard." It seemed both obviously good (everyone hated payments) and absurd (two kids in a trench coat starting a financial-services company; the word "fintech" didn't exist yet).
- Two years from first lines of code (fall 2009) to public launch (Sept 2011) despite YC's launch-fast doctrine — made viable by production users almost from the start (first customer Ross Boucher at Twilio North, Jan 2010, charging cards only), with just-in-time development grounded in real customer requests.
- The AI era may favor more ambitious, divergent starting points over classic lean-startup niches: niches get "aggressively tilled," and the most successful companies of the last decade (the labs, Anduril) are largely anti-lean. "You have to more aggressively decorrelate in the era of AI."
- Schlep blindness (Paul Graham): Stripe's surface was unglamorous (payroll, business financial services), but every business is "an applied theory of how some aspect of the world works." 25% of Delaware corporations are now started via Stripe Atlas.
- Don't fear the labs: 20 years ago everyone asked "what if Google does this?" — human organizations cannot aggressively prosecute 100 priorities. That fear's track record is "checkered" and generally overstated, though model/agentic capabilities themselves will obviate some verticals.
- Stripe formation data: new business starts are up ~2x year-over-year — the largest relative jump ever recorded, bigger than the COVID bump; the median business is doing better; the probability of reaching $1M/$5M/$10M revenue is improving; time to revenue is declining. "There's never been a better time to start a business."
- Enterprise buyers are "spring-loaded": terrified of being left behind with antiquated ways of operating, they now adopt from startups out of the gate — the key dynamic driving faster 0→90 and 1→10→100 growth within YC batches.
- On AI centralization: Stripe's data does not support the hegemonic, centralizing fear — "there are going to be many thousands of winners," and the trend lines point toward a more decentralized world with broad-based prosperity.

## Overview

At Startup School 2026, YC's Harj Taggar interviews Patrick Collison, co-founder and CEO of Stripe — 17 years after the two co-founded Auctomatic together. Collison recounts dropping out of MIT twice, the sushi-walk decision to start Stripe, why waiting two years to launch worked despite YC orthodoxy (production users from day one), the schlep-blindness framing of Stripe's unglamorous but endlessly interesting business, and the "what if you succeed?" question founders should ask before raising significant money. He then turns to AI: a cache model of knowledge (neuronal lookups stay faster), why he still writes everything himself, why the AI era rewards more ambitious and decorrelated starting points rather than tilled niches, why fear of the labs trampling startups is overstated, and Stripe's formation data showing new business starts up ~2x year-over-year with better outcomes across every metric — "never been a better time to start a business."

## Implications

For founders and students: dropping out of college is reversible and low-cost, but the millenarian urgency to "speed run" life is usually a poor intuition — Silicon Valley has a surfeit of opportunities. Ask the converse risk question ("what if you succeed?") before raising significant money, since you may be signing up for a 10-30 year commitment. In the AI era, first-principles knowledge and writing ability retain real value because cognitive speed and judgment are the scarce inputs, while cheaper software production makes ambitious, differentiated starting points more viable than competing in crowded niches. The enterprise "terror of being left behind" is a structural tailwind for startups selling into incumbents. Stripe's data (2x YoY formation growth, better median outcomes, faster time-to-revenue) indicates 2026 is the best environment on record for starting a company, though Collison hedges on anything beyond a few years.
