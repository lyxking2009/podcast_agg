## Key Points

- Gromen argues markets are underpricing the economic implications of the Iran war, even as China appears to be adapting through efficiency gains in its oil import strategy.
- China's true leverage and debt load are deeper than commonly realized, adding another layer of hidden fragility to the global macro picture.
- Gromen's core call: interest rates will keep moving higher until "something breaks" — a dynamic he sees as the dominant macro threat, more so than oil prices.
- He lays out specific yield "pain thresholds," referencing Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's approximate 4.4%–4.9% range on the 10-year note as the level where the Fed and Treasury are forced to step in.
- Gromen's "variant perception": in future risk-off events, yields will only dip briefly before spiking even higher as equities fall — a pattern he says has repeated since 2020, and a reversal of the traditional "flight to safety" playbook.
- Discussion of long-term Treasury supply-demand dynamics, and why he expects the Fed to ultimately prioritize Treasury market functioning over fighting inflation.
- Western sovereign bond fragility is not limited to the US — the UK, Japan, Germany, and France are all flagged as at risk of similar dynamics.
- Despite the sovereign-debt risks, Gromen describes equities as "extremely complacent" in the short term, though he argues they are actually behaving rationally when measured in dollar terms rather than in a hypothetical stable-currency terms.
- He frames dollar weakness as best measured against gold rather than against other fiat currencies, and argues gold is quietly displacing US Treasuries as the preferred global reserve asset — including through China's evolving gold accumulation and offshore yuan-clearing strategy.
- Long-term thesis remains bullish on equities: if gold continues to outperform Treasuries, Gromen sees that as a signal to buy stocks, not avoid them.
- Sector-level ideas include infrastructure investment (referencing PAVE and GRID ETFs) and Japanese industrial equities as beneficiaries of reshoring trends.
- Repeated US policy reversals are cited as a source of eroding credibility that feeds into the broader bond-market fragility narrative.

## Overview

Wilfred Frost is joined by Luke Gromen, founder of the macro research firm Forest For The Trees (FFTT), for a wide-ranging conversation on sovereign bond market fragility, the underpriced economic fallout from the Iran war, and Gromen's view that gold is gradually replacing US Treasuries as the world's preferred reserve asset. Gromen contends that rising Western bond yields — not oil prices — are the primary macro threat, and describes a "variant perception" in which future risk-off episodes produce only brief yield relief before yields spike further as equities sell off, a pattern he says has held since 2020. He identifies specific yield levels (roughly 4.4%–4.9% on the 10-year Treasury, per Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's own framing) as pain thresholds that will force Fed and Treasury intervention. The conversation broadens to sovereign risk across the UK, Japan, Germany, and France, before turning to equities, which Gromen calls "extremely complacent" near-term even while he remains structurally bullish for the decade ahead. He ties dollar weakness to gold rather than other currencies, discusses China's gold-accumulation and offshore-yuan strategy, and closes with tactical ideas around infrastructure (PAVE, GRID) and Japanese industrial reshoring plays.

## Implications

Gromen's framework suggests investors should treat elevated bond yields — rather than headline geopolitical or oil-price shocks — as the key risk to monitor, since a break in Treasury market functioning would likely force policy intervention that reprices both bonds and equities. His recommendation to de-lever ahead of a possible sharp equity drawdown, while holding gold as a hedge against currency debasement, positions gold as a structural allocation rather than a tactical trade. The view that any resulting equity sell-off would ultimately be a buying opportunity implies a "sell volatility, buy gold, prepare to buy dips" posture rather than a fully defensive one. His sector calls (infrastructure, Japanese industrials) point to reshoring and grid-modernization as durable multi-year themes independent of the near-term bond-market risk.
