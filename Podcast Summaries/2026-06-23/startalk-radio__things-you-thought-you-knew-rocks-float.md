# Things You Thought You Knew – Rocks Float

**Show:** StarTalk Radio  
**Date:** 2026-06-23  
**Transcript Source:** web_startalk

## Key Points

1. Oxygen is NOT flammable — it is a supporter of combustion. Movies depicting 'exploding oxygen' are scientifically inaccurate. The Apollo 1 fire was exacerbated by a pure oxygen environment, highlighting oxygen's role in accelerating fires rather than being fuel itself.
2. Some rocks CAN float — pumice has low density due to trapped gas bubbles from volcanic activity. An object floats if its density is less than the fluid it's in.
3. Earth is layered like a cocktail with a dense core and lighter crust. Rocks are the third least dense material on Earth's surface (after water and ice).
4. Antarctic ice sheets preserve and concentrate meteorites, making them easier to find there than in other terrains.
5. Blankets do NOT generate heat — they slow heat loss from your body by trapping a layer of air (a poor heat conductor) close to the body. The 'warmest' bed would be a thermos, as it minimizes all forms of heat transfer.
6. Simply producing more oxygen would not combat global warming, as the primary issue involves greenhouse gases like CO₂, not a lack of O₂.
7. Cold-blooded animals like snakes would not benefit from a blanket because they rely on external heat sources, not internal metabolism.

## Overview

Neil deGrasse Tyson and Chuck Nice tackle common scientific misconceptions in this 'Things You Thought You Knew' episode. They debunk the myth that oxygen is flammable (it supports combustion but isn't fuel), explain how pumice rocks float due to trapped gas bubbles from volcanic activity, and reveal that blankets don't generate heat — they merely slow heat loss by trapping insulating air. The episode also covers Earth's density layers, Antarctic meteorite discovery, and why producing more oxygen wouldn't solve global warming.

## Implications

For science communicators: this episode demonstrates how everyday objects (blankets, rocks, oxygen tanks) can be entry points for teaching fundamental physics concepts like density, heat transfer, and combustion. The misconceptions explored reveal how pop culture (movies like Jaws) shapes public understanding of science in persistent ways.
