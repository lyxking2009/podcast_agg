# Blake Scholl: Breaking the Supersonic Ban

**Show:** Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## Key Points
- Blake Scholl, founder/CEO of Boom Supersonic, delivered this as a Y Combinator Startup School keynote, arguing progress in flight and hard-tech manufacturing has stalled since the Concorde era.
- In 2014 Boom started with a cardboard-and-plywood mockup; a decade later its XB-1 became the first independently developed jet to break the sound barrier, in the Mojave Desert.
- XB-1 broke the sound barrier six times with no audible sonic boom on the ground, demonstrating 'Mach cutoff' — flying so the boom refracts upward and never reaches the ground.
- That boomless supersonic demonstration led to a White House invitation within 24 hours and a presidential executive order re-legalizing supersonic overland flight in the US just 115 days after the flight.
- Boom's manufacturing philosophy treats hardware development like software: internal tools 'Makeboom' and 'Bladeunner' let engineers simulate/redesign aircraft components in minutes.
- Boom builds its own in-house machine shop and test stands, cutting the digital-design-to-prototype-part cycle to about 24 hours.
- To fund capital-intensive R&D, Boom sells a ground-power version of its supersonic engine as a natural-gas turbine ('Superpower'), generating test data and revenue ahead of the passenger airliner Overture.
- Scholl described near-death moments for the company, including seven days of cash left when the board wanted to shut it down.
- On regulation strategy, Scholl contrasted Uber's bypass-regulators approach with Boom's early-FAA-engagement approach — FAA approval for XB-1's first supersonic flight took 90 minutes once formally requested.
- Extended Q&A covered hiring, the 'why now' myth in startups, why AI increases rather than decreases demand for software engineers, and competing with China on manufacturing by inventing new processes.

## Overview
This episode is a Y Combinator Startup School keynote by Blake Scholl, founder/CEO of Boom Supersonic, followed by audience Q&A. Scholl opens with a critique of stalled progress since 1969, arguing modern airliners are no faster than the 707. He traces Boom's decade-long journey from a cardboard mockup to XB-1, the first independently developed aircraft to break the sound barrier, highlighting the discovery of 'Mach cutoff' as the technical breakthrough that made supersonic overland flight regulatorily viable again — leading to an executive order within 115 days. He details Boom's engineering philosophy of treating aircraft design like software, vertically integrating manufacturing, and financing the capital-intensive supersonic airliner Overture by selling a ground-power turbine product. The extended Q&A covers startup philosophy, hiring, regulatory strategy, U.S.-China manufacturing competition, and AI's impact on engineering careers.

## Implications
Scholl frames Boom's story as a template for reviving ambitious, capital-intensive 'hard tech' in the US: a startup can change decades-old regulations by building working technology first and proactively partnering with regulators. His argument that AI lowers the cost of custom software development — and therefore increases demand for software engineers, especially in hardware industries — pushes back on narratives of software-engineering obsolescence.
