# At The Money: Living a Life of Purpose

**Show:** Masters in Business
**Date:** 2026-07-29
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points
- Dr. Jordan Grumet defines purpose not as a single grand 'why' but as 'little p purpose' — the process-oriented things that light you up in the present, distinct from 'big P purpose,' a big audacious goal-oriented legacy quest that tends to create anxiety.
- Research shows up to 91% of people feel 'purpose anxiety' at some point, even though having a sense of purpose is well-documented to increase happiness, health, and longevity — Grumet resolves this paradox by splitting purpose into the big-P and little-p types.
- You don't 'find' your purpose — you build it. 'Purpose anchors' (identified via exercises like 'what would I regret never having the courage or time to do if I were dying tomorrow') point the way, but building a life of purpose around them requires sustained action.
- Grumet distinguishes 'meaning' (a backward-looking story about your past — whether you see yourself as having overcome hardship and being 'enough') from 'purpose' (forward-looking); people with unresolved meaning problems often try to fill that hole through achievement, which he calls the 'achievement treadmill' — citing Elon Musk as an example of someone who may be trying to out-achieve unresolved childhood trauma.
- Big-P, goal-oriented pursuits (status, wealth, career milestones) tend toward unhappiness because they are all-or-nothing and rarely achieved; little-p, process-oriented engagement with the same pursuits (enjoying daily work, 'flow') leads to satisfaction regardless of outcome.
- 'Generational growth' is introduced as the counterpart to generational trauma: passing down a love or practice (e.g., Grumet's own path from his grandfather's love of math to his mother to himself, ultimately becoming a doctor) compounds into legacy across generations more reliably than a single audacious big-P achievement.
- Money is framed as a tool, not a goal — useful for buying time and freedom to pursue purpose, but purpose is attainable without wealth too.
- From his hospice work, Grumet reports that dying patients' most common regret is inaction ('I never had the energy, courage, or time to do X'), not action — and that even attempting something without succeeding (e.g., a patient's failed attempt to summit Everest) prevents that regret, since 'you will regret that you didn't give it your best,' not whether you succeeded.
- Grumet distinguishes 'action regrets' from 'inaction regrets,' noting that action regrets are often inaction regrets in hiding (e.g., wishing you'd apologized or made amends after a mistake, rather than the mistake itself).

## Overview
In this Masters in Business 'At The Money' segment, Barry Ritholtz interviews Dr. Jordan Grumet — a physician working in internal medicine and hospice care, and author of 'The Purpose Code' — about what people get wrong when searching for purpose. Grumet's central reframe is splitting 'purpose' into two types: the anxiety-inducing 'big P' version (a singular, audacious, goal-oriented why, like building a legacy) and the more attainable 'little p' version (the process-oriented things that light you up day to day). He explains that most popular advice to 'find your purpose' backfires because it implicitly points people toward the big-P version, producing widespread 'purpose anxiety' documented in the research literature even though purpose itself is strongly linked to happiness and longevity.

The conversation moves through how purpose is built rather than found (via 'purpose anchors' uncovered through reflective exercises), and how purpose differs from meaning — meaning being a backward-looking narrative about one's past that unhappy people often try to compensate for through relentless achievement (the 'achievement treadmill'), using Elon Musk as an illustrative example. Grumet reframes legacy through 'generational growth' — passing down a little-p purpose (like his grandfather's love of math) that compounds across generations more reliably than chasing an audacious big-P legacy. The conversation closes with reflections from his hospice practice: patients' regrets are almost always about things they didn't try, not choices they made, and the lesson for younger, ambitious people is to identify what future regret would look like now and turn it into a purpose anchor today.

## Implications
Grumet's framework offers a practical corrective for financial and career advisors (and their clients) who conflate purpose-seeking with big, singular achievements — suggesting that satisfaction and legacy are better built through consistent, process-oriented engagement with what one already loves rather than chasing an elusive 'big why.' For high-achievers and wealthy individuals in particular, the 'achievement treadmill' framing offers a lens for understanding why continued success doesn't resolve underlying unhappiness rooted in unresolved 'meaning' problems from the past. The hospice-derived insight that people regret inaction far more than failed action has direct implications for financial planning conversations: money should be treated as a tool for enabling attempted pursuits (travel, career changes, creative projects) rather than an end goal to be maximized indefinitely.
