# Cosmic Queries – Astro-lore with Moiya McTier

**Show:** StarTalk Radio
**Date:** 2026-07-03

## Key Points
- Moiya McTier double-majored in astrophysics and folklore/mythology at Harvard — not a pre-approved combination. She negotiated with both department heads by offering potential thesis topics at the intersection of both fields.
- Her Harvard thesis was a science fiction novel set on a real exoplanet (characterized with Kepler/K2 data), with a plot that paralleled the Hawaiian sovereignty movement. She visited Mauna Kea to interview protectors during the 30-meter telescope conflict.
- McTier's book 'The Milky Way: An Autobiography of Our Galaxy' is written from the galaxy's first-person perspective, framing galactic mergers as romantic relationships — minor mergers as 'flings' and major mergers as serious relationships.
- Different cultures see different constellations: the Dark Emu in Aboriginal Australian astronomy, Orion in various cultural interpretations, and the Pleiades across Babylonian, Greek, and Inca traditions. Constellations are culturally constructed, not universal.
- Science and folklore are 'two sides of the same coin' — ancient humans were observing the world and creating explanations that fit their worldview. As scientific tools improved, the worldview shifted from magic/gods to empirical understanding.
- Vampire myths have natural explanations: there are blood-sucking animals, animals that dig up graves, and other natural phenomena that could inspire these stories across cultures.
- McTier hosts two podcasts: ExoLore (building fictional worlds based on real exoplanet science) and Pale Blue Pod (making space feel warm and accessible for people overwhelmed by the universe).

## Overview
Neil deGrasse Tyson and Matt Kirshen welcome astrophysicist and folklorist Moiya McTier to explore the intersection of astrophysics and cultural storytelling. McTier shares her unusual Harvard double-major path, her science fiction thesis set on a real exoplanet, and her book written from the Milky Way's perspective. The conversation covers how different cultures interpret constellations, why science and folklore serve the same purpose (understanding the universe), and the natural origins of myths like vampires.

## Implications
The intersection of science and folklore reveals how human understanding evolves from narrative to empirical frameworks. Cultural diversity in astronomical observation shows that constellations are constructed, not inherent — relevant to how we communicate science across cultures. McTier's approach of making astrophysics accessible through storytelling represents a model for science communication that bridges expert and public understanding.
