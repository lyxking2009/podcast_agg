# Venture Capital During the AI Revolution with Mamoon Hamid

**Show:** Masters in Business
**Date:** 2026-07-03

## Key Points
- Mamoon Hamid grew up in Frankfurt, Germany, came to the US for Purdue (most astronaut graduates), then Stanford and Harvard. Originally wanted to be an astronaut after the Challenger explosion inspired him as a child.
- His career arc: semiconductors at Xilinx (1997 dot-com boom) → USVP → co-founded Social Capital with Chamath (2011) → Kleiner Perkins (2017). The transition from chips to software happened because he followed where the puck was going during the web 2.0 era.
- Box was his first investment at USVP (2007) — a file-sharing company that pivoted from consumer to enterprise. This led to Yammer (enterprise social, acquired by Microsoft 2012), then Slack (2014, 10-person company). Pattern recognition compounds across investments.
- Kleiner Perkins was the first institutional investor in Sun Microsystems, Netscape, Google, Amazon, and Genentech. Hamid joined because of 'extreme reverence' for the firm and partner John Doerr.
- At Kleiner Perkins, they invest by working down the labor pyramid: top (doctors, lawyers, engineers at $200K+) → middle (financial analysts, salespeople, nurses) → bottom (physical labor, eventually robotics). Companies include Harvey (AI for legal), Windsurf (acquired by Google), Ambience, Hippocratic, Nooks.
- 90% of AI revenue is in the hands of two companies (OpenAI and Anthropic). The remaining 10% is spread across many excellent companies but dwarfed by the scale of those two.
- Hamid's biggest miss was Anthropic Series B — they met the founders over Zoom during the pandemic and didn't get the visceral feeling. Since then, he's driven his entire calendar to in-person meetings only. His last 20 investments all had in-person first meetings.
- Kleiner Perkins uses AI heavily internally: Glean for knowledge management, AI-generated board meeting summaries, automated portfolio reviews, meeting ratings. They have an internal tech team of four building these tools.
- The 'SaaS-pocalypse' is overblown — software isn't going away. CIOs buy from companies that sell to them, not just smart agents. The pendulum swung too far toward hardware/infrastructure.

## Overview
Barry Ritholtz interviews Mamoon Hamid, partner at Kleiner Perkins, about his journey from aspiring astronaut in Germany to one of Silicon Valley's most successful enterprise software investors. Hamid traces his investment thesis through Box → Yammer → Slack, explaining how pattern recognition compounds. At Kleiner Perkins, he's applying a 'labor pyramid' framework to AI investing — starting with the highest-paid knowledge workers and working down. He candidly discusses missing Anthropic's Series B due to Zoom meetings, and how that changed his entire approach to founder meetings.

## Implications
AI investing follows a power law with 90% of revenue concentrated in OpenAI and Anthropic. The labor pyramid framework suggests AI will transform work from top (highest-paid) to bottom (physical labor) over the next decade. In-person meetings remain critical for venture decisions — Zoom-era habits led to significant misses. Software isn't dead despite the AI infrastructure hype. Kleiner Perkins' internal AI adoption (Glean, automated summaries) shows how VC firms are eating their own cooking.
