## Key Points

- **Behavioral biases cannot be eliminated by knowing about them** — they require structured processes, experiential exposure, and team accountability to counteract. Theoretical knowledge of loss aversion doesn't inoculate you against it.
- **Myopic loss aversion** is triggered by evaluating portfolios too frequently. Investors who check daily experience small losses as disproportionately painful, distorting long-term decision-making. The fix is realigning evaluation horizons with the actual investment thesis.
- **Disposition bias** — selling winners too early while holding losers too long — stems from loss aversion: losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel pleasurable (Kahneman/Tversky). The **blank-slate exercise** (would you buy this position at today's price from scratch?) is a simple, repeatable antidote.
- **Experiencing investment pain matters more than learning about it.** Simulated or real loss exposure builds the emotional resilience and self-awareness that intellectual knowledge cannot. BlackRock uses AI to simulate extreme market scenarios so managers can experience emotional reactions before they occur in real markets.
- **Physiological state is an underappreciated driver of decision quality.** BlackRock uses wearable data (heart rate variability, sleep quality) to flag when managers are deciding in a compromised state. Decisions made during high stress or poor sleep show measurable deterioration.
- **Elite investors subordinate ego to process.** They have high process confidence but low ego attachment to individual calls — they update quickly when wrong without ego cost. Ego attachment to being "right" is a leading cause of holding losing positions too long.
- **"Tainted altruism"** — the finding that a prosocial act loses moral credit when the actor also benefits — is Haisley's pick for most troubling bias. In advisory and team contexts, genuine expertise gets discounted when the expert has any personal stake, creating perverse incentives.
- **BlackRock's behavioral profiling** integrates portfolio data, trade rationales, risk/return metrics, and wearable physiological data to build a multi-dimensional bias profile for each fund manager.
- Teams profit from **constructive challenge mechanisms**: pre-mortems, red-team exercises, psychological safety for junior members to challenge senior ones, and structured falsification criteria for investment theses.

## Overview

Host William Green interviews Emily Haisley, Head of Behavioral Finance at BlackRock (the world's largest asset manager at ~$14 trillion AUM), where she leads a team embedded within Risk and Quantitative Analysis. Her team works directly with fund managers to identify behavioral biases, reduce systematic decision errors, and improve investment outcomes through psychological and physiological interventions.

The episode systematically covers the major behavioral biases that affect professional investors: myopic loss aversion (over-frequent portfolio evaluation), disposition bias (selling winners, holding losers), and ego attachment to prior views. For each, Haisley provides the psychological mechanism, BlackRock's diagnostic approach, and practical mitigation strategies.

A central theme is that **knowing about a bias is insufficient** — investors need structural processes and experiential exposure. BlackRock has developed AI simulation tools that let managers experience extreme market volatility synthetically, analogous to flight simulators for pilots. The goal is building emotional responses in safe environments so real-crisis reactions are better calibrated.

Haisley's most surprising insight is on **tainted altruism**: the empirically replicated finding that a genuinely beneficial recommendation is discounted — sometimes penalized — when the recommender also benefits. This creates perverse team dynamics where people with real expertise are incentivized to stay silent if they have personal stakes.

The episode closes with Haisley on embracing uncertainty, cognitive flexibility, and three personal resolutions for greater happiness.

## Implications

- **Physiological monitoring of decision-makers** is moving from sports psychology into professional investing — a leading indicator of how performance management will evolve in high-stakes knowledge work more broadly.
- **Pre-mortem and red-team practices** are among the highest-ROI process changes available to investment teams, yet are rarely institutionalized; this episode provides a practitioner's implementation framework.
- **The blank-slate portfolio test** is immediately actionable for any investor: regularly ask "would I buy every position I own, at today's price, starting from scratch?"
- **Tainted altruism** has implications beyond investing — any advisory or team context where the advisor has stakes. Recognizing it explicitly and separating process evaluation from outcome evaluation is the structural fix.
- **AI experience simulation** for decision-makers under stress is an emerging application area — BlackRock's use is early evidence that this will become standard in professional investment management.

## Notable Quotes

- "People feel the pain of loss twice as strongly" as gains feel pleasurable. — Emily Haisley (on prospect theory / loss aversion)
- On tainted altruism: Haisley calls it "the saddest bias" — the finding that prosocial acts lose moral credit when the actor also benefits, creating perverse disincentives for genuine expertise sharing.
- On experiential learning: simply learning about behavioral biases intellectually is insufficient — investors need to *experience* simulated or real loss to develop the emotional resilience and self-awareness to manage those reactions in real markets.
- On elite investors: great investors "can hold a view strongly and then change it without ego cost when new evidence arrives."
