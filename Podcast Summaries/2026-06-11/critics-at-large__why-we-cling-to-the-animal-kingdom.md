# Why We Cling to the Animal Kingdom

## Key Points
- The episode was inspired by a listener voicemail asking for cultural recommendations about the human-animal bond, prompting an expansive discussion spanning children's literature, film, and contemporary pop culture.
- The hosts trace animal narratives from Aesop's Fables through E.B. White (Stuart Little, Charlotte's Web, Trumpet of the Swan), C.S. Lewis's Chronicles of Narnia, Disney films (Bambi, The Lion King), to contemporary works like Mary Gaitskill's "The Mare" and the 2026 film "The Sheep Detectives."
- A central tension in animal stories: the anthropomorphic impulse (imbuing animals with human characteristics) versus the recognition of animals' "total strangeness" — the most meaningful works inhabit both poles simultaneously.
- Vinson Cunningham shares a personal connection: The Lion King was released in 1994, and his father died in 1995 — the Simba/Mufasa story became a way to process grief through the mediation that animal narratives provide.
- In 2026, our relationship to the natural world is paradoxically more confused than ever: we're more detached from actual nature, yet we treat our pets more like humans than ever before, and animal content goes viral (Timmy the Whale, Punch the Monkey).
- The discussion of Stuart Little reveals the oddness of E.B. White's creation — a mouse born from a human woman who falls in love with a bird and is set up on a date with a tiny heiress named Harriet Ames, existing in a liminal space between human and animal kingdoms.
- The hosts also preview a July 4th project: building a playlist of songs about America, inviting listeners to record voice memos about songs that reflect their feelings about America today.

## Overview
Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry, and Alexandra Schwartz respond to a listener's request for cultural recommendations about the human-animal bond, launching into a wide-ranging conversation that moves from Homer's Odyssey (Argos the dog dying at Odysseus's return) through childhood touchstones like Stone Fox, Garfield, and Peanuts, to adult literary fiction and contemporary viral animal content. The episode's central question — "What does the way we think about animals say about us humans?" — yields insights about how animal stories serve as vehicles for processing grief, exploring the in-between space of anthropomorphism, and reckoning with our increasing distance from the natural world even as we anthropomorphize our pets more than ever.

## Implications
- The enduring power of animal narratives lies in their ability to "ferry things over" from one kingdom to another — they belong to the animal world but can mediate distinctly human experiences like loss, love, and identity.
- The 2026 cultural landscape shows a paradox: maximum distance from nature combined with maximum anthropomorphism of animals, suggesting animal stories serve a compensatory psychological function.
- The explosion of snacking tomato culture (referenced in the Odd Lots episode the same day) parallels the animal kingdom discussion — both reflect how modern consumers seek curated, Instagrammable connections to natural things they're increasingly detached from.

## Notable Quotes
- **Alexandra Schwartz:** "Reckoning with their similarity to us and also their *total* strangeness to us — that's where works about animals really get me. Not just as a direct transfer onto the human experience but also this other thing that really does enrich our lives: to be in contact with species that are not our own."
- **Vinson Cunningham:** "Animals belong to one kingdom, but they can also sort of ferry things over into ours."
- **Naomi Fry:** "We are more distant from the natural world than we've ever been, but at the same time, we treat our animals more like humans than we ever have."
- **Vinson Cunningham:** "In 1995, my father died, so the whole Simba and his father thing — a young cub coming into his own — it was an interesting way through this mediation that animals in narrative can effectuate."
- **Naomi Fry:** "Stuart Little is quite tragic because he has that pull between his human family and the animal kingdom. He falls in love with a bird, with Margalo, and she leaves, and he goes searching for her."
