# Why Tomatoes Are the Most Expensive They've Been in Four Decades

## Key Points
- Tomato prices hit a record $2.69/lb, up ~40% year-over-year, driven primarily by two freezes in Florida that destroyed 80% of the state's winter crop — over $150M in losses.
- Mexico supplies ~70% of US winter tomatoes (reversed from 80% domestic pre-NAFTA). A 17% anti-dumping tariff implemented July 2025 caused Mexico to reduce production 3-4%, compounding the Florida freeze impact.
- Produce demand is inelastic in the short term — when you remove 26 of 100 needed units from the market with no substitution, prices spike dramatically.
- The tomato industry operates on razor-thin margins across the entire chain (growers, distributors, restaurants). Growers contract 60-70% of their crop at guaranteed prices and sell the remaining 30% on the open market to capture upswings.
- Restaurant food costs are only ~30% of total operating costs (vs. 70-75% for retailers), so restaurants are less motivated to lock in long-term supply contracts and prefer to shop around weekly.
- The varietal tomato explosion over the past 15 years was driven by greenhouse production technology originating in Holland, enabling growers to cultivate specialty seed varieties (snacking tomatoes, heirloom-adjacent crosses) that are too disease-susceptible for open-field farming.
- Cold chain logistics remain a core challenge: strawberries need 33-34°F, tomatoes need 48-52°F, and both ride the same truck — requiring sophisticated temperature management from farm to plate.

## Overview
Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal welcome Jacob Kremple, SVP of Procurement and Merchandising at Baldor Specialty Food (a Bronx-based high-end food distributor serving restaurants from Maine to Northern Virginia). The conversation covers why tomato prices are at 40-year highs, the structural shifts in US produce supply chains since NAFTA, and the boom in specialty tomato varieties. Kremple explains how Baldor curates ~7,500 SKUs (vs. Sysco's 20,000+) as a "highliner" distributor, balancing white-glove chef service with the brutal economics of perishable food distribution. The discussion extends to strawberries, the role of social media in food trends, and how NYC's geographic constraints (bridge tolls, cold chain complexity) make it one of the most competitive food service markets in the world.

## Implications
- Food price inflation in produce is structurally linked to climate volatility and trade policy — the anti-dumping tariff on Mexican tomatoes directly contributed to the current crisis by reducing supply elasticity.
- The information asymmetry between professional buyers (restaurants) and retail consumers means retail prices are "sticky upward" — once consumers accept high tomato prices based on headlines, retailers are slow to pass through reductions.
- The varietal/artisanal produce trend is reshaping supply chains: seed companies, growers, and distributors now operate more like fashion supply chains, with R&D cycles, influencer-driven demand, and premium branding.
- Expect more produce price spikes this summer — romaine lettuce is already approaching $100/case due to California weather, and avocados doubled from $30 to $80/case in one week.
