## Key Points

- The Nasdaq 100 ecosystem totals **$1.4 trillion** in average 2025 exposure — 35% growth over 2024 — with $587B in ETFs (QQQ alone $351B), $647B in derivatives, $75B in mutual funds, $51B in structured notes, and $52B in insurance products.
- All **8 of the world's largest companies by market cap** list on Nasdaq; the odds of that being random are **0.4%** (equivalent to flipping 8 heads in a row).
- Nasdaq implemented **new methodology changes effective May 1, 2026**: quarterly reconstitution (was annual), "fast entry" for top-40 companies (evaluated on 7th trading day post-IPO), and graduated float requirements (scale-up until 1/3 of shares are publicly floated).
- The changes were motivated by companies like SpaceX and OpenAI going public at trillion-dollar-plus valuations after years of private VC funding — the old annual reconstitution cycle couldn't keep pace.
- **1999 vs. today on valuation**: In 1999, ~75% of index constituents traded at P/E above 60; today fewer than 20% do. Forward P/E today is ~26 vs. 100+ in the late 1990s. Nine of the top 10 names have reasonable P/Es today (Tesla is the lone outlier); in 1999, nine of the top 10 had P/Es of 100+.
- **1999 vs. today on margins**: In 1999, 10% of index constituents had negative net margins; today essentially zero. Today 20% of the index has net margins of 50–100%; in 1999 that bucket was empty.
- **Earnings**: 20-year compounded EPS growth for the Nasdaq 100 is ~15%/year, matching price appreciation. On track for 45% YoY EPS growth this quarter; full-year 2026 forecast north of 30%. Forward P/E of ~26 vs. trailing ~35.
- The Nasdaq 100 now represents **more than 50% of the S&P 500** by market cap weight; in 2000 it was 10–15%. An 80%+ drawdown today would have a completely different systemic impact.
- 10-year annualized return: **~22%** (over 600% total). Even buying at the 1999 peak and holding through the 82% drawdown yields 8.8% annualized over ~26 years.
- **QQQM** (launched end of 2020, 15 bps vs. QQQ's 18 bps) already at **$90 billion** in AUM as of May 11, 2026. Both BlackRock and State Street now have rights to license the Nasdaq 100 name.
- Host's bottom line: "If anything, it's 1998" — excessive enthusiasm around tech, but not a fundamental repeat of 1999.

## Overview

Recorded May 11, 2026, this "Talk Your Book" episode features Mark Merrick, Global Head of Index Insights at Nasdaq, joining Michael Batnick and Ben Carlson to examine whether the Nasdaq 100 is in bubble territory. Merrick walks through the staggering size of the Nasdaq 100 ecosystem ($1.4T), the reasoning behind the new May 2026 methodology changes, and a detailed comparison of today's fundamentals versus the 1999 dot-com bubble. The overarching argument: while the index has had extraordinary returns and concentration has risen, the underlying earnings and margin profiles of today's constituents are radically different from 1999 — making a direct bubble comparison misleading.

## Implications

The new "fast entry" rule is specifically designed to capture mega-cap private companies (SpaceX, OpenAI, Anthropic) when they eventually IPO, preventing up-to-a-year delays from index inclusion. This could force large passive inflows on short notice. Competition from BlackRock and State Street will likely compress fees and fragment QQQ's near-monopoly over Nasdaq 100 exposure. The biggest structural question isn't whether the index is a bubble — the fundamentals argue otherwise — but whether 20-30%+ EPS growth rates can be sustained as the AI investment cycle matures.
