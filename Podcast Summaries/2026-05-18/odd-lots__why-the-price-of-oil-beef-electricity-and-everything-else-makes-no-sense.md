## Key Points

- Oil is at **~$100/barrel** despite 60+ days of Strait of Hormuz closure — far below the $200+ many predicted. Key buffers: strategic petroleum reserve drawdowns globally (except China), bypass pipelines, and demand destruction of ~**5 million barrels/day** (5% of global supply).
- The Strait isn't reopening soon because neither side has reached the pain threshold needed to compromise: Iran can impose hardship on its population indefinitely, and the US economy is still performing (S&P 500 near record highs, $4.50/gallon gasoline not yet a political crisis-maker).
- **UAE leaving OPEC** is the biggest challenge the cartel has ever faced. The UAE wants to expand from 4.5M to 5M+ barrels/day, has the geology and money to do it. Saudi Arabia loses its most compliant and highest-withholding partner. When the Strait eventually reopens, expect a race for market share and lower oil prices.
- **European electricity is back to near-normal (~€80/MWh)**, vs. a peak of €1,000/MWh in 2022 (vs. pre-crisis €50–75). The 2022 electricity crisis — not the current oil shock — was the truly destructive event: some French bakers saw electricity bills go from €800/month to €7,000/month.
- **Global food supply is abundant** (grain, milk swimming in excess) with one critical exception: **US beef**. Cattle inventory is at a **75-year low** — too few animals, too much demand — driving beef prices to record highs. Tyson lost $150M in three months processing beef.
- The **delayed food crisis**: US wheat plantings are at their lowest since 1919. Farmers are making planting decisions now based on low crop prices vs. high input costs, meaning supply squeezes in 6–12 months.
- The **fertilizer spread** has flipped from 2022: in 2022, fertilizer doubled (~$400 to ~$800/ton) but corn also doubled ($4 to $8/bushel), so farmers could manage. Today, fertilizer has spiked but corn is still ~**$4.50/bushel** — the spread is negative, discouraging planting.
- **Europe's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)** kicked in January 2026 with ~€120/ton on imported urea. European farmers pre-bought 60–80% more fertilizer than normal in Nov–Dec 2025 to beat the tariff, temporarily cushioning the blow but creating a future supply problem.
- **US shale response**: US production will be higher in 2026 than expected 6–12 months ago, but only by a "couple hundred thousand barrels/day" vs. a 10M barrel/day market hole — "it's not going to move the needle."
- **GLP-1 drug effect on food**: People on weight-loss drugs eat less and shift from quick-service restaurants to protein-dense foods. Dairy industry has pivoted: producing whey protein is now more valuable than cheese itself, leading to a soft-cheese glut and a coming shortage of aged cheeses.
- **FX damage**: The dollar is 20% weaker than a year ago, which has hurt European agricultural exports (Irish butter, French wine) more than tariffs per se.

## Overview

Recorded live at Wilton's Music Hall in London on May 7, 2026, this Odd Lots episode features Bloomberg Opinion columnist Javier Blas and Irish Farmer's Journal business editor Lorcan Roche Kelly walking through why commodity prices are defying expectations. The core paradox: the Strait of Hormuz has been closed for over 60 days, yet oil is "only" at $100. The explanation involves interacting buffers (SPR drawdowns, demand destruction, bypass pipelines), structural shifts (UAE leaving OPEC), and a mismatch between where demand destruction is falling and how financial markets price it. Food tells a similar story: abundance today masks a coming supply squeeze driven by negative farming economics, historic low cattle inventory, and fertilizer disruptions.

## Implications

The current commodity pricing environment is deceptively stable — propped up by inventory buffers that are finite. When the Strait of Hormuz eventually reopens, expect a two-phase dynamic: first, aggressive inventory replenishment (briefly bullish for oil), then a race for market share (bearish, potentially $60–70 oil). In food, the squeeze is 6–12 months away as bad planting decisions compound. The CBAM-fertilizer dynamic illustrates how regulatory deadlines create artificial demand frontloading followed by structural price pressure. The UAE's OPEC exit signals a shift in Saudi Arabia's ability to enforce production discipline, potentially ending the cartel's effective price management role in a post-crisis world.
