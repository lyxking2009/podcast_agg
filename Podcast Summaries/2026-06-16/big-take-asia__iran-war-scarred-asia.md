# The Iran War's Lasting Scars Across Asia

**Show:** The Big Take Asia (Bloomberg)  
**Date:** 2026-06-16  
**Duration:** 30 min  
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points

1. The US-Iran interim deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz provides relief but the structural shifts caused by the conflict have fundamentally changed businesses across Asia.
2. Wealthy countries (US, Japan) drained oil stockpiles to cushion prices; poorer Asian nations turned to demand destruction — encouraging industries and consumers to use less, risking recession.
3. US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is at 349 million barrels, approaching the 250M operational minimum — the lowest post-COVID levels.
4. China reduced oil demand by ~9% (1.5M barrels/day) through demand destruction, in stark contrast to developed nations using stockpiles.
5. A brewing food crisis in Southeast Asia: farmers skipping planting seasons because they can't afford diesel for tractors and water pumps. Some leaving crops rotting in the ground.
6. The Strait of Hormuz closure created a 'war tax' on virtually every major commodity. Hong Kong gas prices hit ~$16/gallon (up 15%), diesel up 50%.
7. Helium supply — critical for advanced semiconductor manufacturing — has very little elasticity. Persistent Hormuz closure could intersect with helium shortages, making chip production harder.
8. Global stockpiling race is structurally inflationary: everyone simultaneously building reserves and capacity consumes resources and drives up prices.
9. The AI race feels existential, so traditional macro signals (rates, FX) are being swamped by spending on AI infrastructure regardless of currency movements.
10. Historical parallel: In ancient Roman times, emperors timed wars to avoid planting seasons. We seem to have forgotten that lesson.

## Overview

Bloomberg's Wanha sits down with Odd Lots hosts Tracy Alloway and Joe Wisenthal to discuss how the Iran War and Strait of Hormuz closure have disproportionately impacted Asia. The conversation covers the disparity between wealthy nations using stockpiles vs. poorer nations forced into demand destruction, a brewing food crisis in Southeast Asia where farmers can't afford fuel to plant, the helium supply risk to semiconductor manufacturing, and why the structural shifts from this conflict won't disappear even if the strait reopens.

## Implications

For investors and policymakers: the Iran War's economic scars are structural, not cyclical. The food crisis brewing in Southeast Asia could create political instability (echoing Arab Spring dynamics). The helium-supply-to-chip-manufacturing pipeline is a hidden vulnerability. The global stockpiling race is inflationary by nature and will persist regardless of the peace deal.
