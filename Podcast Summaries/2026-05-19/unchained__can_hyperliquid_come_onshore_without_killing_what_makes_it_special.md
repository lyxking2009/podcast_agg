## Key Points

- CME Group and Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) petitioned the CFTC to regulate Hyperliquid, arguing that price discovery is migrating to unregulated offshore venues and citing concerns about commodity benchmark integrity and potential sanctions evasion.
- Hyperliquid currently operates offshore and blocks U.S. persons from trading, but its 24/7 derivatives markets and full on-chain transparency have drawn the attention of both traditional incumbents and U.S. regulators.
- Three paths forward were discussed: (1) come onshore by obtaining CFTC licenses (DCM, DCO, FCM), (2) stay offshore while continuing to block U.S. persons, or (3) further decentralize to become pure protocol/technology rather than a regulated entity.
- Walt Lukken (FIA) argued that principles-based CFTC regulation — not specific technology mandates — could accommodate innovation while still providing the credibility and market confidence that regulation brings.
- Chris Perkins (250 Digital Asset Management) countered that sophisticated investors should not require intermediaries and that decentralized architectures can fulfill the post-2008 financial reform objectives that created FCM and clearing requirements.
- The central dilemma: coming onshore under current CFTC frameworks would likely require Hyperliquid to integrate traditional intermediaries (FCMs, clearinghouses), potentially degrading the efficiency and decentralization that make it competitive.
- Both guests agreed that 24/7 trading is inevitable across all markets — the question is whether traditional exchanges can adapt, or whether offshore venues like Hyperliquid will define the new standard.
- A deeper regulatory tension emerged: the U.S. risks becoming "cut off from the world financial internet" if onshore and offshore market structures diverge irreconcilably.

## Overview

Laura Shin convenes Walt Lukken (FIA President & CEO) and Chris Perkins (CEO, 250 Digital Asset Management) to examine whether Hyperliquid — the offshore perpetuals exchange that outpaces CME and ICE on 24/7 price discovery and on-chain transparency — can come under U.S. regulatory oversight without sacrificing the features that make it special. The episode is framed by a formal CFTC petition from CME and ICE seeking to bring Hyperliquid-style venues onshore, raising urgent questions about how existing commodity derivatives law applies to decentralized, always-on trading infrastructure. The conversation maps the practical trade-offs across three scenarios and surfaces a broader geopolitical concern: a bifurcated global financial system where U.S. markets operate under separate rules from the rest of the world.

## Implications

If Hyperliquid and similar venues are required to obtain U.S. CFTC licenses, they would face pressure to incorporate intermediaries and limited trading hours, potentially eroding their core advantages and accelerating user migration to jurisdictions with lighter-touch regulation. Conversely, if the U.S. fails to create a regulatory on-ramp, American investors remain blocked and U.S. price discovery cedes ground to offshore venues — weakening domestic market influence. The episode suggests the most viable path may be principles-based CFTC adaptation that recognizes on-chain transparency as a legitimate substitute for some traditional oversight mechanisms, but significant political and institutional inertia stands in the way.
