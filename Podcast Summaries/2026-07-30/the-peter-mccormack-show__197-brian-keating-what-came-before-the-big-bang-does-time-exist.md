# #197 - Brian Keating - What Came Before the Big Bang & Does Time Exist?

**Show:** The Peter McCormack Show
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Transcript source:** youtube_autocaptions

## Key Points

- **Time may not "exist" as a fundamental thing.** Keating argues the laws of physics are time-invariant — a swinging pendulum, a bowling ball, or an electron orbiting an atom "don't care" which direction time runs. The best formal definition he's heard, from Nobel laureate Frank Wilczek ("time is what a clock measures"), is a tautology. Keating's research program is built around the idea that what we call "time" may be an emergent proxy for other physical processes rather than a true dimension.

- **Entropy alone doesn't explain time's arrow.** The common claim that time moves forward because entropy always increases (the second law of thermodynamics) is, per Keating, necessary but not sufficient — entropy is scale-dependent (e.g., milk mixed into coffee looks disordered macroscopically but not at the molecular level), and nothing in physics forbids molecules from "unmixing" given enough time. He's instead investigating a possible "cosmological arrow of time" — a distinct force/field that could set time's direction — potentially detectable via the Simons Observatory.

- **What came before the Big Bang is Keating's central research question.** He rejects Lawrence Krauss's "universe from nothing" framing as description-not-explanation (equations like E=mc² describe phenomena, they don't cause them). His research (via BICEP/BICEP2 and now the Simons Observatory) hunts for primordial gravitational waves imprinted in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) as indirect evidence of cosmic inflation — a pre-existing energy field (the "inflaton") that could have sourced the Big Bang. Detecting these waves wouldn't prove inflation but would rule out competing origin theories (eternal universe, cyclic universe, etc.).

- **The Big Bang wasn't an explosion at a point.** Discussing Fred Hoyle (who coined "Big Bang" as an insult), Keating explains the expansion happened everywhere in space simultaneously — like dots on an inflating balloon — so there is no true center. This overturned roughly 2,000+ years of "steady state"/eternal-universe thinking that held until Hubble's 1929 observations.

- **Science can go back to ~one trillionth of a second after the Big Bang** using known physics (extrapolating observed data/laws backward); before that, current understanding runs out. Keating's team is trying to push that boundary back further using gravitational-wave signatures rather than light, since gravitational waves (unlike CMB light, produced 380,000 years after the Bang) could have originated far earlier and still be detectable today.

- **Dark matter is real and detected (partially); dark energy is closer to a placeholder term.** Neutrinos are actual, detected non-interacting massive particles that constitute some dark matter, though not enough to explain all observed phenomena. Dark energy, by contrast, is more of a name for the current unexplained mechanism driving the universe's accelerating expansion — the acceleration itself is not in dispute, only its cause.

- **AI/LLM skepticism grounded in physics analogy.** Keating argues LLMs are sophisticated matrix-multiplication systems (originally optimized for GPU gaming performance, not intelligence) trained only on existing human-produced knowledge; they cannot generate genuinely novel physics theory the way Einstein did, because Einstein's insight (the equivalence principle) came from an embodied, felt sensation (zero gravity) that a non-embodied system cannot replicate. He doubts current LLM architectures reach AGI/superintelligence, and worries more about societal/economic dependency and an "AI bubble" collapse than about existential AI risk.

- **UAP/alien disclosure skepticism.** Keating is unconvinced by "disclosure" claims, noting that people with power and legal immunity (e.g., Congresswoman Anna Paulina Luna) could simply produce physical evidence if it existed but haven't. He distinguishes rare unexplained anomalies (which will likely never all be explained) from claims of interdimensional beings/craft, which he sees as effectively religious belief rather than science.

- **Meaning, mortality, and introspection.** Prompted by Peter's own reflections after hearing Keating's episodes with Sean Ryan and Tucker Carlson, Keating discusses humans as uniquely aware of their own mortality (citing Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* and Viktor Frankl), and argues attention, not time, is the truly scarce resource.

- **Trust in science and institutions has eroded**, partly due to scandals like Fauci's internal emails (discussed regarding COVID origin claims and treatment of scientists like Jay Bhattacharya), which Keating says damaged public faith not just in individuals but in science and government broadly.

## Overview

Brian Keating, an experimental cosmologist at UC San Diego and host of the "Into the Impossible" podcast, joins Peter McCormack for a wide-ranging conversation spanning cosmology, the nature of time, artificial intelligence, UAPs, and mortality. The core scientific thread is Keating's research question: what happened "before" the Big Bang, and does time itself have a fundamental existence or is it an emergent property? He explains that the laws of physics are time-invariant and that common explanations for time's arrow (like entropy) are insufficient, motivating his search — via the BICEP2 experiment and now the Simons Observatory (a 100+ million dollar, 450-scientist project at 18,000 feet in Chile) — for primordial gravitational waves that would provide circumstantial evidence for cosmic inflation and rule out rival theories of the universe's origin.

The conversation moves through the mechanics of the Big Bang (dispelling the "explosion" misconception and explaining why there's no center to the universe), the difference between dark matter (partially detected, via neutrinos) and dark energy (largely unexplained), and a critique of popular science fiction (Interstellar, Contact) for physical implausibility around wormholes and time travel.

A substantial middle section turns introspective: prompted by Peter's own reflections on mortality and meaning after listening to Keating's appearances on other podcasts, the two discuss Ernest Becker's "denial of death," Viktor Frankl's search for meaning, and Keating's view that attention — not time — is humanity's truly scarce resource. This bleeds into a discussion of institutional trust, referencing the Fauci email disclosures and COVID-era public health messaging as damaging to public faith in science and government.

The final third covers AI and UAPs. Keating argues current LLMs, built on GPU-optimized matrix multiplication, are trained only on existing human knowledge and cannot originate genuinely new physics theory the way Einstein did through embodied intuition; he's skeptical of true AGI/superintelligence under current architectures and more worried about an "AI bubble" and societal dependency than extinction-level AI risk. On UAPs, he remains publicly skeptical of disclosure claims, arguing that people with power (like Rep. Anna Paulina Luna) could produce evidence if it existed. The episode closes warmly, with Keating gifting Peter genuine meteorites (including material tied to the early solar system and Mars) and a custom "Keating Prize" award.

## Implications

For listeners interested in cosmology and physics, this episode is a useful primer on the frontier of inflationary cosmology and the observational hunt for primordial gravitational waves — a live, ongoing experimental program (Simons Observatory) rather than pure theory, with a concrete falsifiability structure (rule out non-inflation origin theories rather than "prove" inflation). It also offers a clear, accessible explanation of why time's arrow remains an open problem in physics despite popular conflation with entropy or motion. The AI segment provides a physicist's outside perspective on why matrix-multiplication-based LLMs may be architecturally limited in generating novel scientific theory, a useful counterpoint for listeners following AI capability debates. The broader themes — institutional trust, disclosure skepticism, and mortality-driven meaning — situate the hard science in the context of a "spirit of the times" argument, useful for listeners tracking why podcasts increasingly blend physics, religion, AI, and introspection.
