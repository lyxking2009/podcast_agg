# The Irony and the Ecstasy of Colson Whitehead

**Show:** Critics at Large | The New Yorker
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Transcript source:** web_search_fallback

## Key Points
- Hosts Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry, and Alexandra Schwartz are joined by New Yorker staff writer Julian Lucas, who recently profiled Colson Whitehead ("Colson Whitehead's Big Score") on the occasion of "Cool Machine," the final book in Whitehead's Harlem Trilogy.
- Julian assigned each host one Whitehead novel to prepare the episode: Naomi read *The Underground Railroad*, Alex read *Sag Harbor*, and Vinson discussed *Harlem Shuffle*.
- *The Underground Railroad* (2016) literalizes the railroad as a dark, carnival-ride device that moves through different states/eras of American history, situating it in the "neo-slave narrative" tradition alongside Octavia Butler's *Kindred* and Toni Morrison's *Beloved*. It pushes back on the idea of straight-line progress from slavery to freedom, resonating differently across the Obama and Trump eras.
- *Sag Harbor* (2009) is described as Whitehead's most personal, semi-autobiographical novel, set in a Black enclave in the Hamptons where the young protagonist Benji and his brother are largely left to their own devices for the summer. Julian's reporting revealed the novel was written after Whitehead became estranged from his own alcoholic father, even while writing it near his father's house.
- *Harlem Shuffle* and the Harlem Trilogy follow Ray Carney, a furniture-store owner and part-time criminal "fence" whose double life dramatizes New York City's cycles of decline and resurgence (from 1960s Harlem through the Koch-era development boom). The concept of "dorvay" — the crooked, in-between waking hours at night — is central.
- The hosts agree Whitehead's defining trait is combining serious historical/political material with genuine readability, humor, and warmth — an "ironic detachment by someone who really cares" — making him, in Vinson's words, a quintessential Gen X writer.
- Julian notes Whitehead's career has repeatedly landed at fortuitous cultural moments: genre-bending early novels amid "post-Blackness" discourse, *Sag Harbor* after Obama's election, and *The Underground Railroad*/*The Nickel Boys* bridging the Obama-to-Trump transition.
- The episode also touches on Whitehead's satirical New York Times op-ed ("Don't Use AI to Do This"), in which he ironically praises an AI tool ("The Gooch") while ultimately making the case for human-authored fiction over AI-generated "content."
- The episode closes with each host naming the next Whitehead book they plan to read: Alex and Naomi pick up *Harlem Shuffle*, while Vinson returns to *John Henry Days*.

## Overview
This episode is a deep-dive tribute to novelist Colson Whitehead, timed to the release of "Cool Machine," the concluding volume of his Harlem Trilogy. The three regular hosts are joined by staff writer Julian Lucas, who just published a major profile of Whitehead ("Colson Whitehead's Big Score") and assigned each host one of three novels — *The Underground Railroad*, *Sag Harbor*, and *Harlem Shuffle* — to discuss as a lens onto his career.

The conversation traces Whitehead's evolution: an early reputation for genre-bending literary fiction (*The Intuitionist*, *John Henry Days*), a turn toward deeply personal, "beautiful coming-of-age" writing in *Sag Harbor*, and then the back-to-back Pulitzer Prize-winning historical novels *The Underground Railroad* and *The Nickel Boys*, which cemented his image as a writer reckoning with American history, race, and injustice. The hosts argue that image, while accurate, undersells the humor, "hypnotic insouciance," and sheer storytelling pleasure that run through all his work — qualities most fully on display in the Harlem Trilogy, whose protagonist Ray Carney embodies the tension between respectability and crookedness that Whitehead returns to throughout his fiction.

Julian shares reporting from his profile, including the revelation that *Sag Harbor* — often read as gently nostalgic — was written in the emotional aftermath of Whitehead cutting off contact with his alcoholic father, even as Whitehead kept returning to Sag Harbor to write it a few blocks from where his father lived. The hosts connect this biographical detail to a recurring theme in Whitehead's fiction: a "Gen X Black artist's contention" with the Civil Rights generation and its more heroic, uncomplicated narratives of Black history.

The episode closes on a broader reflection about the value of the novel as a form in an age of screens, referencing Jonathan Franzen's essay "Why Bother?" and Whitehead's own satirical Times op-ed about AI and creative writing, concluding that Whitehead's career amounts to an implicit, ongoing answer to why the novel still matters.

## Implications
- Positions the completion of the Harlem Trilogy ("Cool Machine") as a moment to reassess Whitehead's full body of work beyond the "serious historical novelist" reputation built by his Pulitzer winners.
- Reinforces a recurring Critics at Large theme (also raised regarding Christopher Nolan) about art that blends serious historical/political ambition with genuine entertainment value and mass readability.
- Frames Whitehead's satirical AI op-ed as part of a broader cultural conversation about the irreplaceable value of human-authored fiction versus AI-generated "content."

- "Whitehead, singularly, I think, has become a very important writer without ever seeming to stretch for importance." (Vinson Cunningham)
