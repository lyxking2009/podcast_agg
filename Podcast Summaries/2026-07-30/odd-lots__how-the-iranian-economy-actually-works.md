# How the Iranian Economy Actually Works

**Show:** Odd Lots
**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points
- Hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway open by noting that the Iran war (which began around March, and has never been formally declared a war) has faded from daily headlines despite its potential to be catastrophic for oil markets and the global economy. They admit to knowing little about everyday Iranian economic life beyond oil, cars, and pistachios, setting up the episode's focus on Iran's political economy.
- Guests Yeganeh Torbati (Iran correspondent, New York Times) and Bozorgmehr Sharafedin (head of digital, Iran International), co-authors of the new book "Stolen Revolution: Betrayal and Hope in Modern Iran," describe the Islamic Republic as a "mafia state": a layered society where citizens' access to resources is determined by loyalty to the regime rather than equal citizenship. Foreign proxies like Hezbollah in Lebanon or Houthi militias in Yemen can be valued more highly by the state than, say, a Tehran University professor.
- The 1979 revolution was a coalition of leftists, secular liberal democrats, and Islamists; Khomeini co-opted leftist rhetoric about the poor (promising free electricity, buses, housing) to build a base, but the book argues the regime abandoned those redistributive promises over time, especially visibly since around 2017.
- Iran's economy rests on oil plus a diversified industrial base (steel, cars, agriculture) built on a post-revolution ideology of self-sufficiency and import substitution after the Shah era and the Iran-Iraq War. These industries started under formal state/ministry control but gradually shifted to an unaccountable "shadow state" tied to security forces and clerical institutions.
- Privatization in Iran was frequently corrupt: state-owned factories were sold cheaply to individuals close to the security forces, machinery was stripped and sold, and land was redeveloped rather than kept productive. Businesses that grow successful in the private sector risk the security forces "coming to take their share," similar to mafia extortion.
- "Bonyads" (revolutionary religious foundations, e.g., for the poor or war veterans) and "invisible ports" (Revolutionary Guard-controlled ports/jetties that import goods without government audit or taxation) are examples of parallel, unaccountable economic structures. The book traces one figure, Mehdi Karroubi, who as an MP secured import exemptions for a bonyad in the 1980s and decades later, as Speaker of Parliament, tried and failed to rein in the same kind of unaccountable institutions he had helped create.
- A 2004 anecdote: on the opening day of a new Tehran airport meant to rival Dubai/Istanbul, the Revolutionary Guards blocked the runway with armored vehicles and scrambled a fighter jet to stop planes from landing, because the Guards had lost the airport operating contract to a Turkish-Austrian firm. The airport was effectively a no-fly zone for months and reopened under state control instead.
- Guests debate the motives behind state extraction from the private sector: a mix of genuine ideological suspicion of foreign economic influence (rooted in Iran's 20th-century history of exploitation by the US, UK, and Russia) and self-interested rent-seeking by competing factions. Example: during COVID, the Supreme Leader blocked Western vaccine imports while a conglomerate he controls (Setad) won the contract to develop a domestic vaccine — ideology and self-interest "melding seamlessly."
- Iran has had one attempt at Japan-style state-led industrial development (President Hashemi Rafsanjani's ambition to make Iran an "Islamic Japan"), but it largely failed. Because state-linked firms — not the most efficient or meritorious ones — dominate the domestic economy, the guests argue Iran is poorly positioned to compete with firms like Siemens or GE internationally, even setting sanctions aside; steel exports are a partial exception.
- Iran had a genuinely vibrant tech startup scene in the early-to-mid 2010s, aided by being the second Middle Eastern country (after Israel) to get internet access, the Rouhani government's rollout of 3G/4G, and a protected market of ~80 million users that US firms (Amazon, Uber, Lyft) couldn't legally enter due to sanctions even under the nuclear deal. Domestic clones emerged: Digikala ("Amazon of Iran"), Snapp ("Uber of Iran"), and various food-delivery apps, funded partly by returning Iranian expats and foreign VC after the 2015 nuclear deal.
- The startup boom triggered a backlash from hardline/security factions (smear campaigns via anonymous Telegram channels, interrogations, arrests, office raids), often because these companies were associated with the more moderate Rouhani government. Middlemen with ties to both the security state and private sector then offered "protection" in exchange for equity stakes (e.g., ~5% of shares); some founders accepted, and eventually Snapp sold a large stake to a bonyad-linked entity and Digikala sold 40% to a bonyad-affiliated telecom.
- One founder's app reportedly evaded Apple App Store restrictions by disguising itself as a music-streaming app, only revealing ride-hailing functionality once a user logged in with an Iranian IP address — a workaround Apple later closed off.
- The guests stress that Iran's business environment is uniquely chaotic compared to, say, China: even striking a deal with one powerful faction (a bonyad, the Guards) offers no lasting protection because rival factions ("underbosses") compete and shift power unpredictably — Sharafedin likens it to Greek mythology, where even the Supreme Leader is not an all-powerful Zeus but must manage rival "gods." This unpredictability, more than outright corruption, is described as most poisonous to business confidence.
- On why Iran hasn't taken Western offers to trade sanctions relief for economic integration (including Trump's and Obama's overtures), the guests argue Western policymakers underestimate that the Islamic Republic is ideologically driven, not purely transactional: money from the 2015 nuclear deal's sanctions relief was largely funneled into military development rather than addressing unemployment or inflation, and current MoU talks face resistance from hardline factions.
- On the ongoing US-Iran conflict, the guests argue Iran has a far higher "pain threshold" than the US: triple-digit inflation and high unemployment are already normalized, and losses of hundreds or thousands of soldiers are more absorbable domestically than the political cost to Washington of even 18 US casualties. Iran is reportedly looking to China, not the West, as its future investment partner, per comments from Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf.
- Torbati concludes that for the Islamic Republic, the economy has never been an end in itself but a means to ideological/self-sufficiency goals; despite some former officials urging a pivot toward economic normalization, no leader — from Khomeini to the current Supreme Leader over nearly four decades — has been willing to subordinate ideology and foreign policy to economic growth.

## Overview
Recorded July 28, 2026, this Odd Lots episode features hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway interviewing Yeganeh Torbati (New York Times Iran correspondent) and Bozorgmehr Sharafedin (head of digital at Iran International), co-authors of "Stolen Revolution: Betrayal and Hope in Modern Iran." The hosts frame the conversation around their own admitted ignorance of everyday Iranian economic life — despite months of an undeclared US-Iran war — and ask the guests to explain how the country's political economy actually functions.

The conversation traces a throughline from the 1979 revolution's redistributive promises (housing, free electricity and buses for the poor) through the gradual capture of state industries by "bonyads" (religious foundations) and the Revolutionary Guards, illustrated by vivid anecdotes: the 1980s import exemption that later enabled Guard-controlled "invisible ports," and the 2004 standoff in which the Guards blocked a new Tehran airport from opening because they lost its operating contract. The guests characterize the resulting system as a "mafia state" — one where loyalty, not citizenship, determines access to resources — but complicate the picture by describing intense factional infighting rather than a single unified extractive elite.

A substantial middle section covers Iran's unexpectedly vibrant early-2010s tech startup scene (Digikala, Snapp, and others), which flourished in a sanctions-protected market before facing a coordinated but chaotic backlash: smear campaigns, arrests, and ultimately forced equity transfers to security-linked entities. The guests use this as a case study for why doing business in Iran is uniquely unpredictable compared to more centralized authoritarian systems like China, invoking a Greek-mythology analogy of competing gods rather than one supreme authority.

The episode closes on the present US-Iran conflict, with the guests arguing that ideology consistently trumps economic rationality in regime decision-making, that Iran has a much higher tolerance for economic and human costs than the US does, and that Iran's leadership increasingly looks to China rather than the West as its economic future.

## Implications
For investors and analysts tracking Iran-related headlines (oil markets, sanctions policy, nuclear negotiations), the episode is a caution against assuming economic incentives will drive Iranian state behavior — the guests' central argument is that ideology and factional survival have consistently overridden straightforward cost-benefit economic logic for over four decades, including during the 2015-2016 sanctions relief window. For anyone evaluating emerging-market opportunities, the "many gods" framing is a useful heuristic: political risk in Iran isn't just about aligning with the regime, but about the near-impossibility of durable alignment given fluid, competing power centers. It also suggests that any future sanctions relief or reconstruction opportunity in Iran would likely continue to funnel disproportionately toward Revolutionary Guard- and bonyad-linked entities rather than open, competitive markets, and that Iran's own tilt toward China (rather than Western capital) is likely to shape how any post-conflict economic opening unfolds.
