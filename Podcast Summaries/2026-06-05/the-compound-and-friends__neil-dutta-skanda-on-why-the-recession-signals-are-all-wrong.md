## Key Points

- AI spending is approaching 7% of the economy by end-2025, surpassing the peak housing investment share seen in 2005, making the tech cycle and the business cycle increasingly indistinguishable.
- The labor market is 'soggy' — job growth has slowed and income growth continues to slide, with rising credit card and mortgage delinquencies signaling household financial stress.
- Wage growth of roughly 4% is lagging nominal GDP growth of roughly 6%, creating a widening gap that pressures household finances even as the headline economy appears strong.
- Corporate profits hit record highs while labor's share of gross domestic income fell to 51%, the lowest level since 1947, reflecting a stark profit-versus-wage divergence.
- The S&P 493 (non-Mag 7 stocks) are outperforming the Magnificent 7 year-to-date (12.6% vs. 7.3%), with industrial stocks leading due to AI infrastructure demand for data centers, power, and cooling.
- 15 non-tech S&P 500 companies now have semiconductor stock correlations above 0.5, suggesting AI infrastructure spending is reshaping sector dynamics across the economy.
- Traditional leading indicators like housing starts have not triggered the expected downturns, and equipment investment — historically a lagging indicator — is now a growth driver.
- Neil is dovish on Fed policy, concerned about hiking into supply shocks; Skanda is more hawkish, warning that if the Fed starts cutting it could pivot quickly to three consecutive hikes.
- Key indicators to watch for cycle turning points include credit spreads, initial jobless claims, and evidence of return on investment from non-hyperscaler AI spending.
- Both guests concluded this cycle differs substantially from historical patterns, making conventional recession playbooks potentially unreliable.

## Overview

Neil Dutta of Renaissance Macro Research and Skanda Amarnath of Employ America joined host Michael Batnick to debate why traditional recession indicators have failed this cycle. They examined the AI investment boom's economic footprint, diverging trends in corporate profits versus worker wages, and the Fed's policy dilemma amid supply-driven inflation. Both guests agreed this business cycle is historically unusual and that conventional leading indicators may no longer be reliable guides.

## Implications

The blurring of the tech cycle and the business cycle means investors and policymakers must develop new frameworks for reading economic conditions, as legacy indicators built around housing and manufacturing cycles may give false signals. The profit-wage divergence and AI investment concentration also raise questions about the sustainability of current growth and the distribution of economic gains.
