## Key Points

- MicroStrategy now carries approximately $15 billion in preferred shares with 10-12% dividend rates, creating ~$1.7 billion in annual cash obligations despite having no operating revenue.
- Four stakeholder groups — Bitcoin holders, MSTR equity shareholders, preferred shareholders, and debt holders — have fundamentally conflicting interests that cannot all be satisfied simultaneously.
- MicroStrategy raised $2 billion in cash to cover preferred dividends, then spent $1.4 billion buying back convertible debt at 92 cents on the dollar, shrinking its cash runway from ~18 months to ~4 months.
- Dorman argues that selling only $2.5 million of Bitcoin was worse than selling nothing, as it signals future larger sales while damaging market confidence without meaningfully addressing cash needs.
- If a Bitcoin sale was necessary, Dorman advocates selling approximately $2 billion at once to remove uncertainty rather than telegraphing ongoing distress through small, incremental sales.
- MicroStrategy only needs Bitcoin to appreciate ~2.5% annually to cover its preferred dividends, making a Bitcoin sale largely unnecessary at current prices.
- All available financing options are suboptimal: further equity dilution is non-accretive, Bitcoin sales spook markets, more debt threatens credit ratings and S&P 500 inclusion, and skipping preferred dividends risks litigation and market access.
- Dorman criticizes UMA protocol validators for resolving a Polymarket prediction market as 'NO' despite confirmed evidence of a May 26-31 Bitcoin sale, arguing it sets a damaging precedent for prediction market integrity.
- Dorman concludes the MicroStrategy flywheel is 'slowly dying' as too many capital structure obligations now compete against each other, with default not imminent but the path forward requiring sacrificing at least one stakeholder group.

## Overview

Jeff Dorman, CIO at Arca, breaks down MicroStrategy's increasingly strained capital structure following its shift to preferred stock financing, which has created roughly $1.7 billion in annual cash obligations for a company with no operating revenue. He examines the conflicting interests of four stakeholder groups — Bitcoin holders, equity shareholders, preferred shareholders, and debt holders — and argues that MicroStrategy's management decisions are systematically eroding confidence. Dorman also criticizes a Polymarket prediction market resolution that ignored confirmed evidence of a Bitcoin sale.

## Implications

MicroStrategy's capital structure evolution serves as a cautionary case study for Bitcoin treasury strategies that layer complex financial obligations on top of a single volatile asset. The Polymarket dispute also highlights unresolved governance challenges in decentralized prediction markets when evidence spans deadline boundaries.
