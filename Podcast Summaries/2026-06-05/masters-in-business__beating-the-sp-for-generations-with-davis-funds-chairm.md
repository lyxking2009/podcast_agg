## Key Points
- Davis Advisors was founded by Chris Davis's father in 1969 and has outperformed the S&P 500 since inception — a span of over 55 years — by focusing on high-quality businesses bought at reasonable prices.
- The Davis investing dynasty spans three generations: grandfather Shelby Cullom Davis started with $100K borrowed from his wife's family and grew it to $800M; Chris now manages roughly $20B AUM.
- Chris Davis took an unconventional path to investing — spending years at University of St Andrews (earning a Master's in Philosophy and Theology), working as a pastoral assistant at the American Cathedral in Paris, and even applying to the CIA before joining the family business.
- His grandfather taught investing through stories, people, and ideas rather than numbers and spreadsheets, instilling a qualitative, business-owner mindset from the start.
- Charlie Munger served as a long-time mentor to Chris Davis; Davis also sits on the boards of both Berkshire Hathaway and Coca-Cola.
- Financial media creates a distorted view of investing by emphasizing short-term noise; Davis argues that turning off the TV and thinking in decades rather than quarters is essential to long-term success.
- Chris's father believed every child should achieve financial literacy regardless of career path — treating it as a fundamental life skill, not a vocational one.
- A family anecdote captures the Davis philosophy: his 106-year-old grandmother, scolding Chris during the 2008 drawdown, said "I don't buy green bananas" — underscoring that even very long time horizons demand resilience through volatility.

## Overview
Barry Ritholtz interviews Chris Davis, Chairman of Davis Advisors, tracing the firm's multigenerational history of market-beating performance back to 1969. Chris recounts his winding personal journey — from Scotland's University of St Andrews, where a gap year turned into a philosophy and theology master's degree, to a stint as a pastoral assistant in Paris and a flirtation with CIA recruitment — before eventually joining the family investment firm. He describes learning the craft from his grandfather, Shelby Cullom Davis, whose approach centered on understanding businesses and their people rather than fixating on financial models. Davis outlines a patient, value-oriented philosophy that prizes high-quality companies held for the long term, discusses the influence of Charlie Munger, and reflects on the dangers of short-term financial media. The conversation also touches on the critical importance of financial literacy as a life skill that transcends any particular profession.

## Implications
- Long-term active management built on fundamental business analysis can sustainably outperform passive benchmarks, though it demands genuine patience and conviction through drawdowns.
- A grounding in the humanities (philosophy, theology, liberal arts) can sharpen an investor's ability to evaluate people, culture, and narratives — complementing quantitative skills rather than replacing them.
- Mentorship from world-class practitioners (Munger, in Davis's case) and proximity to great companies via board service provide compounding informational advantages that are difficult to replicate from the outside.
- The psychological discipline to ignore financial media noise is itself a durable edge; investors who think in decades rather than quarters structurally avoid the behavioral mistakes that drag down most returns.
- Making financial literacy a family-wide priority — regardless of individual career choices — creates a household culture of sound decision-making that compounds across generations.
