# The hidden pattern behind successful products | Mark Pincus (founder of Zynga)

**Show:** Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth
**Date:** 2026-06-14

## Key Points

- Mark Pincus's 'Proven, Better, New' framework: copy what's proven, make it dramatically better so 10/10 people say yes, then add something new — this de-risks innovation by starting from a validated concept.
- At Zynga, 8 of 10 major game launches became massive hits reaching over a billion players, validating the structured iteration approach.
- Being *less* ambitious is the path to the most ambitious ideas — start with a focused, solvable problem to create a foundation for larger ambitions.
- Your instincts are right 95% of the time, but your ideas are wrong 75% of the time — trust gut feelings about market problems but rigorously validate specific solutions.
- 'Kill hope before hope kills you' — ruthless objectivity about product performance prevents hopeful projections from blinding you to reality.
- Successful products at scale often come from copying proven models, improving the core experience dramatically, adding a new compelling element, and validating with real user reactions.

## Overview

Mark Pincus, founder of Zynga (FarmVille, Words With Friends, Zynga Poker), joins Lenny Rachitsky to discuss his 'Proven, Better, New' framework for product development. Pincus argues that the most successful products aren't wild inventions but structured iterations — copying proven concepts, making them dramatically better, then adding novelty. He shares lessons from Zynga's 8/10 hit rate on major launches, the paradox that less ambition leads to bigger ideas, and the critical distinction between trusting instincts (right 95% of the time) versus ideas (wrong 75% of the time). The conversation covers his new book 'Life at the Speed of Play' and references insights from Stewart Butterfield, Brian Chesky, and Nikita Bier.

## Implications

Product teams should resist the urge to innovate from scratch. The 'Proven, Better, New' framework provides a systematic way to de-risk product bets while still creating breakout hits. The instinct-vs-idea distinction is particularly actionable: validate the problem aggressively but hold solutions loosely.
