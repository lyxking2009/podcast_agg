## Key Points
- Sloterdijk's central thesis: all human life is practice — we are always training ourselves through habits and rituals, whether we recognize it or not
- Anthropotechnics: Sloterdijk's term for the study of how humans use deliberate exercises (physical, mental, spiritual) to improve and transform themselves
- Sloterdijk traces how ancient Greek athletics, Christian asceticism, and modern self-help are all manifestations of the same fundamental drive toward self-transcendence
- The title comes from Rilke's poem "Archaic Torso of Apollo" — the imperative to transform oneself is embedded in art and culture, not just philosophy or religion
- Sloterdijk critiques modern life for losing explicit practice while embedding implicit practices in consumption and media — we train ourselves without knowing it, often toward ends we haven't chosen
- The immune system metaphor: humans develop psycho-social immune systems through repeated practices that make them more resilient against disruption and suffering
- Post-religious society still needs "vertical tension" — the striving toward a higher version of oneself — even if the theological framework that once housed that drive has collapsed

## Overview
Stephen West's Philosophize This! episode 250 examines Peter Sloterdijk's 2009 book *You Must Change Your Life* (*Du Mußt Dein Leben Ändern*). Sloterdijk argues that humans are fundamentally "acrobats" engaged in self-overcoming practices he calls "anthropotechnics" — deliberate exercises that shape who we are. The episode explores how modern life, religion, athletics, and philosophy are all forms of practice-based self-transformation, and what it means to live consciously within that framework.

## Implications
Sloterdijk's framework reframes self-improvement not as a modern capitalist invention but as a fundamental human drive, with implications for how we understand education, religion, athletics, and personal development in secular modernity. It offers a philosophical basis for taking practice seriously — not as productivity optimization but as the very structure of a meaningful human life.
