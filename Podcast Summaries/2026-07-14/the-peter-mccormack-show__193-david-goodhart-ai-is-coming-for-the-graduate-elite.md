## Key Points
- The "graduate elite" — lawyers, consultants, journalists, analysts, programmers — face unprecedented job displacement from AI, reversing decades of knowledge-work premium
- Goodhart's Anywheres vs Somewheres framework: the Anywheres (mobile, graduate, liberal) are now as economically vulnerable as the Somewheres (rooted, working-class) they previously looked down on
- AI disruption is politically novel because it hits the class that writes the narrative — media, academia, and policy will be slow to acknowledge the displacement of their own cohort
- The credential crisis: if a degree no longer guarantees cognitive labor income, the entire social contract around higher education breaks down
- Political ramifications: graduate-elite identity is tied to progressive politics; their economic decline may produce a new populism from the left-leaning managerial class
- The speed of AI adoption means there is little time for retraining; unlike previous automation waves, cognitive displacement is happening across years not decades
- Goodhart argues for stronger social safety nets but is skeptical of politicians' willingness to fund them given fiscal constraints

## Overview
Political journalist and author David Goodhart joins Peter McCormack to discuss how AI threatens to upend the professional-managerial class — the "graduate elite" who have long felt economically secure. Drawing on his influential "Anywheres vs Somewheres" framework, Goodhart argues that AI disruption will be uniquely destabilizing because it hits the cognitive tasks that graduates were trained to perform, potentially erasing the educational premium that has defined middle-class identity for decades.

## Implications
If AI hollows out cognitive labor as thoroughly as automation hollowed out manufacturing, the political coalitions of the past 30 years could collapse — the graduate elite who drove globalization and tech enthusiasm may become its loudest critics when they bear the economic cost. This inversion could fundamentally reshape the electoral left in Western democracies.
