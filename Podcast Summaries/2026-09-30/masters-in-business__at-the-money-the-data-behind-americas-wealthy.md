# At the Money: The Data Behind America's Wealthy

## Overview

Barry Ritholtz talks with Princeton's Owen Zidar and the University of Chicago's Eric Zwick, co-authors of
‘The Everywhere Millionaire: Who is Really Rich in America and How They Got There’. This second instalment focuses on
the underlying data, and the headline finding is how badly the popular narrative of American wealth is distorted by
attention. The Forbes 400 dominates media coverage of wealth, yet its members hold a small single-digit share of
household wealth, while roughly a million-plus private business owners worth $25 million or more hold vastly more.
The conversation covers pass-through income concentration, the collapse of estate-tax collection, why broad
non-compete agreements impose a massive labour penalty, and what that implies for tax policy and opportunity.

## Key Points

- For every wealthy public-company CEO there are more than 1,000 private business owners worth at least $25 million in net worth; the top-owner group numbers more than a million households.
- The Forbes 400 receives roughly 50% of all news coverage on wealth but holds only about 3% of total U.S. household wealth; the people they call Main Street millionaires hold about 13 times that combined wealth and account for roughly 40% of household wealth.
- Scale check: about 5 million households are worth over $5 million (top 4%), and roughly 65,000 Americans are worth over $100 million - thirty to fifty times the Forbes 400 headcount.
- Half of Americans worth $5 million or more own a private business; 70 cents of every dollar of pass-through business income accrues to the top 1%, making business ownership more concentrated than public equity.
- Estate tax: roughly $200 billion transfers tax-free each year and only about 0.1% of U.S. estates pay any estate tax at all, down from roughly 0.4% fifteen years ago, after the threshold moved from $1.2 million to $30 million for married couples and avoidance vehicles proliferated.
- Non-compete agreements impose an estimated $300 billion annual labour penalty; the authors argue removing broad, non-senior non-competes (as California does) would meaningfully narrow the K-shaped divide.
- Both authors argue the narrative needs resetting: discussions of opportunity and tax policy are distorted when attention is monopolised by a few hundred billionaires.

## Implications

This is the data layer under the wealth-inequality debate, and the practical takeaway is that business ownership -
not public equity, not executive compensation - is the dominant path to serious American wealth. For investors and
policy watchers, the estate-tax statistic (0.1% of estates paying, $200B transferring tax-free annually) and the
$300B non-compete penalty are the two most quotable numbers, and both point at policy levers that are far cheaper
