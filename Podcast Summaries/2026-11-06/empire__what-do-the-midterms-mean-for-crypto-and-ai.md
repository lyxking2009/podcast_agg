# What Do The Midterms Mean For Crypto And AI? — Empire

## TL;DR
Empire hosts Jason Yanowitz and Rob are joined by VanEck's Matthew Siegel to dissect the market selloff in AI and tech following the midterms. Siegel argues that AI capex is being repriced due to Texas governor's potential restrictions on data centers and tightening credit, but sees buying opportunities in Bitcoin miners and landlords with long-term leases. The group debates whether crypto and tokenization can decouple from Nasdaq, with Siegel expressing skepticism about stablecoin growth and tokenized equities, while Rob highlights rising stablecoin velocity and payment adoption.

## Key points
- On November 5, the Nasdaq dropped 4-6% intraday, led by semis and AI chipmakers (Nvidia, Broadcom, Qualcomm) and neo clouds (Nebius, CoreWeave). Bitcoin held above $80k after a midterm dip and rebound, showing relative resilience amid the tech selloff.
- Matthew Siegel attributes the AI selloff to a repricing of AI capex driven by the Texas governor race outcome: 30% of the US AI data center pipeline is in Texas; if all built, it would be $5 trillion of investment and nearly 1 million jobs, but since 90% of data centers never get built, a realistic scenario is ~$1 trillion and 100k jobs. This creates downside risk for Nvidia if data center space is unavailable.
- Credit markets are tightening, causing many data center projects to demand more equity relative to debt. Two hedge fund liquidations were reported, and Anthropic IPO odds on Polymarket fell 10-20% per day. FT reporting suggests Anthropic debt might be junk rated, leading to equity dilution fears and selling pressure on AI stocks.
- Rob highlights that almost every part of the AI stack has negative unit economics and a highly correlated revenue daisy chain. Companies require significant capital to grow; if debt and equity markets tighten further, risk-off behavior could spread to crypto and other risk assets.
- Matthew, a long-only unlevered investor, sees rare valuation support in Bitcoin miners and data center landlords with 10-year leases and investment-grade counterparties. He argues the market is pricing in no terminal value for multi-billion dollar data centers, and being negative requires assuming white elephants, for which he sees no evidence. He cites Next DC being taken private at a 50% premium as a positive signal.
- AI adoption is driving cost savings: hyperscaler OPEX is ~$1 trillion per year, and 10% efficiency gains equal $100 billion in savings. Microsoft offered a buyout to 25% of its workers, partly due to AI efficiencies. S&P 500 transcripts and conference audience surveys show 100% of attendees would feel disadvantaged without AI access, indicating early adoption phase.
- Rob notes a shift from frontier models to open weights among startups up to Series D/E, including Coinbase and Ramp. This reduces reliance on Anthropic/OpenAI and threatens Neo clouds. He estimates 200+ funded AI companies may need more VC if GPU supply tightens or compute costs rise, creating a potential equity capital gap if LPs turn risk-off.
- Matthew counters that neo cloud token demand is not mostly from VC-funded companies; physical constraints favor highest bidders with strong balance sheets. He believes training investments will not be worthless and that enterprises like VanEck will not contract with open-source wrappers due to institutional requirements.
- Bitcoin and gold outperformed, with slight decoupling from Nasdaq. Matthew says there are always sellers, but divided government may lead to more spending and dollar weakness, which is bullish for BTC. The bear case is that Bitcoin remains linked to liquidity and would fall if credit markets tighten.
- Matthew's personal allocation is 80% SPY, with the rest split between Bitcoin, his own fund, and venture. He has strong conviction that monetary debasement will continue, driving Bitcoin adoption and appreciation for power infrastructure crucial to both Bitcoin and AI.
- At the Galaxy event in New Orleans, C-suite executives from major banks and financial institutions expressed that money and markets are moving onto public blockchains. Jason Yanowitz put his conviction at 95% that stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities, and capital markets will move onto public blockchains, with the key investment question being whether value accrues to equity or tokens.
- Matthew expressed skepticism on tokenization and stablecoins: stablecoin AUM declined this quarter, ETFs are the original tokens, and there is pricing pressure across exchanges and brokers. He sees no winner-take-most dynamics in tokenized equities. He prefers Figure over Circle due to Figure's 50% margins, 100% YoY growth, and 20x earnings valuation vs Palantir's 100x; he avoids money-losing small caps.
- Rob argues stablecoin market cap decline is offset by tokenized cash equivalents, with stablecoin velocity up 3x since the GENIUS Act. Payments use is growing 25-35% quarter-over-quarter; Visa now does $25 billion in direct stablecoin settlement vs $7 billion a year ago. Stripe and Rain pipelines include major companies, and stablecoins serve as digital oil for tokenized assets.
- Prediction markets Polymarket and Kalshi were accurate in the midterms, missing only a few races. Rob emphasizes their role as information aggregators, disseminating consensus faster than polls or news, with 95-98% of visitors being non-traders. This utility likely makes them a hot topic in DC next year.
- Texas data center policy debate: gubernatorial candidate Hina Josa proposed a framework requiring union jobs, transparency, no NDAs, utility price protections, water neutrality, and removal of tax subsidies. Rob views these as reasonable but potentially slowing buildout. Matthew notes a WSJ report that one Democratic governor wants to restart data center construction to save jobs.
