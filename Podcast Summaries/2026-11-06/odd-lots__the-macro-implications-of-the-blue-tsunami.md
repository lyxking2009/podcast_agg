# The Macro Implications of the Blue Tsunami — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
The episode analyzes the macro implications of a Democratic sweep in the midterms, dubbed a 'blue tsunami.' Guest Skanda Amarnath discusses how Senate control reshapes Federal Reserve appointments, tariffs, and state-level data center politics, while markets adjust to the political shift. Key macro risks remain from AI capex concentration and sticky inflation driven by energy and tariffs.

## Key points
- Democrats won both the House and Senate in a 'blue tsunami' exceeding expectations, with Nate Silver's prediction of a blue wave proving correct and the Senate not being a coin flip as many believed.
- The Senate's structural GOP advantage due to equal state representation persists, but the election showed Democrats can compete in traditionally red states like South Carolina, Mississippi, Texas, and Kansas, reshaping the political map and party strategy.
- Market reaction initially contradicted pre-election expectations: the S&P 500 rose 1.5% the day after the Democratic win, but tech and political-linked stocks like SpaceX and Tesla fell sharply, reflecting an unwind of the 2024 Trump trade.
- Texas transformed into a deep blue state overnight, with Democrats winning all statewide races including comptroller and lieutenant governor; this affects the state's business environment, including its new business court designed to compete with Delaware.
- California voters rejected a wealth tax and passed Proposition 42 banning new taxes on personal property like business holdings and financial assets, potentially making California more attractive for business despite blue politics.
- Divided government likely means gridlock; Trump can still use executive orders for tariffs, but some Republican senators from agricultural states like John Thune may peel off to restrict executive trade power due to costs to farmers.
- Senate control shapes Federal Reserve appointments: Jay Powell's term ends early 2028, and with Democratic Senate scrutiny, filling vacancies will be difficult; Trump's previous failed nominees (Judy Shelton, Steve Moore, Marvin Goodfriend) illustrate the challenge.
- Data center construction faces a sea change: key AI states (Texas, Georgia, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Virginia, Wisconsin) now have Democratic governors, and OpenAI is altering its data center practices; state-level politics will now shape AI infrastructure more than federal.
- The AI boom dominates macro: AI-sensitive segments approach 8% of GDP (tech hardware, software, industrial equipment), and a sudden cooling of AI capex would be binary and unlikely to be offset quickly by housing or other sectors.
- Inflation risks persist from three sources: Strait of Hormuz disruptions raising jet fuel and airfares, diesel prices affecting food and transport costs, and tariffs not yet fully passed through, especially autos; core inflation may stay near 3% longer than expected.
- For 2028, a Democratic socialist on the presidential ticket is unlikely; Jon Ossoff is emerging as frontrunner due to his large Senate victory, with governors Andy Beshear and Josh Shapiro also potential candidates.
