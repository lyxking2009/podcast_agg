# Friend Acquisition Rate — Money Stuff: The Podcast

## TL;DR
Matt Levine and Mary Childs discuss how Kalshi's prediction markets allowed Levine to go to bed early on election night, praising their social utility despite skepticism about hedging. They also analyze the collapse of law firm Weil Gotshal, attributing it to partner poaching and the lack of non-compete enforcement in legal ethics, and explore implications of AI for law firm economics. Finally, they cover the alleged North Korean hack of Binance for $6.3 billion, noting that half was frozen in stablecoins while the rest was laundered via decentralized exchanges, causing a 15% jump in Ether price.

## Key points
- Kalshi allowed Matt Levine to know the midterm election outcome early: Democrat-controlled Senate odds started around 73% at 5pm and rose to 98% by 8:30pm, enabling him to go to bed at his usual 8:30 target instead of watching results.
- Levine retracts some previous criticisms of Kalshi, praising its election contracts for making the information environment 'cleaner, nicer, more user friendly and more relaxing,' though he remains critical of sports gambling.
- Levine argues prediction markets are more useful for elections than sports because watching election results is unpleasant and people just want the answer, whereas watching sports is the point.
- Mary Childs questions the social utility of prediction markets beyond early bedtime; Levine agrees that real-world hedging use is overstated, citing Jeremy Mallets of Susquehanna who trades prediction markets and uses them for data center moratorium hedging but notes the market may not support tens of billions in hedges.
- The law firm Weil Gotshal collapsed due to a run on its partnership, not severe financial distress; top partners were poached by higher-paying firms, leading to a cascade of departures as average profit per partner fell.
- John Morley's paper on law firm runs is cited; such firms often have mild distress but collapse because key revenue generators leave, and law firms cannot use non-competes or equity lockups due to legal ethics rules designed for an era of gentlemanly practice.
- Levine contrasts law firms with investment banks, which use non-competes, gardening leave, and vesting stock to retain talent, making them less fragile.
- The rise of big platforms optimizing for private equity work has created a mercenary environment; Sujit at the FT noted law firms wanted hedge fund pay but also got hedge fund volatility.
- Levine discusses potential AI impact on law firms: either top partners leverage AI to do more work with fewer associates, earning $50 million, or clients bypass firms entirely, leaving firms unsettled; Weil collapse is not an AI story but part of that transition.
- If Levine had stayed in law at Wachtell Lipton, he likely would have died of overwork; he left due to physical breakdown. Wachtell's lockstep compensation still gentlemans, but average partner profit is around $10 million, and a single Elon Musk Twitter deal added $1 million per partner.
- The Binance hack allegedly by North Korea stole $6.3 billion; hackers used fake job interviews and social engineering to get an employee to download malware.
- About half the stolen funds were in centralized stablecoins like Tether and USDC; Binance called issuers and got those frozen within 40 minutes, effectively reversing the theft for that portion.
- The remaining crypto was irreversible; hackers converted stablecoins to crypto on decentralized exchanges, causing Ether price to jump 15% due to massive buy orders.
- Crypto trading firms on the other side described the trades as a great opportunity, effectively profiting from North Korea's money laundering, though they may not have known the source at the time.
- Levine argues that centralized stablecoins' reversibility is antithetical to crypto ideology but obviously superior for theft recovery, and that Binance's purpose is financial transactions, not permissionless irreversibility.
- Levine has become more worried about AI existential risk after reading Kevin Roose's AGI Chronicles and Dario Amodei's 'Pacing the Frontier' essay, noting Anthropic's stated preference to slow down AI development may now be satisfied by data center moratoriums and chip supply chain fragility.
- Gideon Lewis-Kraus's New Yorker story on semiconductor supply chain highlights the fragility of chip manufacturing; a sneeze in a TSMC fab could set back AI by three years, and tapping expertise is not easily replicated by AI.
- There is discussion of behavioral implications of prediction markets: a cab driver might not vote for a candidate with low odds, suggesting markets may influence election outcomes in ways not fully understood.
