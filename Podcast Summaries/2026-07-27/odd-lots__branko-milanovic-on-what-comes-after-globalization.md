# Branko Milanovic on What Comes After Globalization

**Show:** Odd Lots
**Date:** 2026-07-27

## Key Points
- Milanovic argues the neoliberal globalization order that dominated from ~1990 is expiring, replaced by what he calls 'national market liberalism' — domestically liberal (free pricing, competition) but internationally mercantilist (tariffs, beggar-thy-neighbor)
- His new book traces four economic orders since 1974: the New International Economic Order (failed), unipolar US dominance, neoliberal globalization, and now national market liberalism
- China is a special case that defies simple categorization — it adopted market principles within a communist framework, and 15 years ago nobody predicted its current technological capabilities
- The US-China relationship has shifted from pure economics to national competition — when people frame China as a 'threat,' they've already moved from economics into mercantilistic thinking
- Milanovic's personal trajectory mirrors the globalization era — he grew up in mandated Palestine/Israel, benefited from international education and career mobility, but sees the order that enabled this as less durable going forward
- The injection of market principles into communist systems (1989) was analogous to Keynes injecting socialism into capitalism after the Great Depression — both were attempts to save one system by borrowing from the other

## Overview
Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway interview Branko Milanovic, research professor at CUNY and author of 'The Great Global Transformation,' about the end of neoliberal globalization and what comes next. Milanovic describes his entire life as spanning four distinct economic orders and argues we're witnessing the birth of 'national market liberalism' — a system that keeps liberal domestic economics but abandons free trade internationally in favor of mercantilism. The conversation covers China's unique development path, the tension between viewing China as an economic partner vs. security threat, and how the post-WWII order that benefited educated elites like the hosts may be less durable in the decades ahead.

## Implications
For investors and economists: the shift from globalization to national market liberalism means supply chains, trade relationships, and capital flows will be increasingly shaped by geopolitical competition rather than comparative advantage. The framework Milanovic provides — distinguishing domestic liberalism from international mercantilism — offers a more precise lens than 'deglobalization' for understanding policy shifts. The China discussion suggests the threat framing has already biased economic analysis toward zero-sum thinking.

## Notable Quotes
- "I am not so sure that the order from which I've enjoyed many fruits and privileges is going to be as durable in the second half" — Branko Milanovic
- "What is happening now is that we take this entire international part, chop it off and say, these rules do not apply anymore. What applies now is essentially national sort of mercantilist policy" — Branko Milanovic
- "I was trying to save socialism and Jeff [Sachs] kind of looks at me. He says, I'm trying to bury socialism" — Branko Milanovic on his 1989 misunderstanding with Jeffrey Sachs
- "Once you start speaking of the threat to somebody, then we are really no longer in economics as we studied it. We are now exactly in that world of mercantilistic international competition" — Branko Milanovic
- "Fifteen years ago, nobody expected what has happened. And if people tell me they did expect, I just go back to what they wrote, and they got Nobel prizes for writing that China would never technologically develop" — Branko Milanovic
