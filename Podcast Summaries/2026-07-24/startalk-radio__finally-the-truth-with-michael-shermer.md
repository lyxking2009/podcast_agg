## Key Points

- The episode explores whether objective truth exists in an era of motivated reasoning, misinformation, and alternative facts
- Fallibilism — the principle that you might be wrong about anything — is the foundation for why we keep checking and verifying
- Carl Sagan's invisible dragon in the garage thought experiment illustrates why internal conviction isn't enough without external validation
- The brain functions more like a lawyer than a scientist — it starts with a conclusion and builds the case, rather than following evidence objectively
- AI confabulation and overconfidence are new threats to truth-seeking — clicking the actual source still matters
- Bayesian reasoning helps navigate different fields (history, religion, literature) that have different relationships to truth
- Religious stories work as literature but clash with truth when they demand scientific standards of evidence
- The biases most likely to lead you astray: motivated reasoning, hindsight bias, and patternicity

## Overview

Neil deGrasse Tyson, Chuck Nice, and Gary O'Reilly sit down with science historian and professional skeptic Michael Shermer to build a toolkit for finding truth in an era of misinformation. They explore fallibilism, Carl Sagan's invisible dragon thought experiment, why the brain functions like a lawyer not a scientist, AI confabulation, Bayesian reasoning, and the biases most likely to lead you astray. Shermer argues that objective truth exists and that humans, uniquely among species, are getting better at finding it.

## Implications

In an age of AI-generated content and deepfakes, the epistemological toolkit discussed becomes even more critical. The distinction between 'my truth' and 'objective truth' has real consequences for policy, science, and public discourse. AI confabulation adds a new dimension to the post-truth challenge — automated misinformation at scale.
