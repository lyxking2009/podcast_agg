## Key Points

- The franchise business model pioneered the legal framework that enabled the gig economy — Uber, Amazon DSPs, and gig platforms built on franchise-era legal precedents
- Franchisors in the 1960s-70s fought strategic legal battles to gain control over independent businesses without being held liable as employers
- McDonald's owns the land under its restaurants, giving it a 'hammer' over franchisees — if they lose the trademark, they get evicted from their own business
- Technology (broadband, POS systems, AI) now gives franchisors more control over workers than vertically integrated corporations like US Steel had in 1950
- Franchise workers earn lower wages than company-owned locations (Starbucks > Dunkin', Chipotle > Taco Bell) and have higher safety violations
- The Taft-Hartley Act (1947) makes it illegal for franchise workers to strike against or target the parent corporation — only their immediate employer
- Amazon's Delivery Service Partner (DSP) model is closer to franchising than Uber — drivers show up at Amazon facilities, wear Amazon uniforms, drive Amazon trucks, but are employed by contractors
- AI now enables real-time surveillance of franchise workers — headquarters can monitor individual employee performance and send notes to franchisees

## Overview

Brian Calacci joins Odd Lots to discuss how the franchise restaurant business model created the legal and economic framework that enabled today's gig economy. The conversation traces how franchisors in the 1960s-70s fought strategic legal battles to control independent businesses without employer liability, creating a template that Amazon, Uber, and other gig platforms later adopted. The episode reveals how technology — from broadband POS systems to AI surveillance — has given franchisors unprecedented control over workers, while the legal structure shields them from responsibility for wages, safety, and unionization.

## Implications

The franchise model's legal loopholes have fundamentally reshaped American labor relations. The 'joint employer' rule flip-flops between administrations create regulatory uncertainty. AI-powered worker surveillance in franchise/gig settings raises new questions about the nature of employment. Policy proposals to close the franchise loophole could have massive implications for Amazon, Uber, and the broader gig economy.
