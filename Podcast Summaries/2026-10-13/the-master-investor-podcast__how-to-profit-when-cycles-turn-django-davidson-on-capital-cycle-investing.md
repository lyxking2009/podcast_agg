## Overview

Wilfred Frost interviews Django Davidson, founding partner and portfolio manager at Hosking Partners, an $8 billion global investment firm built on the capital cycle approach. Davidson explains how high profits attract devastating competition, why low returns on capital can be excellent entry points, and why outperformance requires measuring supply and replacement cost rather than forecasting demand. He argues the zero-rate era was an aberration, that a real cost of capital has revived mean reversion and creative destruction, and applies the framework live: Hosking bought memory chip makers Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix a decade ago and is now selling as enterprise value to replacement cost flashes red; copper and platinum are core holdings on supply shortage; the "Death of the Brand" thesis he published a decade ago called Nike's current decline; and Japan is the firm's biggest country bet.

## Key Points

- Capital cycle investing measures supply and replacement cost, not demand forecasts and growth narratives — high profits attract competition that destroys those profits, while low returns on capital create entry points.
- The key skill is spotting cycle turning points before the crowd.
- The zero-interest-rate era was an aberration; the return of a real cost of capital revived mean reversion and creative destruction.
- Memory chips: Hosking bought Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix roughly a decade ago on the capital cycle framework and is now selling, because enterprise value to replacement cost has reached a red-flag level.
- Copper and platinum are core holdings because of acute supply shortages and long lead times; gold is described as less constructive.
- His decade-old "Death Of The Brand" thesis predicted Nike's current fall from grace.
- Japan is Hosking's largest single country bet — they see it at the start of a long upswing.
- He also covers US capital returns declining, buying chemicals, unconstrained global mandates, and rising yields.
- Recorded 12 October 2026.

## Implications

The actionable position is explicit and contrarian: sell the AI-driven memory complex on replacement-cost grounds, own copper and platinum for supply reasons, and rotate toward Japan. The framework's testable claim is that EV/replacement cost is the signal that matters — a metric that most equity research does not track, which is precisely why the approach can find turning points early.
