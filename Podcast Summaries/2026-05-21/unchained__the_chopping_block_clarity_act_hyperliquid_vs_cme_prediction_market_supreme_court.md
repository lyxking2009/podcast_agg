## Key Points
- The CLARITY Act cleared the Senate Banking Committee on a bipartisan vote, but two sticking points remain: stablecoin yield provisions and a presidential ethics clause targeting officials profiting from crypto ventures like World Liberty Financial
- The stablecoin yield compromise settled on a "do stuff yield" framework — transaction-based rewards are permitted, but passive bank-deposit-style yields are not — satisfying both crypto platforms (Coinbase) and banking lobby concerns
- Even if the CLARITY Act passes immediately, actual compliance requirements are years away: the 309-page bill triggers ~45 rulemakings across Treasury, SEC, and CFTC; near-term impact for token founders is minimal with mandatory disclosures being the clearest early requirement
- CME and ICE are lobbying Congress and the CFTC to force Hyperliquid into full CFTC registration, citing market manipulation and sanctions evasion risks; Hyperliquid counters that on-chain transparency exceeds opaque centralized orderbooks
- Hyperliquid's pre-IPO markets demonstrated significant price discovery advantages over traditional investment banking: Cerebras shares were priced at ~$1.30 by bankers but traded at ~$2.80 on Hyperliquid — validating the decentralized market's assessment
- Real-world asset (RWA) perpetuals now make up roughly half of Hyperliquid's trading volume, making its challenge to CME and ICE increasingly concrete
- If U.S.-regulated perpetuals markets open up, the panel expects Coinbase and Robinhood to dominate due to existing distribution advantages rather than pure DeFi entrants
- A circuit split is forming in prediction market litigation: the Third Circuit ruled CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction; the Ninth Circuit is expected to rule differently, setting up a likely Supreme Court showdown by 2027
- The panel debated whether the CFTC's "economic impact" test could draw a line between high-stakes macro event contracts (e.g., MLB championships) and frivolous parlay markets (e.g., single-player props) — with the latter potentially falling under state gambling authority
- The SEC is rumored to release an innovation exemption permitting tokenized equities trading by third parties; Rebecca Rettig was skeptical, noting the SEC likely coordinates timing with CLARITY passage to avoid conflicting frameworks
- Rebecca Rettig introduced the term "on-chain finance" to distinguish platforms like Hyperliquid (closed-source, operationally constrained) from open-source permissionless DeFi; the panel immediately and humorously rejected the terminology

## Overview
In this episode of The Chopping Block, hosts Haseeb Qureshi, Tom Schmidt, and Tarun Chitra are joined by Rebecca Rettig (Chief Legal Officer, Jito Labs) for a deep-dive into the three biggest regulatory fronts facing crypto in May 2026. They walk through the CLARITY Act's committee passage and remaining political landmines, the escalating institutional war between CME/ICE and Hyperliquid over derivatives market turf, and the widening legal battle over prediction markets that is now on a path to the Supreme Court. The episode functions as a mid-season regulatory recap, with the panel assessing both the near-term and multi-year implications of each development.

## Implications
The convergence of the CLARITY Act's progress, traditional exchange lobbying against Hyperliquid, and mounting prediction market litigation signals that crypto is entering a phase of forced regulatory integration — where the question is no longer whether oversight comes, but which incumbents and newcomers will be positioned to win once it does. The outcome across these three fronts will largely determine which platforms dominate U.S. derivatives and event-contract trading through 2027 and beyond.
