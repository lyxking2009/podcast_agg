# Super Solar Storms with Lika Guhathakurta — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR
Neil deGrasse Tyson and comedian Chuck Nice discuss the heliosphere, the Parker Solar Probe, and solar storms with heliophysicist Lika Guhathakurta. The episode covers what's beneath the sun's surface, how heliophysicists infer internal activity, how the sun maintains mass despite radiating energy, the sounds the sun would make, the heliosphere's protective role, how NASA spacecraft punched through it, coronal mass ejections and auroras, and what another Carrington Event would do to electronics on Earth and in orbit.

## Key points
- Inside the sun: heliophysicists use techniques like helioseismology to infer activity beneath the surface — how we know what's going on inside the sun.
- Mass conservation: how the sun maintains its mass despite all the energy it radiates since starting fusion.
- Solar sound: what the sun would sound like if you could hear it in space, and how that differs between large and small stars.
- Boiling plasma: whether you can see boiling plasma on the sun's surface.
- The heliosphere: the region influenced by the solar wind, and how it shields us from solar radiation; NASA spacecraft that have 'punched through' it (Voyagers).
- Parker Solar Probe: its mission and what it's doing now — the closest-ever sampling of the solar atmosphere.
- Coronal mass ejections (CMEs): how they interact with Earth's atmosphere and create auroras; Neil and Lika's personal experiences with solar storms.
- The Carrington Event scenario: could a CME wipe out all electronics on Earth or in orbit, and what another Carrington-level geomagnetic storm would mean.

## Overview
A classic StarTalk science episode: Guhathakurta (a heliophysicist long associated with NASA's Living With a Star program) walks Tyson and Nice through the modern picture of the sun and the solar wind. Topics progress from internal structure and helioseismology to the heliosphere's protective bubble, the Voyager crossings into interstellar space, and the Parker Solar Probe's ongoing close passes. The fun half covers solar acoustics, boiling plasma, and aurora mechanics, then turns serious: CMEs, the risk to power grids and satellites, and the historical benchmark of the 1859 Carrington Event. The episode lands on how solar-storm monitoring protects modern infrastructure.

## Implications
- Space weather is a real infrastructure risk: a Carrington-scale CME could disrupt power grids, satellites, and electronics globally — monitoring (Parker Solar Probe, SOHO, etc.) is the first line of defense.
- The heliosphere's shielding role matters for deep-space missions and any future Mars colonization planning.
