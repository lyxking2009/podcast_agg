# Eric Vishria - A Decade of Lessons Investing in Software & Hardware - [Invest Like the Best, EP.486] — Invest Like the Best with Patrick O'Shaughnessy

## TL;DR
Eric Vishria, partner at Benchmark, reflects on a decade of investing in software and hardware, highlighting lessons from companies like Fireworks, Cerebras, and Sierra. He argues that running large AI models is far harder than it appears, leading to significant performance differences even with the same hardware. Vishria draws parallels to the early days of cloud computing, predicting an oligopoly of winners in AI rather than a single dominant player, and emphasizes that the biggest mistake is underestimating market size. He also discusses the changing nature of SaaS, where AI shifts the competitive frontier, making database migration trivial and altering how product development should be done. Vishria shares his approach to investing: prioritizing deep partnerships with exceptional founders, questioning inherited assumptions, and focusing on opportunities where success would truly matter. He touches on robotics, energy bottlenecks, and the importance of naivete in pursuing ambitious hardware ventures.

## Key points
- Running large AI models (2-4 trillion parameters) is extremely hard, with 5x performance differences between providers on the same open-source model and hardware, making inference a deep expertise play rather than a commodity.
- The AI market will likely result in an oligopoly of winners, not a single company dominating everything, similar to how cloud evolved with AWS, Azure, GCP, and smaller players like Cloudflare.
- Enterprise AI adoption is faster and more enthusiastic than cloud adoption was, with enterprises treating AI as both an opportunity and a threat, creating a need for 'AI Sherpas' to guide them.
- The competitive frontier for SaaS has completely shifted: AI makes database migration trivial, changes evaluation criteria for databases, and upends product development by requiring deep understanding of AI's 'jagged edge' of capabilities.
- Traditional product management is obsolete; effective teams now combine understanding of customer problems, taste, and AI's jagged capabilities to build valuable solutions.
- AI companies pull demand rather than push it, breaking traditional sales quota capacity models; top salespeople act like founders, bridging AI capabilities to customer needs.
- Many experienced leaders from the previous generation fail in AI companies because they rely on outdated mental models, such as quota-based sales planning.
- SaaS companies faced a stark choice: embrace AI or be worth only 3x revenue; hitting traditional plans can destroy equity value if it ignores the AI shift.
- Hardware investing is vastly different from software: it involves physics, supply chains, and a 'roofline' where software improvements slowly approach theoretical maximum performance.
- Cerebras invested in wafer-scale chips to maximize cores, communication, and memory proximity for deep learning, a bet that took years to pay off and required productive naivete.
- A new CPU architecture opportunity is emerging because AI-generated code runs on CPUs designed for legacy constraints, and new designs could be more efficient.
- Robotics needs internet-scale data to bootstrap models; current efforts focus on gathering high-value data first, akin to how LLMs prioritized quality data sources, with the key being a pre-training flywheel.
- The energy bottleneck could limit AI progress; China is bringing on 10x more energy capacity than the US, potentially impacting token costs and availability.
- Smart predictions can be wrong due to real-world complexities: Geoff Hinton's 2016 claim that radiologists would be replaced by AI missed data fragmentation, reimbursement models, and liability issues, leading to a co-pilot scenario instead.
- The best investment questions include: 'If it's right, does it matter?' and 'Could I talk someone I care about into joining this company as their life's work?'
- Benchmark's new growth fund is justified because the circle of high cash-on-cash multiple opportunities has expanded beyond early-stage due to larger outcomes.
