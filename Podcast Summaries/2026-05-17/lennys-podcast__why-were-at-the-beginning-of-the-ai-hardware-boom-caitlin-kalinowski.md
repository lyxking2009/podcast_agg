# Lenny's Podcast: Why we're at the beginning of the AI hardware boom | Caitlin Kalinowski (ex–OpenAI, Meta, Apple)

**Guest:** Caitlin Kalinowski — previously at OpenAI (robotics/hardware team builder), Meta (head of AR glasses & VR hardware, first consumer electronics hire), and Apple (technical lead on MacBook Air, Mac Pro, original unibody MacBook Pro). Now focused on robotics.

## Key Points

1. **VR was a necessary technological stepping stone** that taught us spatial computing, SLAM positioning, and depth sensing technologies that are now foundational for robotics and autonomous systems. While VR itself remained niche due to social isolation issues, the R&D investment has proven invaluable for the physical AI revolution.

2. **AI is approaching saturation in digital-only applications**, creating a dawning realization in labs that the next frontier is the physical world — robotics, manufacturing, industrialization, and space. This represents a fundamental shift from keyboard-based AI to embodied intelligence that can manipulate the real world.

3. **Hardware development is fundamentally different from software** — you only get to "compile" your design 4-5 times ever, compared to software's unlimited iterations. This requires extreme conservatism, extensive reliability testing, and solving for the last half-percent of edge cases because you can't ship over-the-air updates to physical products.

4. **Supply chain dependencies on China and Asia represent a critical vulnerability**, especially for actuators, magnets, and memory components essential to robotics. The U.S. needs to re-industrialize significantly to maintain military and economic security, as people who are allies today may not be allies tomorrow.

5. **Memory prices are experiencing a "meteor" crisis** driven by AI data center demand, potentially doubling again after already increasing 6x. This represents an existential threat to consumer hardware and robotics companies that are less cost-insensitive than enterprise AI buyers, forcing companies to pre-buy inventory or face supply shortages.

6. **Humanoid robots face significant safety challenges** and aren't necessarily the answer to most automation needs. Specialized robots designed for specific tasks are more practical for manufacturing, while humanoids may serve long-tail applications. The key safety consideration is impact energy — both from moving arms and actuator rotation — requiring softer, lighter designs.

7. **Building successful hardware teams in the AI era** requires a mix of strong generalists who can adapt across fields, some specialists with scaling experience, and critically, "AI-native" young engineers (20-21 years old) who use AI tools fundamentally differently than older engineers. Mission alignment is essential to unify teams across different technical backgrounds.

8. **Apple's hardware excellence culture** emphasizes understanding the "why" behind every design decision, with obsessive attention to detail (even the back of the cabinet) that forces methodical thinking about what really matters. This creates simple-looking end products that emerge from comprehensive consideration of every component and assembly.

9. **Current AI models are inadequate for real engineering work** because they don't understand physics, friction, weight, contact pressure, or material properties. CAD and engineering require world models that can predict physical behavior, not just text generation or video prediction capabilities.

10. **Military technology is changing faster than consumer electronics**, with drone warfare representing a fundamental shift toward cheaper, adaptable weapons systems. The U.S. needs to invest more in drones than aircraft carriers and develop capabilities to counter potential swarms of 100,000 drones from adversaries.

## Overview

Caitlin Kalinowski, a veteran hardware leader from Apple, Meta, and OpenAI, provides a comprehensive view of why we're entering an AI hardware boom. The discussion transitions to the broader trend of AI reaching saturation in digital-only applications, forcing labs and companies to look toward the physical world as the next frontier. A significant portion covers critical supply chain vulnerabilities, particularly around China dependencies for actuators, magnets, and memory components, with implications for both commercial robotics and national security. She addresses the practical realities of humanoid robots versus specialized automation, safety considerations around robots interacting with humans, and the skills needed to build hardware teams in the AI era. ## Implications

For founders entering AI hardware: understand that hardware is fundamentally different from software with no room for iteration after manufacturing. Focus on solving the hardest technical problems first, secure supply chains early (especially for memory and actuators), and prepare for 3-5x longer development cycles. For engineers: developing "AI-native" workflows is becoming essential, but current AI tools are still inadequate for core engineering tasks like CAD and physics simulation. The biggest opportunities may be in specialized robotics rather than humanoid generalists. For PMs: success requires obsessive attention to detail and clear KPIs defined upfront, as hardware can't adapt to changing requirements mid-development. Supply chain risk management is now a core product strategy, not just an operational concern. For investors: the AI hardware boom is real but faces significant bottlenecks in manufacturing capacity, supply chain independence, and safety validation that will determine winners and losers.
