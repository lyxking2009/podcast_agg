# The World’s Largest Electric Aircraft Just Flew — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
Anders Forslund, CEO of Heart Aerospace, recounts the seven-year journey from a 3D-printed model at Y Combinator to flying the world's largest electric aircraft—a 100-ft wingspan demonstrator with 25,000 lb takeoff weight that costs just $5 in electricity to get airborne. The company is now building the ES-30, a 30–36 seat hybrid electric regional airliner that can fly 125 miles all-electric or 500 miles as a hybrid, recharge in 30 minutes, and targets the underserved short-haul market dominated by 40-year-old turboprops. Key insights include using simple electric motors with one moving part, solving reserve requirements with a hybrid turboprop, and applying SpaceX-style risk philosophy to build a software-defined aircraft.

## Key points
- Heart Aerospace's full-scale demonstrator is the world's largest electric aircraft, with a 100-ft wingspan, 25,000 lb takeoff weight, and electricity cost of just $5 to get off the ground; its successor ES-30 will fly up to 125 miles on battery alone, up to 500 miles as hybrid, and recharge in about 30 minutes.
- CEO Anders Forslund and co-founder Klara started Heart Aerospace seven years ago with only a 3D-printed model at Y Combinator; the company now has a 40-person team in LA building a real airliner.
- Heart's mission is to reduce the cost of air travel by building hybrid electric aircraft, using electric motors that have essentially one moving part, no combustion, and near-zero wear, compared to thousands of parts in jet engines.
- The plane is powered by eight battery packs laid across the fuselage floor, equivalent in energy to about four Tesla battery packs, replacing roughly $40 worth of jet fuel per flight.
- Jet engines are inefficient for short regional flights: taxiing alone can consume 10% of fuel on short routes, and engine complexity/cost is similar for 30-seat and 70-seat planes, pushing the industry toward larger aircraft and longer routes.
- Half of all flights worldwide are under 2 hours, and the regional aircraft market consists of 40-year-old designs; Heart is targeting replacement not driven by cost but by obsolescence and fuel inefficiency.
- Heart's operating economics have improved from 33% to 48% better overall compared to conventional turboprops due to rising oil prices, making electric regional flight increasingly cost-competitive.
- Anders Forslund was inspired by Elon Musk's 2012 MIT talk where Musk predicted all transport except rockets would go electric and cited 400 Wh/kg battery targets; Forslund later did a PhD on jet engines and researched electric planes for the Swedish government before founding Heart.
- Early traction came from letters of intent from SAS, Braathens, and other Nordic airlines before Heart had any real technology; then building a 400 kW electric motor (the size of a small jet engine) attracted pre-orders from United Airlines, which came via an unsolicited email found in the spam folder.
- For hardware startups, Forslund advises that every time you need more capital you must show something material and ideally physical, like a working motor, not just slides or LOIs.
- Heart deliberately designed the ES-30 to look like a conventional turboprop rather than a futuristic aircraft, aiming to replace existing regional planes without appearing radical.
- The company builds many aircraft components in-house, including a 6061 aluminum actuator cylinder with about 15 machined parts, using the same architecture for aileron, rudder, landing gear, braking, and propeller pitch; this allows rapid iteration compared to sourcing from sub-suppliers.
- The pilot plant is wired as a 1.6-megawatt test bench that can simulate flight loads and perform fault injection testing, including cutting wires and introducing programming errors, to ensure systems work even when things go wrong.
- The cockpit is inspired by Tesla's single-screen Model 3 interface and was designed by an engineer who worked on the Dragon space capsule; it can seat up to 36 passengers but will most commonly carry 30, giving each passenger an extra 6 inches of legroom.
- Heart's battery lab tests cells from Chinese, American, and Korean manufacturers; the best cell currently on the table is 370 Wh/kg, and a 400 Wh/kg cell is expected within a couple of months, meeting first-generation aircraft targets.
- The hardest engineering challenge is reserve requirements: because 1 in 1,000 US flights gets diverted and may need 45 minutes of loiter plus a 100-mile diversion, a pure battery-electric aircraft would have to carry 2/3 of its battery capacity as reserves; Heart solved this with a hybrid turboprop engine.
- The hybrid system uses a simple, inexpensive turboprop engine that adds about 20% to upfront cost but enables all-electric flights up to 225 miles and hybrid flights up to 500 miles, providing the necessary reserve capability.
- Heart's risk philosophy, influenced by SpaceX, contrasts with traditional aerospace: instead of minimizing probability of failure because impact is always catastrophic, they focus on minimizing the impact of being wrong, allowing cheaper and faster iteration; planes crash due to broken logic, not broken wings, so they build software-defined aircraft.
- Heart chose to attack the mainline regional airplane market rather than flying taxis or helicopters, because their 36-seat aircraft uses existing airport infrastructure (5,000 US airports) and targets routes underserved by current technology.
- Compared to hydrogen, hybrid electric already has a negative green premium, and the planes being replaced are 40 years old; Heart argues their aircraft will become an appreciating asset that improves over time like buying a house.
- Long-term, Heart plans to build larger narrow-body aircraft to compete with the 737/A320 backlog, similar to what SpaceX did for rockets; they also foresee remote piloting and eventual autonomy starting with cargo.
- The first flight of the full-scale demonstrator took place on Wednesday, August 12th, in Plattsburgh, New York, seven years after the YC Demo Day with a 3D-printed model.
- Forslund advises aspiring hardware founders to start a company because they enjoy a specific problem, not just to be a founder, and to know enough to recognize great people when they meet them.
