# Why Laser Beams Are the Hottest New Tech in Defense — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Odd Lots speaks with Wayne Sanders, senior defense research analyst at Bloomberg Intelligence and former Army colonel, about directed energy weapons—lasers and high-powered microwaves—and their potential to change the cost calculus of air defense. Sanders explains the differences between precision lasers and wide-area microwave systems, reviews decades of R&D including chemical lasers, and highlights current systems like the Army's 50 kW Stryker lasers, the Navy's HELIOS, and Israel's 150 kW Iron Beam. He also covers the market outlook ($6-8 billion today, growing 2-3x over the next decade), supply chain risks from rare earths, and the human-in-the-loop approach to AI in weapon systems.

## Key points
- Wayne Sanders graduated from West Point in 2002, served in military intelligence, signals intelligence, NSA, US Cyber Command, and as an offensive cyberspace operations officer (17 Alpha); his last Army role was Chief of Staff for the Undersecretary of Defense for Research and Engineering under Heidi Hsu before joining Bloomberg Intelligence.
- Directed energy weapons split into lasers, which concentrate energy into a narrow beam to attack one target at a time via heat, and high-powered microwaves, which use a larger beam to attack electronics and can affect multiple systems simultaneously—analogous to a sniper rifle versus an electronic shotgun; GAO notes HPM weapons can generate peak powers up to 100 MW, but this is not directly comparable to laser kW ratings.
- Military interest in lasers dates to the 1960s, with Star Wars-era chemical lasers; programs like the US-Israel Tactical High Energy Laser (THEL) and DARPA's MIRACL demonstrated destruction of rockets and artillery, but chemical lasers were too large and logistically intensive, leading to program termination in 2006; the YAL-1 airborne laser on a Boeing 747 used chemical oxygen iodine but was impractical.
- Current operational or near-operational systems include the Navy's HELIOS on USS Preble, Army Stryker vehicles with 50 kW lasers deployed in multi-domain task forces, and Israel's Iron Beam (also called Laser Dome) at about 150 kW, which is the leading real-world testbed; Epirus has high-powered microwave contracts with the Army and Marine Corps, including the Leonidas H2O to disable boat motors.
- Power generation and compression remain the main bottleneck; current 50 kW lasers can knock down a drone, but the Pentagon seeks a one megawatt laser by 2030 to counter hypersonic weapons traveling at Mach 5 (4,000 mph). Size, weight, and power (SWaP) constraints mean many systems rely on shore power or large generators, limiting deployability.
- Cost per shot for laser and microwave weapons is very low as long as energy is available, but they are short-range; layered integrated air and missile defense is essential, with exquisite interceptors like Lockheed's PAC-3 and RTX's SM-3/SM-6 reserved for hypersonic threats, while cheaper interceptors and guns (Coyote, Vampire, APKWS, Skynex/Skyranger) handle drones. Israel layers Arrow 2/3, David's Sling, Iron Dome, and now Iron Beam.
- Recent conflicts (Iran/Israel) have revealed the economic vulnerability of using $4 million interceptors against $30,000 drones; the US and allies face munitions depletion, driving demand for low-cost containerized munitions and directed energy. The DoD is pushing multi-year framework agreements to scale production: PAC-3 from 620 to 2,000 per year (3.3x), THAAD ($12-15M each) 4x, and Precision Strike Munition 4x.
- Short-term scaling favors primes like Lockheed Martin and RTX for exquisite munitions, but the middle tier of low-cost interceptors has been contracted to five companies: CoAspire, Leidos, Anduril, Zone 5, and Castilian, with demand for tens of thousands of units. Joint ventures with European firms such as Rheinmetall are also expanding capacity.
- The directed energy market is currently valued at $6-8 billion and projected to grow 2-3x over the next decade, with over $15 billion already spent on R&D; the transition from development to procurement is underway, though R&D continues for higher power levels.
- Supply chain risks are significant: critical rare earths such as terbium, dysprosium, and neodymium boron iron magnets are used in directed energy and other defense systems, with China controlling 85-90% of supply and adding 12 critical minerals export restrictions; the MP Materials deal aims at domestic onshoring, but dual-use demand complicates security.
- AI integration in weapon systems remains human-in-the-loop; target execution authority (TEA) is delegated to human commanders, and AI is used mainly for imagery analysis and change detection, not autonomous kill decisions. Trust models and policy keep humans responsible for any lethal action.
- Kill decisions are made through Target Execution Authority, with tactical commanders prioritizing self-defense without cost consideration, while higher strategic and national levels weigh economic factors; the goal is to give commanders multiple options—exquisite missiles, cheaper interceptors, guns, high-powered microwaves, and lasers—to avoid economic defeat.
