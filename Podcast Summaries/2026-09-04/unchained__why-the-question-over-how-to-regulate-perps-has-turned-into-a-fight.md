# Why the Question Over How to Regulate Perps Has Turned Into a Fight — Unchained

## TL;DR
This panel from the RWA Summit discusses the regulatory fight over perpetual futures ('perps'), focusing on whether they should be classified as swaps or futures. Panelists Jake Czerwinski, Tiffany Smith, and Katie Yun argue that perps are better classified as futures to allow retail access, citing standardization, pricing mechanisms, and smart contract technology. They also address CME's lawsuit against the CFTC, the need for fit-for-purpose regulation, and the potential for decentralized infrastructure like Hyperliquid to coexist with traditional intermediaries.

## Key points
- The central debate is whether perpetual futures should be classified as swaps or futures under US law, which determines access: swaps are predominantly institutional-only, while futures can be traded by retail customers.
- Jake Czerwinski of the Hyperliquidity Policy Center argues that perps should be classified as futures because they are more useful for a wide range of participants and legal analysis supports futures classification.
- Tiffany Smith of WilmerHale notes that perps have characteristics of both but fit futures better because they are standardized, not bilateral contracts like most swaps, and their pricing mechanism resembles futures.
- Katie Yun of Temporal emphasizes that smart contract-based perps are fundamentally different from traditional swaps; they are venue-oriented and can democratize access without requiring intermediaries.
- Confusion arises from past CFTC enforcement actions that treated perps as swaps, while a recent policy-making division decision classified them as futures, creating inconsistency in the market.
- CME Group filed a lawsuit against the CFTC, arguing that the process and decision to allow perpetual futures (such as for Coinbase and Crypto.com) was flawed and that perps should be swaps, revealing competitive tensions with incumbent exchanges.
- The CFTC has taken a 'crawl, walk, run' approach, starting with perps on digital assets before considering real-world assets like oil or agricultural commodities to assess systemic impact.
- Panelists argue that regulation must adapt to new technology: existing rules designed for intermediaries may not fit decentralized protocols, and regulators should focus on actual risks rather than forcing old frameworks.
- Jake Czerwinski states that Hyperliquid should not be treated as an exchange but as neutral infrastructure that any registered exchange can use, so regulatory requirements should apply to the exchange using it, not the blockchain itself.
- There is a need for harmonization between the SEC and CFTC because underlying assets can fall under either regime depending on whether they are securities or commodities.
- Panelists express concern that misclassification or over-regulation could drive innovation overseas and prevent US traders from accessing beneficial products.
