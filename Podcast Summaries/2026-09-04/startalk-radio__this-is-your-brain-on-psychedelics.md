# This Is Your Brain on Psychedelics — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR
Neuroscientist Gül Dölen explains how psychedelics like LSD, MDMA, psilocybin, and ibogaine reopen critical periods—windows of heightened neuroplasticity normally present only in early development—offering potential treatments for PTSD, stroke rehabilitation, and other conditions. She discusses her lab's octopus MDMA experiments showing that these effects operate at the molecular level even in animals lacking a cortex, and emphasizes the importance of set, setting, and therapeutic context. The episode also covers ibogaine's promise and cardiac risks, drug policy, and the philosophical implications of psychedelic experiences.

## Key points
- Gül Dölen, a UC Berkeley neuroscientist with an MD-PhD, studies how psychedelics reopen critical periods—developmental windows when the brain can easily learn and rewire—which were first described by Konrad Lorenz in 1935 for imprinting in geese.
- Critical periods govern many forms of learning, including language and social reward; there are dozens, and the inability to reopen them is a major reason brain diseases are hard to treat after the window closes.
- Psychedelics reopen critical periods through a shared downstream mechanism involving restoration of metaplasticity and extracellular matrix remodeling, even though different drugs bind different receptors: ketamine acts on NMDA glutamate receptors, psilocybin/LSD on serotonin 2A receptors, MDMA on monoamine transporters, and ibogaine on multiple targets including kappa opioid and NMDA receptors.
- The duration of critical period reopening scales with trip length: ketamine (~30–60 min trip) keeps the window open ~48 hours; LSD (~8–10 hr) about three weeks; MDMA and psilocybin (~3–6 hr) about two weeks; ibogaine (2–3 days) at least one month.
- In her octopus experiment, giving MDMA to normally asocial octopuses caused them to become social, demonstrating that complex behavioral effects can arise from molecular mechanisms conserved across species, even in animals lacking a cortex, nucleus accumbens, or amygdala.
- MDMA's acute effect differs from SSRIs: SSRIs block serotonin reuptake, while MDMA reverses the serotonin transporter to actively extrude serotonin into the synapse, producing a much larger serotonin release.
- Psychedelic effects are highly context-dependent; Dölen's lab found that MDMA reopens social reward learning only when given in a social context, not in isolation, aligning with Timothy Leary's 'set and setting' framework.
- The noetic property of psychedelics—the feeling that what one experiences is 'the really real'—was described by William James and is likely important for therapeutic effects, but it can also lead to narcissism or a messiah complex if misused.
- Apollo 14 astronaut Edgar Mitchell experienced a profound cosmic perspective during his return from the Moon, later founding the Institute of Noetic Sciences, illustrating non-drug ways to achieve altered states akin to critical period reopening.
- Deprivation experiments, such as blindfolding for two weeks, can reopen critical periods and produce mystical experiences phenomenologically similar to psychedelics, connecting ancient contemplative practices like Zen 'beginner's mind' to neurobiology.
- Ibogaine, derived from the Tabernanthe iboga tree in Gabon and used by the Bwiti tribe for at least 1,000 years, is an oneirogenic (dream-like) psychedelic with a 2–3 day trip and holds promise for opioid use disorder and PTSD, but carries cardiotoxicity risks that require magnesium stabilization.
- Texas has passed legislation to unlock state money for ibogaine research, and the National Institute on Drug Abuse awarded Harvard an $11 million grant for a phase 1 safety study, signaling growing interest despite Schedule I status.
- Dölen is collaborating with Johns Hopkins on a stroke rehabilitation trial using psilocybin to reopen motor learning critical periods, pairing the drug with physical and occupational therapy rather than psychotherapy.
- Psychedelics are not expected to help neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer's or Parkinson's because they do not cause neurons to regrow or halt degeneration; they primarily enhance plasticity and relearning.
- Alexander Shulgin and his wife Anne popularized MDMA for couples therapy before it became Schedule I, using it to foster vulnerability and communication rather than as an aphrodisiac.
- Dölen identified two subtypes of oxytocin neurons: large ones encoding 'mad love' (romantic/parental) and smaller ones encoding platonic or communal love, which evolved in parallel across species.
- The episode critiques drug criminalization, noting that marijuana and crack cocaine penalties disproportionately target Black communities, and argues for treating addiction as a health issue rather than incarceration.
- Dölen maintains a physicalist view: consciousness arises from molecular interactions, and psychedelics strengthen her conviction that reality is physical, not evidence of a non-physical realm.
