# Susan Kare: Designing Icons & Graphics For the Original Mac — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
Susan Kare recounts her path from art school to designing the original Macintosh's icons and fonts, emphasizing simplicity, metaphor, and working within constraints. She shares behind-the-scenes stories from Apple, Microsoft, NeXT, and Facebook, including the origins of the Happy Mac, command key symbol, and Chicago font. The talk also distills advice from design and tech luminaries like Paul Rand, Steve Jobs, and Alan Kay, highlighting the importance of meaningful design and iterative creativity.

## Key points
- Susan Kare, an art major with a PhD in art history and no computer experience, was hired as the Macintosh artist at Apple after her friend Andy Hertzfeld invited her to create bitmap graphics and typefaces for the new computer.
- The original Macintosh screen had a button labeled 'do it' which users misread as 'don't', so she changed it to 'okay' to avoid confusion.
- She designed the Chicago system font at 9 pixels cap height using only horizontal, vertical, and 45-degree lines, and was excited about proportional spacing which made text look much better than monospaced displays.
- Steve Jobs overruled naming fonts after Philadelphia suburbs, insisting on world-class cities, leading to names like New York and Geneva, which shipped with the Mac.
- Bill Atkinson wrote an icon editor for her that allowed toggling individual pixels and automatically generated hex code, which she called a marvel that eliminated the need for graph paper and erasers.
- The Happy Mac icon, a smiling computer shown during boot, was inspired by a button she received at age 14; it was designed to reassure users that the system was okay.
- She advocates for simplicity in icons, citing Scott McCloud's 'Understanding Comics': less detail makes faces universal so users can project themselves, like a simple pencil better representing writing than a specific fancy pen.
- She regrets the bomb icon for system failure because a user called Apple fearing the computer would explode; it taught her that even 'hidden' icons can have unintended consequences.
- She warns against depicting specific product designs in icons, such as the 3.5-inch floppy for save or the ImageWriter printer, because they become outdated; metaphors are more enduring.
- The command key symbol originated when Steve Jobs forbade using the Apple logo for keyboard shortcuts; she found the 'feature' symbol in a book, which later turned out to represent Borgholm Castle in Sweden.
- At Microsoft for Windows 3.0, she used 16 colors and dithering to create subtle, non-garish icons and also designed the Solitaire card deck.
- Facebook's digital gifts project, where users paid $1 to send images, taught her that cute items (teddy bears, hearts, kiss mark) vastly outsold expensive-looking ones; penguin editions always sold out.
- Paul Rand, the legendary designer, advised focusing on making things meaningful and memorable; he charged Steve Jobs $100,000 for one NeXT logo and refused to do multiple options.
- Alan Kay's user interface principle, quoted by Bud Tribble, is to not put everything at the same level; nest features so they appear as needed for ease of use.
- Steve Jobs gave her bad financial advice: he told her not to buy Apple stock and predicted she would never make more than $20,000 twiddling bits, but she later returned to hands-on design work.
- Andy Hertzfeld advised showing Steve Jobs multiple design options because he would reject a single option but could choose among several after criticizing.
