# #202 - Barry S. Strauss - "We Are In The Final Stages Of Western Collapse" — The Peter McCormack Show

## TL;DR
Peter McCormack speaks with Barry Strauss — Cornell historian, senior fellow at the Hoover Institution, and author of Ten Caesars — about whether the West is in its final stages of decline. Strauss frames the moment through Ibn Khaldun's theory of asabiyya (social cohesion): hard societies conquer soft ones, adopt luxury beliefs, soften, and get conquered in turn. He diagnoses Western failures (hollowed-out industrial base, underestimated China, failed education, assimilation-free immigration, a "flaccid" democracy), evaluates modern leaders through the lens of the five good emperors, and uses Rome's Third-Century Crisis — and its recovery under Aurelian, Diocletian, and Constantine — to argue that decline can be reversed, but usually only after a crisis forces change. His closing message: democracies, when called to the test, summon reserves of strength they don't know they have.

## Key points
- **The decline framework — asabiyya:** Ibn Khaldun's 14th-century cycle (Muqaddimah): societies start rough, tough, and poor, conquer soft wealthy civilizations, then their children soften, abandon martial values, adopt luxury beliefs, and criticize their own society — until the next raiders from the horizon take over; Europe and the UK are "one step ahead" of the US in this cycle.
- **"We are the same people":** technology changes but human nature doesn't — "there's nothing new under the sun"; Henry Ford's "history is bunk" is the classic American attitude, and it's exactly wrong; "we are luckily forewarned from history, so we should be forearmed."
- **If we want things to stay the same, everything has to change:** the line from The Leopard — change is inevitable; societies must embrace it while deciding what to preserve.
- **Western failures diagnosed:** the US underestimated China and allowed its industrial base to be hollowed out (rearming now means importing from China); China played a long strategic game in the Sun Tzu mold ("the height of the art of war is winning without fighting"); the Chinese are now trying to undercut Germany the same way.
- **Leadership qualities (from the good emperors):** proper education, the right mentors, enormous self-confidence with humility, a great second-in-command, a good partner who can tell you when you're wrong, intelligence, courage, communication — and incorruptibility; Marcus Aurelius was a wonderful philosopher but problematic as emperor because he had no military experience.
- **Assessing modern leaders:** Reagan, Churchill, FDR, and Thatcher were great (Thatcher overstayed); Netanyahu is great on foreign policy and the Iranian threat; Trump has "some elements of greatness" — turned US China policy around, an astonishing communicator with a historic comeback — but January 6 was "not so great," and his second-term Iran war conduct is "an awfully strange way" to run a war; Obama gets credit for covering preexisting conditions; Bukele brought law and order to El Salvador (with some innocents caught up in the crackdown).
- **Democracy needs to be hard, not flaccid:** Churchill's "worst system except for all the others"; a realist, pragmatic democracy must be able to do tough things in crisis (Defense of the Realm Act in WWI); authoritarianism's fatal flaw is that nobody can tell the emperor he has no clothes — and you can't vote the bums out.
- **Immigration and assimilation:** "you can only beat a story with a story"; insist on assimilation and civic education, "there's some things that are not negotiable"; not all values are equal — multiculturalism without citizenship meaning is "a recipe for disaster"; luxury beliefs (Rob Henderson) are held by people who "think they're invulnerable."
- **Education is failing:** public education teaches shame ("on stolen land, no one is illegal"), Howard Zinn's A People's History as the standard text; the answer is a revival of civics; hopeful sign: 1,500 classical academies in the US.
- **Rome's Third-Century Crisis as recovery model:** 50 years of revolving-door emperors, epidemics, invasions, and a more dangerous Sassanian Persia — then military reformers (Aurelian's walls, Diocletian's persecution and failed price freezes, Constantine's Christianity and the solidus — "the dollar of the Middle Ages") saved the empire for another two centuries; "a recipe that works for 200 years ain't bad"; Augustine's "sacking the Egyptians" — preserve the best of the old culture.
- **The Roman Republic's lesson — the middle class:** a successful republic needs a strong middle class as its backbone; the Gracchi were murdered for land reform, Marius/Sulla/Caesar/Octavian armed the poor ("proletarian" literally derives from the Latin for "breeders"), and the republic fell; in the late empire, the Senate became a debating society, government militarized, and flexibility was lost — "they invested in the military, they just invested in the wrong things."
- **Inflation and coin clipping:** Diocletian's wage-and-price freeze never works ("it won't work for Milei either"); Constantine's solidus stabilized the currency and caused real pain, but it worked.
- **Hard times make hard men:** Herodotus ends his Histories with Cyrus refusing a soft land — "soft lands make soft people"; we've lived in a bubble while rivals haven't; "we need more reality therapy, preferably not from epidemics and war."
- **Security warnings:** the US was blindsided by drones when Ukraine has the cutting edge for years; Russia and China "have plans for dividing the United States up into pieces" (the WWI German-Mexico playbook); revolutions need only tiny minorities (Lenin's sealed train, the Bolsheviks).
- **Hope:** people change and learn; Rome came back twice (Augustus, then the Third-Century reformers); Britain won WWII at its darkest hour; democracies summoned to the test "come up with reserves of strength that they don't know they have"; the military remains the most trusted US institution; "a little push... I'm hoping it will only take a small crisis, a small bit of pain for us to rise to the occasion."
