# Ben Thompson on Big Tech, China, and the AI Boom Running Out of Money — Invest Like the Best with Patrick O'Shaughnessy

## TL;DR
Patrick O'Shaughnessy brings on Stratechery founder Ben Thompson (EP.487) for a wide-ranging look at the winners and losers of the AI era. Thompson argues it would be dangerous for the US to "win" the AI race, walks through the capital curve (free cash flow to debt to Google's equity issuance to Nvidia's $500B pension-and-insurance vehicle) and the risk of a financing "air gap" before AI revenue bridges the gap, explains why aggregation theory's zero-marginal-cost logic breaks in an era of real inference costs, and makes the case that advertising is the natural consumer AI business model. He also covers TSMC's offloading of risk onto big tech (and how scarcity saved Intel), Amazon's "first best customer" flywheel, Microsoft's IBM playbook, Meta's under-sold ad business, and why power — not chips — may be the durable asset the AI bubble produces.

## Key points
- **Winning the AI race is a dangerous goal:** if the US achieved meaningful AI superiority, the game-theory optimal response from China would be to blow up TSMC; the degree of US dependence on China is underappreciated and won't be fixed outside a conflict — "paying an insurance policy that's astronomically expensive, you're just not going to pay it" (Apple moving only a sliver of iPhone manufacturing to India).
- **The current equilibrium is fragile but favorable:** OpenAI and Anthropic are clearly on the frontier, Grok and Meta chase them, and China distills ~6-9 months behind; the open question is how long that lasts, especially as AI improves AI (self-improvement acceleration is "definitely a real thing").
- **"Free" open-source models are a narrative myth:** marginal inference costs are real — GLM and Kimi are expensive to serve — so everyone referring to open models as free is wrong: "you're not paying necessarily the R&D to create the AI, but you're definitely paying the inference to run it."
- **The capital curve is the near-term risk:** tech burned through free cash flow, then the debt markets "incredibly fast," then Google issued equity and Nvidia assembled a ~$500B vehicle tapping pension funds and insurance floats; the railroad analogy (1870s, the world ran out of money before railroads paid off) frames the risk of an air gap where funding runs out before returns arrive — a blowup wouldn't stop AI, but it could be devastating in the interim.
- **Berkshire/BNSF as the Google model:** See's Candies (high margin, small absolute profit) vs. BNSF (low margin, gigantic absolute profit) — Google's search business is See's Candies; the AI opportunity is BNSF; diluting shareholders for a smaller slice of an astronomically larger pie is rational, which is why the Berkshire investment in Google was so symbolic.
- **Verifiable vs. unverifiable domains:** LLMs are incredible in verifiable domains (coding, math) but the evidence that this translates to long-verification-loop domains is still TBD; LLMs distilled the end-state of human thought (typed output), not the traces — hence Neuralink's potential payoff; medicine is "by far one of the biggest opportunities" though regulation-gated.
- **Aggregation theory meets real marginal costs:** distribution was the old constraint (aggregators control demand via discovery); now inference costs are real, and test-time scaling makes every second of "thinking" a direct marginal cost — the free-ChatGPT user and the theorem-prover are "not even remotely in the same universe."
- **Microsoft's usage-based pricing is fraught:** the E7 plan ($100/user/month + usage charges) forces buyers to think about spend every month, untethers cost from headcount, and makes customers question what they're paying for; Microsoft must meter the extreme users without breaking the set-price model for everyone else.
- **Advertising is the consumer business model:** "consumers do not want to pay for software, and consumers do not care about being productive" (the Dropbox rebuild story); OpenAI replayed Dropbox at 100x — subscriptions sold a lot but not enough, and the pivot to advertising came years late; had OpenAI leaned into ads at ChatGPT's launch, Google and Meta would be in much bigger trouble.
- **Compute shortage is structural and self-inflicted:** TSMC cut its growth rate in 2023-2025, and fab lead times exceed data-center lead times, so today's money manifests as compute in 2028-29; tech doesn't understand commodity markets — in commodities the price is set by the marginal supplier, and fixed costs get run regardless (shipping containers: $3-4K to $17-18K, then everyone builds ships).
- **TSMC offloaded risk onto big tech:** TSMC stayed conservative; risk doesn't disappear, it manifests as foregone revenue for every company that could make more money with more compute; memory makers analogized to Iran and the Strait of Hormuz — choke points get routed around; Samsung's counter-cyclical investing wiped out the Japanese memory industry.
- **Scarcity saved Intel:** "the cure for high prices is high prices" — the compute scarcity is so acute that big tech will endure the pain of getting Intel (and Samsung logic) up to speed; expect a major Intel partner announcement soon.
- **Amazon has the best setup:** Amazon is its own first best customer — AWS, Graviton/Trainium iteration (crappy chips hidden under managed services until they're good enough to sell), logistics, and AI call-center software; their core business feels impervious to model-level AI.
- **Apple is "better lucky than good":** the aggregator owns access to customers, may eventually run on-device AI on customers' electricity, and is the king of deterministic products — AI is probabilistic, and "I'm generally prefer companies to do what they're good at."
- **Frontier contenders:** OpenAI/Anthropic are riskiest with the biggest upside — "never discount the power of belief; they think they're creating God"; Google just needs search to not die too fast; Meta is only on the frontier because of Zuckerberg's founder energy; SpaceX AI's case is weakest (highly differentiated data-centers-in-space play — why own the model?); Microsoft's IBM playbook (Gerstner's middleware + consulting gave IBM a 30-year lease on life; Oracle's "don't get locked into IBM" sales pitch is the joke) is rational and also existential — AI agents aimed "like an arrow to the heart" of Microsoft's UI/systems-of-record business.
- **Meta's ad business is under-sold:** Facebook pays zero for content vs. YouTube sharing revenue; TikTok's insight was that entertainment content from the whole network beats social-graph content; AI-generated content would be a worse margin profile for Meta than today's free user content; but LLMs make ad matching predictive (show what the person wants to see next), and the ad marketplace is a verification machine — a few percentage points of relevance = billions; Zuckerberg "doesn't love ads" the same way Altman doesn't, and Apple's ATT was "one of the worst antitrust violations in the history of technology."
- **Nvidia's margins are "unnatural" but the risk is real:** circular financing (25% backstops, Neo clouds, take-or-pay to 2030) is a price cut in disguise — risk never disappears, it just moves; Nvidia's real competitors are the hyperscalers with lower cost of capital selling chips as commodities (Google already selling 20% of TPUs to Anthropic; Jassy signaling external Trainium sales).
- **The durable asset of the bubble is power:** "you want a bubble that produces something that lasts" — the dot-com era left fiber (WorldCom), the railroads still throw off BNSF cash into Google; GPUs don't last, data centers are fine, but power lasts; Nvidia hoped power scarcity would be its moat, but the US brought more power online than expected (behind-the-meter builds, West Texas natural gas, nuclear restarts), which only gives Amazon and Google more time to close the efficiency gap.
