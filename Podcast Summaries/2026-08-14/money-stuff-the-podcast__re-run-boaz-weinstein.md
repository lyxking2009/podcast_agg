# Re-Run: Boaz Weinstein — Money Stuff: The Podcast

## TL;DR
Boaz Weinstein of Saba Capital explains his tender offers for shares of private BDCs (Blue Owl, Starwood) at 65 cents on the dollar, a 35% discount to NAV. He argues that retail investors were promised more liquidity than these funds can actually provide when redemptions spike, and that NAVs are likely overstated due to generous marks. Weinstein is short public high-yield credit as a hedge and says Saba may lower its bid if redemptions worsen, but views the trade as an early opportunity in a market that could face systemic stress. Also touches on DATs and why he avoids them due to dilution risk.

## Key points
- Saba Capital Management has launched tender offers for shares of Blue Owl private BDC and Starwood SREIT at discounts to NAV: 65 cents on the dollar for Blue Owl (a 35% discount) and roughly 27 for Starwood, aiming to provide liquidity to retail investors stuck in redemption queues.
- Weinstein says Saba had these tenders ready before the Blue Owl story escalated, having learned from RIAs about a $20-30 million block of Starwood SREIT shares whose owners wanted out after waiting years for redemptions.
- Interval funds and private BDCs promise quarterly redemptions of up to 5% of shares, but when requests exceed that cap, investors receive only a pro-rata portion; Starwood SREIT has been gated for four years and still pays out only a fraction each month, illustrating the liquidity mismatch.
- Weinstein argues that retail investors did not understand that the underlying loan portfolios are illiquid, and that sales commissions incentivized advisors to push these products; he calls the sales practices "drek" and suggests disclosure of advisor compensation would be scandalous.
- He contends that NAVs across private credit are overstated: managers use stale or optimistic marks, and JPMorgan's recent markdowns reveal that some second-lien positions are off by up to 25 points relative to other funds, constraining leverage and creating systemic risk.
- Saba is short public high-yield credit as a hedge because private credit distress could infect public markets; Weinstein notes that since the Cliffwater redemption news, high yield has been suffering, and he expects redemptions to continue rising.
- Weinstein criticizes managers of publicly traded BDCs like FSK for not buying back shares at deep discounts (FSK trades at a 50% discount to NAV) while continuing to make new loans; he attributes this to fee structures based on NAV, which incentivize keeping AUM high.
- He praises Blackstone for paying out BREIT redemptions at NAV and stabilizing the fund, contrasting with HPS which paid only the contractual 5% despite having inflows that could have covered more, calling such behavior damaging to brand value.
- Weinstein explains that Saba partnered with John Cox, an expert in private BDC secondaries, after connecting on Twitter; Cox had been doing small tender transactions and understood the flow dynamics better.
- He says if the tender gets filled or oversubscribed, Saba would likely lower its next bid; if no shares are tendered, they may wait or raise the bid, but the outcome will provide valuable information about investor desperation and NAV accuracy.
- On DATs (digital asset treasury companies), Weinstein avoids them because unlike closed-end funds under the 40 Act, DATs could issue shares below NAV to select investors or adopt poison pills, creating dilution risk that activist investors cannot easily prevent.
