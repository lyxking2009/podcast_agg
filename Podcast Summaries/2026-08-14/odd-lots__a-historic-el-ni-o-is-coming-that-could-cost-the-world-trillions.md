# A Historic El Niño Is Coming That Could Cost the World Trillions — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal talk to Justin Mankin, professor of geography at Dartmouth and director of the Climate Modeling and Impacts Group, about the unfolding El Niño — forecast to be potentially the strongest on record — its mechanics, why it shows up in sell-side notes from Citi, Morgan Stanley and BofA, and the trillions in economic damage it can do via droughts, floods, and cascading political effects.

## Key points
- Mechanics: El Niño is the sloshing of the tropical Pacific's warm pool eastward when easterly trade winds break down (Bjerknes feedback) — "the tropics are the heat engine of global weather." Neutral/La Niña is a space heater in one corner; El Niño is wall-to-wall radiators.
- The forecast is extreme: every model converges on a record El Niño, warmer than 2016 (the current record by some indices), possibly since 1877-78 (the Great Famines). The RONI index projection of 3.3 has never been reached before. NOAA has had an El Niño advisory since June.
- It is already manifesting: delayed South Asian monsoon onset, which links to farmer welfare, diesel prices, and the political fates of leaders; 2026 has a non-trivial chance of being the warmest year on record, and 2027 is nearly locked in for a record.
- Timing matters: El Niño peaks in the boreal winter (Dec-Feb), intersecting harvest calendars, hurricane seasons, and the Australian summer.
- Economic cost is measurable with causal inference: El Niño is an effectively exogenous "treatment" on economies; Mankin's group (with collaborator Chris Callahan at Indiana University) isolates El Niño costs from global-warming costs — the total runs into the trillions across heat waves, droughts, floods, landslides, and wildfires.
- Cultural forecasting: Peruvian potato farmers forecasted El Niño 500 years ago by watching the visibility of the Pleiades from the Andes — cloud redistribution tells them to shift planting dates (documented in a Nature paper by Cane, Chiang, and the anthropologist Orlove).
