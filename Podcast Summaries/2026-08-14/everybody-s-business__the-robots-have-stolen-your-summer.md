# AI Is Eating the Memory Chips (And the Music Business) — Everybody's Business

## TL;DR
Bloomberg BusinessWeek's panel (hosts Stacey-Marie Ishmael and Max, with tech editor Josh Brustein and media reporter Ashley Carman) explains two ways AI is reshaping the real economy: a memory-chip shortage that is raising prices on everything from Macs to PlayStations, and generative music (Suno) that is both democratizing songwriting and threatening artists' livelihoods.

## Key points
- AI inference consumes far more memory chips than expected — the "unsexy" commodity chips are now the bottleneck. Memory prices are estimated to triple; PPI for electronics components +28% YoY in June; chip prices broke their decades-long deflationary trend.
- Only three companies make memory: Micron, Samsung, SK Hynix. They were burned by past boom-bust cycles (Micron's Boise layoffs) and were slow to add capacity; supply relief isn't expected until ~2028.
- The shortage is creating regional boomtowns: Boise, Idaho (Micron HQ) now has helicopter "James Bond dinners" ($1,400), sold-out luxury fishing trips, and a wealth manager whose client said "I'm a million dollars richer" after an 8-day Micron run.
- AI music has hit an inflection: Suno (~$5B valuation, Mikey Shulman — "the Instagram to music") and Udio let anyone generate songs; hip-hop artist Phoenix Flexen's AI "Rubbers" is charting on real charts.
- Copyright is the industry's original sin: labels allege Suno trained on copyrighted music without payment; Suno claims fair use; lawsuits are in court. Warner Music CEO Robert Kyncl signed a licensing deal and argues AI music played in restaurants can actually generate royalties for his artists.
- Streaming platforms are responding: Deezer labels AI-generated songs; Spotify is labeling "AI personas" rather than individual songs.
- AI acts are making real money: "Breaking Rust" (an AI country band) has made ~$300K total, ~$18K/month — Slop Tracker tracks this long tail.
