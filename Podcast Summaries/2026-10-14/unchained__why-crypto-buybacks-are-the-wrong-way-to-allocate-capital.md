## Overview

Laura Shin interviews Lorenzo Valente, Director of Research for Digital Assets at ARK Invest, against the backdrop of two shocks: Justin Drake's warning that ECDSA — the cryptography securing Bitcoin and Ethereum — could break before Q-Day, in the worst case "in months, not years," and the Ledger supply-chain attack in which devices sold through a third-party reseller led to roughly $100 million in losses. Valente argues both threats accelerate a trend ARK already saw: self-custody is slowly dying. He explains why ARK favours a barbell of exchanges that own the user and infrastructure assets like Solana, Uniswap and Aave, says Ethereum has to pick a lane, and calls crypto buybacks "crazy."

## Key Points

- Justin Drake warned ECDSA could break before Q-Day — worst case "in months, not years" — which would compromise the cryptography securing Bitcoin and Ethereum.
- The Ledger reseller attack produced roughly $100 million in losses, reinforcing the argument against do-it-yourself key management.
- Valente's thesis: both threats speed up a trend ARK already identified — self-custody is slowly dying.
- ARK favours a barbell strategy: exchanges that own the customer relationship, plus infrastructure protocols such as Solana, Uniswap and Aave.
- On Ethereum: it has to pick a lane rather than pursue every direction simultaneously.
- Crypto buybacks are "the wrong way to allocate capital" — he calls them "crazy."
- The framing of the paradox the episode title implies: quantum fears and hardware-supply-chain risk are arguments against the very self-custody ethos crypto was built on.

## Implications

The investment logic is that if self-custody declines, value concentrates in custodial venues that own users and in the settlement and lending layers they depend on — which is the opposite of the decentralisation thesis most crypto allocation is justified by. For protocol teams, the buyback argument is a direct challenge to using treasury capital to support token price rather than build.
