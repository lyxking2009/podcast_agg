# The Steamy, Billion-Dollar World of Romantasy

**Show:** Everybody's Business  
**Published:** 2026-10-14  
**Transcript source:** `rss_omny_srt`

## Overview

This episode is a cross-post of The Big Take from Bloomberg News, hosted by David Gura, with Bloomberg reporters Hannah Miller and Madison Muller. The subject is romantasy — romance plus fantasy — and how a genre that critics dismiss as unserious has become a near-billion-dollar industry. The hook is scale: Rebecca Yarros's Onyx Storm sold 2.7 million copies in its first week, the fastest-selling adult novel in 20 years. From there the episode traces the commercial machinery — BookTok creators who left corporate jobs to review full time, bespoke romance bookstores, merchandise lines, film adaptations, conventions and masquerade balls — and interrogates the genre's critics on tropes, power dynamics and whether it romanticises villains.

## Key Points

- Romantasy is romance plus fantasy — werewolf shifters, dragons, fairies — and readers pick their own combination of elements.
- Onyx Storm by Rebecca Yarros sold 2.7 million copies in its first week, becoming the fastest-selling adult novel in 20 years.
- The industry around the genre is approaching a billion dollars, spanning merchandise, bespoke bookstores, podcasts and film adaptations.
- BookTok is the primary growth engine: creators with tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of followers now make careers out of reviews and reaction videos, and some have left corporate jobs to do it.
- The fan community has real-world infrastructure — book retreats, community events, trivia nights, conventions and masquerade balls.
- Critics attack the genre for trope problems: "shadow daddies" who hold power over their human lovers and are often centuries older, raising questions about whether the books romanticise villains rather than heroes.
- The counter-argument made in the episode: many of these books feature genuinely strong female protagonists carrying responsibility for saving a kingdom or world, with difficult pasts they have to work through — serious themes underneath the marketing.
- A recurring dismissal problem: readers report the genre is not treated as serious reading.

## Implications

For anyone in media or consumer businesses, the episode is a case study in a fan community doing the distribution that publishers used to do — the demand signal originates with creators, not marketing budgets, which is why the merchandising and convention layer formed so fast. The reputational issue is the commercial constraint: a genre that is not treated as serious reading is easier to underpay and easier to dismiss, which is exactly the pattern publishing has run before.
