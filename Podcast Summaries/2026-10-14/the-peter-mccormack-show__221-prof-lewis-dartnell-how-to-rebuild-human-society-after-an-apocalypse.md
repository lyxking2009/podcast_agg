## Overview

Peter McCormack interviews Lewis Dartnell, astrobiologist, Professor of Science Communication at the University of Westminster, and author of The Knowledge: How to Rebuild Our World from Scratch. Dartnell's day job is researching whether microbial life could survive the radiation on Mars; his books are about how little any of us understand the systems we depend on. The episode runs the post-apocalyptic thought experiment properly: what kills you first, where you go, how long a supermarket actually keeps you alive (55 years), how many people you need before you can rebuild anything (10,000), why we outsourced all the handiwork, gasifier stoves and the cars that ran on wood, making glass from a bag of sand, printing a page from scratch, and the "Kindle apocalypse plan." It closes on the Mars questions — whether we could actually live there and the radiation problem.

## Key Points

- A supermarket keeps you alive for 55 years — the answer the episode flags as the one nobody expects.
- You need roughly 10,000 people before you can rebuild anything meaningful.
- We have outsourced all the handiwork: the knowledge of how things are made has been removed from most people's lives.
- Preservation is the binding constraint — you have to preserve everything, and every technology is also a weapon.
- The gasifier stove and the cars that ran on wood are examples of technologies that can be reconstructed at low industrial complexity.
- Making glass from a bag of sand, making his own photograph, and printing a page from scratch are the concrete reconstruction demonstrations.
- The "Kindle apocalypse plan" — what happens to stored digital knowledge when the infrastructure that reads it disappears.
- Dartnell describes modern civilisation as resilient and fragile at the same time.
- His framing on invention: "we invent because we are lazy."
- Mars: could we actually live there, and the radiation problem for both humans and microbes.

## Implications

The episode's practical content is about knowledge that is stored in systems rather than in people — the Kindle plan and the outsourcing observation are the same warning from different directions. It is a useful counterweight to preparedness framings built on stockpiles: the constraint is not calories but the ability to reproduce the tools you are using, and that requires a population scale most survivalist plans do not have.
