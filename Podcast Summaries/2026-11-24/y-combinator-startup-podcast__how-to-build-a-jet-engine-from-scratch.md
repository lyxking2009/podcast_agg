# How To Build A Jet Engine From Scratch — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
Boom Supersonic, after Rolls-Royce pulled out of its engine partnership in 2022, built its own jet engine core, which fired up on November 21st, and discovered it can be sold as a 42 MW gas turbine for AI data centers, making the company cash-flow positive on first unit. The 200,000 sq ft South Denver superfactory vertically integrates 85% of production, including single-crystal blades, aiming to ship turbines by mid-2027 while funding the Overture supersonic jet.

## Key points
- Boom Supersonic's first full-scale jet engine core, the first ever built by a startup, fired up on November 21st, marking a milestone for its Overture 60-80 seat commercial supersonic jet.
- The company's XB1 became the first independently developed jet to break the sound barrier; Boom's first employee was an engine designer from Pratt & Whitney because no off-the-shelf engine existed for Overture.
- After Rolls-Royce abruptly ended the partnership in September 2022 without warning, Boom nearly collapsed; Blake Scholl described it as the 'startup equivalent of a stage four cancer diagnosis' and launched a crash program announced December 2022 to build its own engine.
- In 2025, while reading about grid interconnect queues (5-10 years) and sold-out power turbines (GE orders 5 years out), Scholl texted Sam Altman, a day-one Boom investor, who confirmed demand for 40 MW class gas turbines, leading Boom to adapt the engine as a 42 MW generator running on natural gas.
- Boom plans to sell turbines to data centers first, generating a multi-billion dollar business at just 2 turbines per month; the first unit shipped flips the company cash flow positive, providing funding for Overture development.
- Ground-based turbine operations provide 24/7 real-world testing: instead of the typical 5,000-hour engine test, running in data centers could accumulate 1 million hours, making it 'the most tested new jet engine ever.'
- The US needs about 100 GW of new generating capacity over the next 10 years for AI data centers, worth about $300 billion in turbines; Boom aims to produce 1 GW per year, with one prospective customer wanting 1.6 GW every 30 days.
- Incumbent turbine makers (e.g., GE) are sold out through 2030 and have little incentive to expand because margins, prices, and stock options are high; Blake links industrial stagnation to the 1973 ban on supersonic flight and flat/negative electricity per capita growth since 2000.
- Turbine blades require single-crystal superalloys; an initial supplier quote was $1.3 million and 18 months for one ship set, but Boom bought a $1.1 million 3D printer in 2 weeks and now prints blades in-house.
- Boom's 200,000 sq ft 'superpower superfactory' in South Denver vertically integrates production; by weight, it expects to make 85% of every turbine in-house, going from raw materials to completed units under one roof.
- Boom is building its own single-crystal foundry next door, aiming for the world's most advanced all-digital single-crystal process: design a blade Monday, cast it in the afternoon, test by Tuesday.
- Boom heavily uses custom software: the BladeRunner tool with real-time aerodynamic simulation, custom CAD kernel, and GPU cluster allows one engineer to do work that previously took a team of 100 six months.
- The entire company is 165 people (40 manufacturing, 125 engineering/other), with an internal cap of 250 outside manufacturing, reflecting Blake's belief in small elite teams; XB1 was built by just 50 people.
- First turbine shipments are targeted for mid-2027; Blake estimates better than 50% odds that the public will fly supersonic on Boom's airplane, up from previously lower confidence.
