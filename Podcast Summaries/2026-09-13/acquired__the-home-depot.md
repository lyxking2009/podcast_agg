## TL;DR

The Fall 2026 season opener is a full Acquired treatment of Home Depot, and Ben Gilbert opens by admitting he previously underrated the company badly. The numbers justify the episode: Home Depot is the world's largest specialty retailer — only general merchants like Walmart, Amazon and Costco are larger — and the 45th most valuable publicly traded company in the world at roughly a $350 billion market cap, worth more than Netflix, Alibaba, Goldman Sachs, LVMH or Disney, despite operating only in North America. ## Key points

- **The scale claim:** Home Depot is the world's largest specialty retailer. The only larger retailers are general rather than vertical — Walmart, Amazon, Costco.
- **$350 billion market cap**, making it the 45th most valuable publicly traded company in the world. It is worth more than Netflix, Alibaba, Goldman Sachs, LVMH or Disney — and unlike those companies, it operates only in North America.
- **The IPO comparison.** Home Depot went public in 1981, one year after Apple Computer. $1,000 invested in both at IPO and held to today would leave the Home Depot position ahead of the Apple position — and with dividends reinvested it has compounded at nearly 25% per year for 45 years.
- **The headline return statistic:** it is the number one performing stock in the S&P 500 in total investment return from the day it went public to today. That $1,000 is worth roughly $17 million, versus about $170,000 for the same $1,000 in the S&P 500.
- **Employment:** roughly 470,000 people — more than any US big tech company except Amazon, more than any bank in the world, more than almost every car company or hotel chain, and more than any restaurant chain including Starbucks.
- **The founding premise** was unlikely: two men unceremoniously fired from their old jobs, with little savings and no retirement funds, who bet on a revolutionary idea — home improvement stores in giant warehouses, five times more square footage than any other hardware store at the time, which seemed risky at best and unthinkable at worst.
- **The cast is framed as four retail superheroes** who come together. The two introduced in this stretch are founding CEO Bernie Marcus and CFO Arthur Blank.
- **Bernie Marcus's background:** a poor family of Jewish immigrants in the rough neighbourhoods of Newark, New Jersey in the 1930s during the Depression. He was in a gang as a kid — a detail Gilbert and Rosenthal take from Marcus's memoir *Kick Up Some Dust*, whose foreword was written by his friend Pitbull.
- **The career path that made him:** super smart, first in his family to go to college (Rutgers, down the road from Newark). He wanted medical school and psychiatry, but the family had no money for it, so he became a pharmacist, which led to being a concessionaire — a store within a store — in a New York-area discount store, which began his retail education. From there he joined the discount chain Two Guys, and then landed an executive role at the Los Angeles-based retail conglomerate Daylin Corporation.
- **The 1960s retail landscape:** shopping malls being built across the postwar suburbs, Sears building big anchor stores attached to them, Kmart rising as a discounter, and Sam Walton's Walmart emerging in the rural South. Almost all American retail outside Sears, Kmart and department stores was regional chains — there were no national giants as there are today.
- **Daylin as a portfolio of retail concepts** — pharmacies, hardware stores, clothing, home furnishing, discounters — operating almost like an index fund on post-general-store suburban formats.
- **1972: Marcus becomes CEO of Handy Dan**, Daylin's Los Angeles-based hardware chain. He has no equity in the company — he is an employee, though a well-paid executive. Crucially, he approaches the business from the retail side rather than the hardware-expertise side.
- **Marcus recruits Arthur Blank**, a younger Daylin company man and financial whiz, to Los Angeles as Handy Dan's CFO. Marcus knew his own weak spot was finance.
- **Why a subsidiary had its own public company:** Handy Dan was a standalone publicly traded company. Daylin had bought all of it, then followed a 1970s Wall Street fad of spinning off 19% equity stubs of divisions and floating them publicly — 19% being below 20% so the parent could still consolidate the division's financials. The theory was to unlock value from the conglomerate discount; in practice the stubs traded at a further discount to the parent's already-discounted valuation.
- **The 1970s backdrop is brutal.** Oil crises, stagflation, and federal interest rates going up into the teens — briefly 18-19%. Suburban retail spending dried up and Daylin started failing across the board, except for Handy Dan, which Marcus and Blank turned into the crown jewel of an otherwise withering empire.
- **Home improvement wasn't a category yet.** The hardware store landscape at the time was a patchwork of clubby, regional, unsophisticated and not especially good businesses — which is precisely the opening the founders identified.
- **Equity ownership is a stated thread.** Rosenthal flags early that who holds Home Depot's stock is a critical part of the company's strategy — at least historically — and that the episode will return to it.
