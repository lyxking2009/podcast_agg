# 8 Ways To Improve Your Outbound Sales — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

*(Guest: Christina Gilbert, founder of OneSchema and YC Visiting Partner. YouTube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wr6PMD06hP0)*

## TL;DR

Christina Gilbert, who personally generated and closed millions in revenue through founder-led sales at OneSchema and now coaches hundreds of YC companies as a Visiting Partner, lays out eight tactics for better outbound. Her core claim: a failed outbound campaign is almost never a copywriting problem — it is a targeting, sequencing and follow-through problem, and the diagnostic order matters more than the message. Do the first 100 outreaches genuinely by hand before automating anything.

## Key points

- **Start manual before you automate.** The biggest mistake Gilbert sees in office hours is a founder who loaded up a tool, blasted a thousand emails, and got zero replies — learning nothing about *why*. Zero replies from an unconsidered campaign could be messaging, targeting, prospect selection, deliverability or subject line. Do at least 100 outreaches truly by hand first: think about one specific person, her specific problem, visit her LinkedIn page and read about her company. That homework is how the highest-converting ideas emerge.
- **Targeting beats perfect messaging.** Founders spend hours tweaking copy and barely think about who they are emailing. Outbound is a needle-in-a-haystack problem — without a person who has the exact pain point you solve, a perfectly crafted message won't land.
- **Pick the person with the most to gain.** Look at who has already replied, and ask who in the organisation benefits most from buying. Example: a chief security officer would be fired over a data breach and so cares about device-monitoring software; an engineering manager would rather not be monitored at all and is a poor target.
- **Verify companies individually, don't trust list-building output.** A LinkedIn Sales Navigator search for 'tech companies' often returns IT implementation consultancies. Every company needs to be checked against the customer profile or the message falls on deaf ears.
- **Learn intent signals.** Job postings reveal a company's priorities, and growth stages reveal need. A compensation-planning tool is irrelevant below ~100 employees (no levels yet) and redundant above ~500 (bands already set) — so crossing the 100-employee mark is the signal. Every product has signals like this.
- **Write to the executive's worries, not your feature list.** Executives don't care that uploads are blazing fast or that your database is built in Rust; they worry about hitting the quarterly target. The product explanation should be one sentence — detail belongs on the first call. Establish credibility either with impressive logo names or, failing that, your own relevant background.
- **End with a specific call to action.** 'Got 20 minutes to hop on a call this week?' beats 'Does this sound interesting to you?' Add a calendar link or a couple of time slots. Keep it short: roughly 5-8 sentences for email, 2-5 for LinkedIn — every filler word dilutes the words that matter. The strong example cited is from the founder of Apten (YC S24): informal, peer-to-peer tone, no jargon, credibility from having solved the same problem at a previous company.
- **Optimise subject lines as their own asset.** Only about the first 30 characters show in the Gmail preview. Creative and slightly weird beats descriptive: a company selling an OpenClaw-style agent product found 'broken agents' substantially outperformed 'better agents for every workflow'.
- **Treat your LinkedIn profile as outbound surface area.** Have a profile picture (trust), turn the banner into a clear jargon-free ad for the company, rewrite the one-line description and About section concisely, and cut irrelevant personal material so buyers can see how you help. Mutual connections are the single biggest predictor of connection-request acceptance, so make maxing out the connection allowance a daily habit.
- **Use reply rates as a diagnostic, in order.** Two to three replies per 100 emails is a fine starting baseline. Debug in this sequence: (1) right person, (2) right companies, (3) subject lines, (4) messaging, (5) materials (website and LinkedIn), (6) deliverability. If all six look reasonable and you still get zero replies, that points at a deeper product-market-fit problem.
- **Follow up two to four times, then send a breakup message.** One email is not a campaign — the person may simply have missed it. Space follow-ups a couple of days apart; the final one is a low-pressure breakup note ('Since I haven't heard back, it seems like sales-tax automation isn't a priority right now — just let me know and I can spare your inbox'), which often gets a reply because saying no is easy. A real no is useful: in aggregate the nos sharpen future targeting.
- **When they reply, be a heat-seeking missile.** Nothing should get between you and their calendar — 'time kills deals' is a lesson every founder learns at least once.
- **Block out time for outbound.** Outbound is one of the least fun parts of a founder's week, so it loses to everything else. Decide how many messages per day, how long each takes, and exactly when you'll send them — then put it on the calendar, or have a co-founder hold you accountable. Following through on committed activity is the number one difference between founders who succeed and those who don't.
- **Get your copy from customers, not friends.** The best messaging feedback comes from customers, never a friend, adviser or co-founder. Ask on the first call 'What got your attention about my message?' — they name the lines that worked, and the question also gets them talking openly about their problem. Also ask existing customers for the single thing that made them buy and reuse their phrasing. Customers write better copy than you do.
- **The founder's compounding advantage.** The slog of thousands of outbound emails pays off because customer knowledge compounds: you start spotting a job posting and instantly knowing the company has pain, and you learn which pain point to lead with for smaller companies. No salesperson or agency can learn your customers the way you can.
