# TIP846: Stock Picker: How to Live Off Your Portfolio w/ Ian Cassel — The Investor's Podcast (We Study Billionaires)

## TL;DR

Stig Brodersen interviews Ian Cassel, founder of MicroCapClub and CIO of Intelligent Fanatics Capital Management, about his memoir-cum-investing book *Stock Picker*. Cassel never held a normal job: his parents handed him $20,000 at 16, he peaked at $120,000 by 2000, lost 90% in the dot-com crash, and was living off his own microcap portfolio by 28. The conversation covers why his $2 million threshold was about pain tolerance rather than expected returns, why most microcaps must be sold within 36 months even when they win, why he never holds a large cash position, and how he explains 'there is no such thing as savings' when your portfolio is your paycheck.

## Key points

- **A memorably personal book.** Cassel says the hardest chapters were the personal ones — a mentor, losing his mother, and the closing chapter on compounding and his kids. The opening essay, 'What Is Chasing You', grew out of a 2009 cocktail-party argument in New York: a fund manager sneered that Cassel 'wasn't good enough to keep a job', and Cassel fired back that a fund manager is 'someone who isn't skilled enough to support themselves on their own capital'. That anger fuelled him for three or four years.
- **Forgiveness is not the opposite of edge.** Cassel argues the two aren't mutually exclusive: resolving conflict quickly frees up stored negative energy that can be redeployed on competitiveness, 'almost like selling a loser out of your portfolio and then focusing the effort and capital on the winners'. He quotes Lincoln: 'Do I not destroy my enemies when I make them my friends?'
- **The first big win shaped his risk tolerance permanently.** At 16 his parents offered him the $20,000 they had saved for his education; $5,000 went into a tech stock that doubled in two months, and the account reached ~$120,000 by the 2000 peak before losing 90% in the dot-com crash. Cassel is blunt that 'a monkey could have picked winning stocks in that environment' — it was luck, not skill — yet making money early left him permanently on a higher rung of the risk scale and gave him the self-belief to rebuild.
- **Mentors are there for 'a reason, a season, or a lifetime'.** His first and most influential mentor, Skip, was met on the Raging Bull message board in 2002 when Cassel was 21; Skip trained stockbrokers and taught him the qualitative art of talking to management and pitching a stock in under a minute. Two rules came out of it: only take advice from people who are genuinely happy, and only from people who have already done what you are trying to do.
- **Mentorship should be reciprocal, not extractive.** Cassel got Skip's attention by adding value first, and says accomplished people end up pulled into younger people's orbits the same way. Approaching a mentor as 'a piece of software you can download' is unattractive; adding value repeatedly until reciprocity happens naturally is what works.
- **The $2 million number was about pain tolerance.** Full-time private investing means above-market returns *minus* your expenses while still growing capital faster than the S&P, with no 401(k) match, no employer healthcare and no safety net — 'what you eat is what you kill'. Cassel chose $2 million because that was the amount where a 50% drawdown would not change his strategy or the kind of investor he is, not because of a return projection.
- **'There is no such thing as savings' when you live off a portfolio.** It becomes degrees of spending, flexed up or down. Safeguards he built: keeping fixed costs low (he lived off ~$2,000/month early on), always holding two years of cash in his personal bank account so he never had to sell stocks at a bad time, and splitting household bank accounts so one covered the fixed costs and the other the variable ones.
- **Returns are 'consistently inconsistent'.** Cassel describes years like: lose a couple hundred thousand, break even, then make a million. The trap for new full-time investors is extrapolating a bull-market year and shaving 4-5% off for living expenses — then being down 25% in year one and having to sell 5% anyway. He counts making the decision right after the GFC as beneficial precisely because he had already proved he could survive a 50% drawdown.
- **Most microcaps must be sold within 36 months — even the winners.** Microcaps are small businesses stacked with concentration risk: key-person risk (the CEO wearing every hat), customer concentration (one big customer that kick-started the business), and geographic or end-market risk. They are fragile rather than anti-fragile, so winning seasons typically run 6-24 months, sometimes up to 36. The key question on any microcap that just posted a good quarter is 'how long will this last?' — and the honest answer is usually shorter than you think. Coffee-can logic borrowed from mega-cap buy-and-hold does not transfer.
- **The greats' best years were their highest-turnover years.** Cassel cites Buffett and Greenblatt as evidence that peak returns coincided with peak turnover — Buffett has held only about 10 of the hundreds of stocks he has owned for 10+ years. He is sceptical of managers who wear a low turnover rate as a badge of honour, and notes the incentive problem: 'buy and hold' advice conveniently lets a fund manager point back at it in a down year.
- **No big cash positions.** Holding 20%+ cash is either a macro bet or an admission you cannot find ideas — 'the reality is you just aren't looking hard enough'. Cassel runs a 3-5% cash buffer instead, sized so he can buy half of a new position; he then has to fund the other half by selling his least-conviction holding, which forces portfolio discipline mechanically.
- **More positions, smaller weights.** As capital grew, Cassel moved from 10-15% at-cost positions (and 25% when he was private) to 3-5%, framing it as 'more chances to win'. If a stock is a genuine multi-bagger, whether it starts at 5% or 10% barely matters — it will become the largest position anyway. The fund has grown from ~10 to 15-16 names.
- **Capacity discipline over asset gathering.** In capacity-constrained microcaps (his bread and butter is sub-$100M market cap), successful managers must either diversify or lengthen holding periods as capital grows. Cassel sees $40M growing to $100M, but wouldn't force it by taking a $50M institutional cheque — he is the largest investor in his own fund and wants it to stay that way, preferring 'a table with 40 legs on it' where pulling one out leaves the table standing.
- **A mental hack for cutting losers.** Cassel sometimes asks himself how he would position the portfolio if he were up 30% year-to-date instead of down 10% — the answer is usually that he would cut the weak position immediately. Stock pickers are at their best when winning, because a rising P&L makes it easier to shave off mistakes; down years tempt you to hold and rationalise.
