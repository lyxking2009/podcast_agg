# “The Social Reckoning” Shows How It All Went Wrong — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
Critics at Large compares David Fincher's 2010 The Social Network with Aaron Sorkin's follow-up The Social Reckoning, tracing how cultural portrayals of tech founders shifted from dark anti-heroes to outright villains. The hosts praise the earlier film as a stylish, myth-making origin story for Facebook, while finding the new film a flawed but didactic whistleblower narrative centered on Frances Haugen and Facebook's deliberate engagement optimization that fueled radicalization. The episode concludes that whistleblowers and outside critics are becoming the new cultural heroes amid growing distrust of Big Tech.

## Key points
- The Social Network (2010) is praised by all three hosts as a great movie with snappy Sorkin dialogue, Fincher's influential gray visual palette, and Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross's Oscar-winning score; they see it both as a character study of Zuckerberg and a parable about America's meritocratic leadership class.
- The film opens with Zuckerberg's breakup with Erica (Rooney Mara), establishing his arrogance, insecurity, and revenge motive, and it structures the story around civil lawsuits from the Winklevoss twins and Eduardo Saverin (Andrew Garfield), emphasizing themes of betrayal and ruptured social bonds.
- Despite being an anti-hero portrait rather than a straightforward villain, The Social Network already shows Zuckerberg's amorality: he creates Facemash (a site for rating women's appearances) and relentlessly exploits users' desire for social connection, presaging later platform harms.
- The Social Reckoning, written and directed by Aaron Sorkin without David Fincher due to Fincher's Netflix contract, is set in two timelines: Zuckerberg's preparation for congressional testimony and journalist Jeff Horwitz's investigation with whistleblower Frances Haugen.
- The hosts largely agree The Social Reckoning is not a very good movie; they cite a loss of Fincher's cinematic texture, an overly didactic and stage-like structure reminiscent of Sorkin's theater roots, and weak performances from Jeremy Allen White and Mikey Madison, though Bill Burr and Jeremy Strong receive some praise.
- Naomi Fry specifically criticizes Mikey Madison's portrayal of Frances Haugen as stuttering and unconvincing compared to the real Haugen, whom she describes as self-assured, smart, and truth-to-power in her public appearances before Congress and on 60 Minutes.
- The film's central revelation is Facebook's shift to the 'meaningful social interactions' (MSI) metric, which prioritized reshares over likes, accelerating the spread of divisive, radicalizing content and overriding user-safety concerns to maintain engagement.
- The movie's emotional climax is the January 6 Capitol riot situation room, where Zuckerberg must decide whether to shut down Donald Trump's live Facebook account; the film implies he does not, underscoring the concentration of power in one founder's choices.
- Alex Schwartz notes that Frances Haugen herself cringed at the film's depiction of Zuckerberg's office, explaining in an interview that the real Zuckerberg sits in a bulletproof, quarter-mile-long building due to security threats and buys his neighbors' houses, making him 'a prisoner of his own decisions.'
- The hosts discuss a broader cultural shift: tech founders have moved from figures of hope (e.g., Zuckerberg as Time Person of the Year in 2010) to villains, as seen in recent works like Alex Gibney's Musk documentary, Luca Guadagnino's Artificial about Sam Altman, and Nathan Fielder's Elizabeth Holmes documentary.
- Alyssa Wilkinson's New York Times piece is cited: as Big Tech takes over Hollywood, screen portrayals of founders have become darker, depicting them as 'narcissistic, amoral menace[s] unbothered by things like ethics and laws as long as the stock price goes up.'
- The episode argues that the whistleblower has emerged as a new cultural hero, citing Frances Haugen, Edward Snowden, Daniel Ellsberg, Sarah Wynn-Williams's book Careless People, and Claire Stapleton's memoir Don't Be Evil about Google.
- Vinson Cunningham connects The Social Reckoning to the 1930s Federal Theatre Project's 'living newspapers,' suggesting that even belated, didactic information-bringing can raise political consciousness about systems that shape daily life.
- The hosts also reference Jill Lepore's essay on Edward Snowden, noting that the proliferation of whistleblowers indicates institutional self-regulation has failed, leaving outsiders to expose systemic problems.
- A 60 Minutes segment about local backlash to data centers, featuring Texas rancher Blake O'Quinn, signals a possible new hero: ordinary outsiders rejecting Big Tech's impact on their communities, framed not as partisan but as a question of what kind of country and world people want.
