# #219 - Paul Morland - Why The Next Global Financial Crisis Is Mathematically Inevitable — The Peter McCormack Show

## TL;DR
Paul Morland argues that declining fertility rates and aging populations are mathematically making a global financial crisis inevitable, as welfare states are Ponzi schemes requiring each generation to be larger than the last. He contends that immigration is not a solution and that only a combination of financial incentives and cultural shifts can reverse the trend. The episode covers the economic and social consequences of low birth rates, the limits of AI as a remedy, and the need for national purpose and pronatalist policies.

## Key points
- Fertility rates have fallen below replacement in most developed countries and increasingly in developing ones, with examples like South Korea (lowest in the world for a time), Britain around 1.4, Jamaica lower than Britain, and India now below replacement.
- Morland describes the 'four grays' of demographic decline: gray consumer (shrinking and risk-averse demand), gray government (fiscal strain from aged-related spending), gray labor force (fewer workers), and gray capital (older investors prefer safe assets, starving innovation of risk capital).
- The welfare state is fundamentally a Ponzi scheme because it relies on each generation being larger than the previous one to fund pensions and eldercare; with declining cohorts, the system cannot be sustained without severe cutbacks or default.
- Japan and Italy, the two oldest societies, have the largest debt-to-GDP ratios, and Morland predicts the next financial crisis will originate in government bond markets, possibly triggered by a country like the UK or France, due to demographic-driven borrowing.
- Immigration is not a viable solution because source countries' fertility rates are also falling, migrants' birth rates drop within a generation, and rich countries effectively engage in 'biological imperialism' by recruiting talent from poorer nations that need it themselves.
- Historical patterns show fertility decline began with upper and educated classes and spread downward, leading to eugenicist panics in the past; Singapore's 'love cruises' and government encouragement for educated women to marry are a modern example of such concerns.
- Countries with consistent pronatalist policies or strong cultural/national identity, such as France, Israel, and certain US subcommunities (Amish, Mormons, Orthodox Jews), maintain higher fertility rates, though not always at replacement level.
- Successful policy examples include France's long-standing pronatalism, Australia's baby bonus (which lifted birth rates until it was reduced), and tax changes under Blair-Brown in Britain that briefly increased fertility around 2008-2010.
- Morland advocates for both financial measures (tax breaks for parents, higher taxes for the childless, housing reform, workplace nurseries, student debt write-off for first child) and cultural shifts (school curricula promoting parenthood, national purpose, and reducing stigma around having children).
- The relationship crisis between men and women, exacerbated by political polarization and the commercialization of sex (including sex robots), contributes to falling fertility, but Morland notes that most women still want two or three children.
- While AI and robotics may alleviate some labor shortages, Morland is skeptical they will eliminate demand for labor; he warns against a future where humans stop reproducing and are cared for by robots, calling it a 'depressing future' and emphasizing the personal joy of parenthood.
