# Narrative intelligence and the human advantage — Practical AI

## TL;DR
Reed Frerichs, a leadership coach and former actor/producer, explains his concept of 'narrative intelligence'—a three-step process of owning, authoring, and acting on one's personal story to adapt in the age of AI. He argues that AI creates both fear and opportunity, emphasizing emotional intelligence, aligning personal and company values, and embracing impostor syndrome. Frerichs offers practical advice for leaders and individuals to use AI creatively and stay adaptable.

## Key points
- Reed Frerichs, who has a background in acting, media, and startups, developed the concept of 'narrative intelligence' as a framework for evolving one's personal and professional story in real time. He breaks it into three steps: 'own' (acknowledge where you are), 'author' (reframe and write the next chapter), and 'act' (take a concrete first step).
- Frerichs distinguishes narrative intelligence from traditional storytelling, noting that people often get stuck in static stories, whereas narrative intelligence involves continuous adaptation to new information, audiences, and AI tools. He first presented the concept at South by Southwest last year in a sold-out 50-person 'core conversation,' which included a former NASA leader transitioning careers.
- Frerichs cites a statistic that people have about 60,000 thoughts per day, with roughly 80% negative, and argues that because AI is powerful and game-changing, individuals should double down on their unique story and personal growth to remain adaptable.
- He emphasizes the importance of aligning personal values with company values and focusing on the 'why' to maintain motivation and focus. He recommends setting realistic, small habits, referencing James Clear's book Atomic Habits, to compound personal growth over time.
- Frerichs says impostor syndrome is nearly universal among founders and high-achievers, and advises embracing and normalizing it rather than hiding it. He describes coaching a female founder from Daniel's cohort who initially felt she couldn't be CEO but after reframing her story gained confidence to take the role.
- Daniel Whitenack shares his own struggle with humility in AI adoption: his rigid code standards and assumptions about software development were blocking his team, and he realized that not owning his current knowledge gaps made him a liability to the company's mission. Frerichs encourages leaders to admit what they don't know to foster collective learning.
- Frerichs is coaching an AI company team in what they call 'the ownership project,' where leaders who were previously in survival mode must now grow and help team members take initiative. He notes that siloing is no longer effective given the rapid pace of AI change.
- To manage fear and acceleration, Frerichs recommends developing emotional intelligence skills such as self-awareness, presence, self-regulation, empathy, and learning to respond rather than react. He distinguishes between essence (core self) and identity (changeable), encouraging people to adopt new identities like 'leader' or 'athlete' to adapt.
- Frerichs references Rick Rubin's book The Creative Act to argue that AI makes it the best time ever to be a creator or innovator, removing barriers like needing specific jobs, schools, or skills. He highlights that people can now be software engineers and also musicians, storytellers, or authors.
- For those feeling left behind by AI, Frerichs suggests making AI practical by tying it to existing interests, such as using it to plan a reunion trip (as he did with his AI-skeptical parents) or find recipes and restaurants, sparking curiosity and reducing fear.
- Frerichs envisions a future where storytelling and AI enable rich connections and a sense of belonging, reimagining his brand The Lesson as a platform akin to Netflix for people's stories and lives.
