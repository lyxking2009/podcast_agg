# What Everyone Gets Wrong About the Economic Problems in Europe — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
In this episode, Joe and Tracy speak with economist Dominik Leusder about common misconceptions regarding Europe's economic problems. Leusder argues that claims of Europe's dramatic productivity decline relative to the US are based on flawed data, particularly PPP adjustments and differences in price deflators. He contends that when properly adjusted, Europe's welfare and productivity are comparable, and that Europe's shorter work hours reflect deliberate choices. The conversation also covers Germany's industrial decline amid China's rise, AI's role, and the slow, fragmented European policy response.

## Key points
- The core claim of Europe's relative economic decline, popularized by Draghi's competitiveness report and Jamie Dimon, rests on flawed data. The commonly used PPP-adjusted GDP per hour ratio, fixed at 2010 prices, overstates US productivity advantage because US statistical agencies apply more aggressive quality adjustments, especially in tech, leading to lower measured inflation and higher real growth. This methodological difference, compounding annually, creates a misleading wedge between EU and US productivity trends.
- When comparing living standards using current-price purchasing power parities and adjusting for working hours, the US advantage largely disappears. Excluding the top 10% of income distribution, the bottom half of US earners are significantly worse off than those in almost any European country except Portugal. Europe's shorter working hours are a deliberate political choice to trade productivity gains for leisure, not a sign of labor market dysfunction.
- The US healthcare system's excessive costs distort cross-country consumption comparisons. The US spends roughly 50% more per person on healthcare than would be expected at its income level, totaling $1.7–1.9 trillion in annual excess spending, driven by high prices, administrative overhead, and rent-seeking rather than better outcomes or utilization.
- Germany's industrial decline is closely tied to China's shift up the value chain and its export-led growth strategy. Since 2021, Germany has lost over a million manufacturing jobs, with chemical exports and employment hit particularly hard. Labor hoarding due to skilled worker shortages masks the full extent of the shock, as employment has fallen less than output in some sectors.
- Europe's response to China's trade practices has been slow and fragmented due to the EU's 27-member consensus and veto structure, which leads to suboptimal policy outcomes. The proposed Industrial Accelerator Act, while containing 'buy European' provisions effectively targeting China, may be too little, too late. Meanwhile, China's retaliatory capacity is institutionalized and rapid, as seen when it immediately sanctioned European firms after EU actions.
- On AI, Europe is unlikely to develop frontier models but can benefit from near-frontier models like Mistral, which are adequate for most business needs. However, dependencies persist, as Mistral itself uses a Chinese model chassis due to Europe's lack of compute. The real competitive threat is not AI but China's expansion in legacy manufacturing sectors that are still a large part of European value-added.
- The political discourse around Europe's decline has shifted significantly; once-dismissed views like Peter Navarro's 'Death by China' are now mainstream. Europe's initial reluctance to confront China was partly due to political associations with Trumpism, and it took persistent work by analysts like Brad Setser and Sander Todorov to shift the consensus.
- Despite the alarmism, the relevant economic problems for Europe are domestic: internal barriers, low investment, and regional disparities like the stagnation of southern Italy. The fixation on relative decline versus the US may distract from these more tractable issues. Draghi's report correctly identifies barriers and underinvestment but its core productivity comparison is flawed.
