# What Is Intelligence? With David Krakauer

## Overview

A StarTalk Special Edition with Neil deGrasse Tyson, Chuck Nice and Gary O'Reilly in conversation with David Krakauer, evolutionary biologist and president of the Santa Fe Institute. Note that this episode is a re-release of the August 2025 conversation published as 'Emergence Explained with David Krakauer' (S16E48); the full transcript was retrieved from the show's own archive page for that earlier release. The conversation runs from the Santa Fe Institute's mission - 'searching for order in the complexity of evolving worlds' - through Warren Weaver's 1948 taxonomy of simple, disorganized and organized complexity, into a working definition of emergence, why complexity is observer-dependent, how cities follow near-physical scaling laws, and whether AI is intelligent at all. Krakauer's answer on AI is pointed: it has capability but not intelligence.

## Key Points

- The Santa Fe Institute (founded 1984, in the mountains of New Mexico, near Los Alamos) exists to find order in the complexity of evolving worlds - the mission statement is deliberately one sentence and deliberately contested.
- Warren Weaver's 1948 taxonomy is the spine of the episode: simple phenomena (classical physics), disorganized complexity (gases, thermodynamics - beautifully describable), and organized complexity in the middle (organisms, brains, cities), which remains under-theorised.
- Complexity is observer-dependent: how much order you see is a function of the observer's computational power - an analogy to, but distinct from, quantum measurement.
- Krakauer's definition of emergence has three parts: new states or phases of organisation; a new language of description and prediction; and - the hard part - not everything deserves to be called emergent.
- The test of a genuinely emergent level is that it screens off microscopic degrees of freedom - mathematics is his best example, since knowing what the neurons were doing adds nothing to the correctness of a proof.
- On cities: impose strong constraints on a society and it starts to look like a physical system - urban GDP scales with population to roughly the 1.15 power, a near-universal law. Remove the constraints and the theories fail.
- On AI, Krakauer is blunt: 'AIs have no intelligence, but they have tons of capability' - much of current AI is best understood as a very fast, very clever lookup, i.e. capability rather than intelligence.
- His definition of intelligence is comparative, not absolute: intelligence is someone or something that makes a hard problem easy, and the corollary is that stupidity is making easy problems hard.
- A recurring concern is the offloading of cognitive tools: what is lost when problem-solving is outsourced to LLMs run by large companies, and how many generations before that loss becomes irreversible - with GPS dependence offered as the existing example.
- He resists the AI-doom and AI-hype framing alike, arguing for the more interesting question of which level of description actually predicts the phenomenon.

## Implications

The useful discipline here is Krakauer's three-part test for emergence, which is a filter against a lot of loose systems language: a level of description only earns the name if it brings a new predictive language and screens off the level below. Applied to AI, his distinction between capability and intelligence is the more practical claim - the current wave is very good at retrieving and recombining compressed human output, which is not the same thing as making hard problems easy. And the argument about offloading cognitive tools raises a genuinely hard measurement question: the cost of delegating problem-solving may be invisible for a generation, and therefore systematically underpriced.
