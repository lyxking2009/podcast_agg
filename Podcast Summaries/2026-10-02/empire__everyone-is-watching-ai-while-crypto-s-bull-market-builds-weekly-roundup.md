# Everyone Is Watching AI While Crypto’s Bull Market Builds | Weekly Roundup — Empire

## TL;DR
The hosts discuss AI's deeply negative unit economics—Anthropic reportedly loses about $159 for every $1 of revenue on compute costs alone, with a quarter of revenue from Meta and Alibaba—while VC froth continues. They argue crypto is in an early bull market with many investors still sidelined, and highlight tokenization plays like Backpack and Securitize, the hypothetical $200 billion FTX portfolio, and fintech/stablecoin growth (Blackstone token spend up 20x YoY).

## Key points
- Anthropic's leaked S-1 reportedly shows deeply negative unit economics: for every $160 of compute spend, the company generates about $1 of revenue, and about a quarter of revenue comes from just two customers, Meta and Alibaba. OpenRouter data also shows no Anthropic model in the top 10 since early August, suggesting possible plateauing ARR.
- Current AI compute capacity, not lack of demand, is the main constraint on revenue, and hyperscalers are turning to debt markets after exhausting cash flow. A private credit executive said data center buildouts' payback periods and cash-flow profiles make no sense from a risk-return perspective, requiring a venture outlook, while VCs 'know nothing about credit or downside risk protection'.
- One gigawatt of current-generation AI compute costs $50–60 billion to build (about half for Nvidia chips) and can earn $10–20 billion per year at rental rates of $250–350 per hour and 80–90% utilization, implying a 3–5 year payback. This hinges on sustained rental prices and chip useful life; residual values of GPUs have held up better than expected, unlike Bitcoin ASICs.
- New open-source and cheaper models are reducing compute intensity: a model referred to as Jev is 238 times cheaper than Fable, 70 times cheaper than Opus, and 38 times cheaper than Sonnet, and many enterprises are shifting workloads to lower-cost open-source models.
- Blackstone portfolio companies' annual token spend is jumping from $25 million last year to $500 million this year, a 20x increase, indicating rapid enterprise adoption of stablecoins and tokenized assets.
- The crypto market is in early bull territory with euphoria not yet widespread: many investors remain sidelined, and hosts admit to building watchlists but not buying. Backpack token has risen 15x since being mentioned on the podcast, contributing to FOMO.
- For tokenization exposure, the hosts identify Backpack and Securitize as key names; the 'tax implication' test is offered as a way to determine true ownership of tokenized assets.
- FTX's early-stage portfolio—including Anthropic, SpaceX, Cursor, Solana, and Robinhood—would be worth roughly $200 billion if it hadn't been sold, which would have made it a top-five venture fund by dollars returned. But the hosts argue this wasn't real venture because FTX used stolen money and did no underwriting.
- SEC guidance indicates token buybacks on functional protocols are not managerial efforts, potentially making fee switches viable; however, the definition of 'functional protocol' remains unclear.
- Robinhood rolled out perps in the US via a foreign board of trade (Fbot) using Bitstamp, similar to Coinbase's original route, because US perps are not directly regulated.
- Growth-stage crypto investing is attractive: roughly 60 crypto-related growth companies exist, mostly fintechs using stablecoins/tokenized assets, with much better multiples than 2021. LP interest is shifting from AI back to crypto, though only a few funds have earned the right to do late-stage deals.
- AI VC froth is high: funds that missed SpaceX, Anthropic, or OpenAI are chasing deals aggressively, rounds jump valuations rapidly, and some LPs believe pre-seed/seed AI funds are now too crowded for outsized returns.
- Personal AI agents may increase movement between financial products and likely pay with traditional rails for most user transactions, but LLMs tend to give median/mediocre advice, so wealth management stickiness may persist, especially for older, buy-and-hold Americans.
- Derek Thompson's new piece highlights that the share of Americans hosting friends or family monthly fell from 42% in 1975 to 19% today, a larger decline than church attendance.
