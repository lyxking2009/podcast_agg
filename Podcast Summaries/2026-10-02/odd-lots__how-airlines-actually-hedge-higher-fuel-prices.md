# How Airlines Actually Hedge Higher Fuel Prices

## Overview

Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway finally land the guest they have wanted for years on a topic everyone invokes and nobody explains: airline fuel hedging. David Kang, former group treasurer (VP treasury and risk management) at Qatar Airways and a career energy trader across FX, rates, oil broking and structuring at J.P. Morgan and Sumitomo, walks through how an airline actually runs a hedge book. Jet fuel is the second-largest cost after labour and the most volatile, but the jet market is too thin to hedge directly, so airlines use Brent (or heating oil for US carriers) as a proxy and trade swaps, call options and zero-cost collars - and, at the structured end, target redemption notes. The conversation covers why most US carriers retreated from hedging after getting burned in 2014-15 and simply pass costs through as surcharges, and what happened when oil spiked after the Iran conflict.

## Key Points

- Fuel is the second-biggest airline cost after labour and by far the most volatile, which is why hedging strategy can be a genuine competitive advantage - Southwest pioneered a famous version that others copied with varying success.
- The jet fuel market is too thin to hedge directly. North-West Europe SIFJET is thin, so airlines use Brent as their proxy; US carriers use heating oil instead.
- The plain-vanilla instruments: swaps (symmetric, best bought when oil is low, roughly $25-$35), call options (you pay a premium), zero-cost collars (funded by selling puts) and - at the structured end - target redemption notes where the bank pairs a vanilla zero-cost collar with an exotic note that redeems at five months, one year or two years.
- Kang's approach at Qatar Airways forecast three years out because the airline was accepting about 1.5 aircraft a month and opening a new route roughly every two months, so consumption growth was known in advance.
- The hedge book was sized against a $6.6 billion balance sheet consuming 24-27 million barrels in the first year alone.
- Most US carriers pulled back from hedging after being badly hurt in 2014-15, and Delta still lost over a billion dollars in 2020. The smaller US airlines simply pass costs to customers via fuel surcharges instead.
- The international carriers keep hedging because they have both a balance sheet and a hedge book big enough - and a revenue book big enough to charge a surcharge at the same time.
- The structural insight: Kang compares an airline to an oil refinery - both take in a volatile input and sell a product, so both are effectively trading the crack spread, just from opposite sides.
- Surcharges are opaque: a ticket will say 'fuel surcharge' without disclosing the percentage, and some carriers have abolished the line item entirely and rolled it into the fare.

## Implications

This is one of the better explanations of why corporate hedging is not the same as speculation: the airline is matching a physical consumption exposure, and the constraint is market liquidity rather than conviction. Two practical lessons transfer beyond airlines. First, when the underlying physical market is too thin to hedge, you accept basis risk by hedging a liquid proxy - and that basis is the real risk nobody models in the headline. Second, the surcharge is the release valve that lets carriers hedge and pass through cost simultaneously, which is why hedging shows up as relative advantage rather than absolute protection. Worth noting that Joe Weisenthal's own family exposure to Southwest stock comes up, and Southwest's hedging was the industry template.
