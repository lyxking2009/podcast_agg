# Using Decision Science in Investing: Masters in Business with Omar Aguilar

## Overview

Barry Ritholtz interviews Omar Aguilar - president, CEO and chief investment officer of Schwab Asset Management, which runs roughly $1.9 trillion and sits inside Schwab's $13T-plus asset base - about how a decision-science background shapes the way he runs money. Aguilar's path runs from actuarial science and applied statistics in Mexico City to a Fulbright at Duke (master's in statistics, PhD in decision sciences), then Bankers Trust, Merrill Lynch, Lehman Brothers, ING and Financial Engines before Schwab in 2011. The conversation covers the 4% retirement withdrawal rule (which he argues is a static answer to a dynamic problem), Bayesian updating as the bridge into behavioral finance, Schwab's bias-diagnostics 'biagnostics' tools for advisors, the rise of alternatives and the private-credit liquidity confusion, and why a low-cost core is the firm's deliberate product philosophy.

## Key Points

- Aguilar's mandate on joining Schwab in 2011 was explicitly to use technology and systematic strategies to create scale - with three non-negotiables: transparency, accessibility and low cost.
- Schwab Asset Management is now about $1.9 trillion; the firm is the fifth-largest ETF manufacturer in the world and runs an asset-weighted expense ratio of roughly 8 basis points.
- The critique of the 4% rule: income needs are not static, and depending on the rate environment 4% may be below the risk-free rate - so the drawdown should be dynamic, tied to inflation, real growth and rates.
- Longevity is the unspoken variable: a healthy 68-year-old may need 15-25 more years of spending, which was not true when 4% was originally calibrated.
- His PhD was in Bayesian statistics, which he presents as the natural bridge to behavioral finance - a prior (your experience) updated by all available data.
- He frames investor behaviour through the amygdala (fast, emotional, fight-or-flight) versus the frontal cortex (analytical), the same duality as thinking fast and slow.
- Schwab's BIagnostics tools diagnose advisor and client biases; more client information correlates with longer client relationships.
- On alternatives: the firm is constructive and recently acquired Forge Global; the biggest misconception about private credit is that it was ever a credit problem - it has always been a liquidity misunderstanding.
- Private marks are smoother and lag public markets by two or three quarters when markets fall, so treating public and private as directly comparable in a backtest or efficient frontier is a category error.
- One product anecdote: the Schwab dividend strategy started when he joined in 2011 and is now the largest dividend ETF in the world at over $100 billion.

## Implications

Two durable takeaways for practitioners. First, retirement drawdown should be treated as a state-dependent policy rather than a constant: the 4% rule embeds 1990s longevity and rate assumptions that no longer hold. Second, Aguilar's diagnosis of private credit as a liquidity-education problem rather than a credit-quality problem is the cleanest framing of where retail alternatives blow up - the risk is not the underlying assets, it is the mismatch between a multi-year lockup and investor expectations of daily liquidity. His point that private equity and credit marks lag public markets by two to three quarters also argues against comparing them in the same efficient frontier.
