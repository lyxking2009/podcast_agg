# Cambria Fund Profile: Shareholder Yield Suite

## Overview

A short Cambria Fund Profile episode in which Meb Faber, co-founder and CIO of Cambria Investment Management, reads a profile of the firm's shareholder-yield fund suite. The shareholder yield discipline targets companies returning cash to shareholders through the three levers of dividends, net share buybacks and debt paydown - deliberately broader than a dividend-yield screen, which can be dominated by companies whose price has fallen and whose dividend is about to be cut. Cambria applies the same discipline across US and international exposures; the corresponding published fund profiles and white papers on mebfaber.com cover the US-focused Cambria Shareholder Yield ETF (SYLD), the international developed-market Foreign Shareholder Yield ETF (FYLD) and the emerging-market Emerging Shareholder Yield ETF (EYLD). Content sourced from the mebfaber.com Cambria Fund Profile archive page for the shareholder-yield suite; this is a re-run of the original fund profile rather than a new interview.

## Key Points

- Shareholder yield is the sum of cash returned to shareholders via dividends, net buybacks and debt reduction - not dividend yield alone.
- The stated reason for going beyond dividends: very high dividend yields often reflect a collapsed share price and a dividend that is about to be cut, so a pure yield screen selects for distress.
- Buybacks carry a tax efficiency that dividends do not, which is part of the case for weighting total shareholder yield rather than income alone.
- The discipline is applied consistently across geographies via a suite: US (SYLD), international developed (FYLD) and emerging markets (EYLD).
- Cambria publishes the underlying white papers for the US and international versions of the strategy alongside the fund profiles.
- These fund-profile episodes are short, reference-style readings of the strategy documentation rather than interviews with guests.

## Implications

The useful framing is that shareholder yield is a quality filter disguised as a yield screen. Because high dividend yield is often a distress signal, a manager that ranks on total cash returned - and explicitly includes net buybacks and debt paydown - is screening for balance-sheet capacity rather than for a high headline number. The same logic travels to the international versions of the suite, where dividend-only screens run into sector and country concentration.
