# Richard Falk-Wallace – Risk Models for the Front Office with Arcana (S8E1) — Flirting with Models

## TL;DR
Richard Falk-Wallace, founder of Arcana, argues that fundamental portfolio managers need to adopt factor-based risk and portfolio construction tools historically built for risk officers. He explains how factor exposures drag Sharpe ratios, why sizing and timing often destroy value, and how breaking track records into components reveals persistent weaknesses. Arcana provides a front-office platform to measure ex-ante risk, performance decomposition, and iterative process improvement, amid a secular convergence of quant and fundamental investing.

## Key points
- Richard Falk-Wallace founded Arcana after roles at JP Morgan, Silver Point, Viking, and Citadel, observing that factor risk models were built for risk officers, not portfolio managers, leaving a gap in front-office portfolio intelligence.
- Arcana provides a portfolio intelligence layer offering ex-ante volatility decomposition into idiosyncratic and factor exposures, factor drill-downs, what-if portfolio construction, optimization, hedging, and performance decomposition including sizing, selection, timing, hit rates, earnings vs ex-earnings, and aging buckets.
- A secular trend is driving fundamental PMs to adopt quant techniques: pod shops demonstrated superior risk-adjusted returns, factor volatility has risen due to passive investing and market concentration, and alpha has become less abundant, making risk management table stakes.
- The convergence of quant and fundamental is two-sided: quants are moving up the stack from trading signals to alternative data and now AI-driven qualitative insight, while fundamental managers are adopting portfolio construction and factor risk management to avoid being front-run.
- The most important portfolio construction lesson is that knowable correlations act as concentration and drag Sharpe ratios; factor models measure forward-looking correlation, and even skilled fundamental books suffer if they carry unintended factor exposures.
- A counterintuitive insight: even correct factor timing can reduce a PM's Sharpe ratio because most factors have low Sharpe ratios; the market factor's long-term Sharpe is only 0.6–0.8, while good market-neutral PMs can achieve 1–2, so any factor exposure pulls the portfolio's Sharpe toward the factor's level.
- In pod shops, the most effective guardrails are a predictive (ex-ante) beta band and a minimum idiosyncratic share of variance, optionally with single-factor variance contribution limits; these improve outcomes without over-constraining construction.
- Long-only benchmark-relative managers are effectively market neutral against the benchmark; adopting a tracking error and information ratio framework reveals factor exposures relative to the benchmark, but structural size and liquidity constraints can force unwanted factor bets.
- Breaking track records into sizing, selection, and timing shows that sizing skill is often volatile and frequently a Sharpe ratio drag, whereas selection return is more stable; many PMs lose money on sizing even if overall returns are positive.
- Consistent patterns emerge in performance decomposition: earnings hit rates often mirror overall residual hit rates; PMs tend to consistently make or lose money around earnings and in early position aging, such as losing money in the first 15–30 days before the thesis plays out.
- The easiest performance improvement is not finding new alpha but isolating and eliminating persistent losing behaviors—such as specific timing losses or earnings bleed—while preserving the core skill, akin to sports teams iterating on execution.
- Factor models do not dictate direction; they enable hedging by decomposing exposures and scanning the full universe for alpha, avoiding the dead weight of pair trades when the PM has no view on the paired name.
- A reasonable evaluation horizon for a market-neutral PM's residual hit rate is about two years, assuming monthly portfolio rebalancing; higher volatility requires longer periods, lower volatility shorter, and quant books with higher turnover can use shorter windows.
- The core workhorse factor model—beta, momentum, growth, value, quality, industry—remains the stable baseline; adding thematic factors like AI or crowding increases explanatory power but reduces tractability and persistence, so the most important factor is buy-in across PMs, CIO, and risk.
- PMs should treat a theme as idiosyncratic only after checking its overlap with core factors and estimating residual vol impact; there is "no gold star" for the highest idio share—once above a threshold, the goal is P&L subject to risk constraints.
- Fundamental investors are overwhelmed by noise; factors are orders of magnitude more important to portfolio outcomes than individual data points, so adding more information without prioritizing risk factors can make investors worse, while those focused on risk tend to perform better.
- AI and natural language interfaces (including MCPs, APIs, Excel plugins) increase interactivity with risk data, enabling PMs to slice, drill down, and iterate on process; this creates a Jevons paradox where lower cost of analysis leads to more analysis and better portfolio construction.
