# George Osborne: Inside OpenAI & Can Britain Keep Up With US? — The Master Investor Podcast with Wilfred Frost

## TL;DR
Former UK Chancellor George Osborne, now at OpenAI, argues that AI, robotics, and digital finance are deflationary forces despite war-induced oil shocks, and that ChatGPT's 1.4 billion users show globalization continues. He attributes the UK's slower growth to the banking crash, Brexit (costing 7-8% of GDP), and political instability, and says the Conservative Party must avoid becoming the hard Brexit, anti-business party. Osborne also discusses AI cyber risks, data center local opposition, and central bank failures to maintain price stability.

## Key points
- George Osborne is now OpenAI's managing director and head of countries, alongside roles at Robey Warshaw (sold to Evercore), Coinbase advisory council, British Museum, and co-hosting Political Currency with Ed Balls.
- On data center politics, Osborne says the assumption that companies can build data centers anywhere without local acceptance is over; OpenAI now works with local communities, such as in Abilene, Texas, making commitments on electricity prices, water, and benefits for students and technical colleges, with similar deals in states like Michigan.
- The UK government's most recent budget included an AI compact that wants data centers built but only under certain conditions, which OpenAI supports; Osborne says countries generally prefer to have data centers and compute onshore for sovereignty and control rather than relying on foreign infrastructure.
- Osborne challenges the deglobalization narrative: ChatGPT has 1.4 billion users and has spread worldwide without being impeded by tariffs, making AI labs profound globalizing forces even amid trade disputes and tariff barriers.
- On inflation, he argues that AI, robotics, and digital finance are inherently deflationary, even though war with Iran has spiked oil prices and disrupted supply chains; overall direction remains deflationary.
- Osborne agrees with Kevin Warsh's critique that central banks were behind the curve on inflation after COVID and the Ukraine invasion, and that they should focus narrowly on price stability rather than pursuing social goals.
- He defends the Bank of England's single eight-year term for the governor as a good innovation to avoid politicized reappointment, noting that Liz Truss's government attacked the Bank but collapsed after 50 days.
- Osborne identifies three main causes for UK underperformance relative to the US since 2008: the banking crash hit the UK disproportionately hard, Brexit was a self-inflicted wound costing 7-8% of GDP, and political instability with rapid changes of prime minister and chancellor deterred investment.
- On regulation, he says the UK can be more permissive than the US because it is a smaller, centralized state; the US has multiple regulators, still uses physical checks, and lags in digital payments, while the UK risks drifting toward a European model with a growing state since 2019.
- Osborne credits the 2010 election mandate for spending cuts with enabling tough reforms like raising the pension age to 66-67, saving trillions in future liabilities; he says he might have cut current spending more and invested more in capital.
- He stresses that bond markets are real, as demonstrated by the Liz Truss/Kwasi Kwarteng budget and later Rachel Reeves budgets, and that a clear plan ('plan beats no plan' from Tim Geithner) is essential for confidence.
- On US-China relations, Osborne says he was pro-engagement rather than pro-Chinese Communist Party, and argues that the West gains leverage by engaging; global AI regulation requires US-China dialogue, with tentative steps at G20 and UN.
- He declines to comment on US chip export controls, saying that is above his pay grade and suggesting an interview with Jensen Huang.
- Osborne identifies the biggest current AI risk as bad human actors using AI for cyberattacks and disruption, not robots or machines; OpenAI has released a new cyber model to identify flaws in systems, and he expects a race between good and bad actors for cyber tools in the next year.
- Andrew Bailey, heading the Financial Stability Board, lists cyber disruption to the financial system as a top risk, where AI will be both friend and challenge, according to Osborne.
- Osborne remains optimistic on AI, seeing it as democratizing tools previously reserved for the rich, and predicting major breakthroughs in disease cure and treatment.
- On crypto and digital finance, Osborne argues that decentralized finance disrupts existing monopolies and unfair costs; tokenized assets can fractionalize shares and property, and the UK has the lowest equity ownership among G7 nations.
- As Chancellor, Osborne forced banks to transfer customer data within seven days when switching accounts, which he says unintentionally sparked the neo-bank revolution of Revolut and Monzo.
- He calls Brexit a massive mistake and warns that if the Conservative Party remains hard Brexit and anti-EU at the next election, it risks becoming the anti-business party; he believes rejoining would likely require a referendum.
- His career advice is to embrace change: in his early 50s he moved into AI and American tech, and he cautions against wallowing in past glories.
