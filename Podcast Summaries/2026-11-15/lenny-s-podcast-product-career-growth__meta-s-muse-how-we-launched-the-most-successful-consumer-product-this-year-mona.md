# Meta’s Muse: How we launched the most successful consumer product this year | Mona Sarantakos — Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth

## TL;DR
Mona Sarantakos, VP of Product at Meta Super Intelligence Labs and head of product for Muse, shares the inside story of how Nat Friedman's tip about OpenClaw ignited a seven-month sprint to build what Mark Zuckerberg calls the fastest-growing AI consumer product ever. The team prioritized trust, safety, and reliability over speed, leading to distinctive design choices like the Jolly avatar and chat bubbles, while relying on agent swarms, rigorous evals, and hands-on leadership from Friedman, Alex Wang, and Zuckerberg. She also discusses activation challenges, surprising use cases like shopping, and a future where Muse becomes an ambient personal superintelligence across devices.

## Key points
- Muse originated in late January or early February when Nat Friedman messaged Mona Sarantakos and others about OpenClaw, prompting her to set it up on a Raspberry Pi and give it access to her email and calendar; she experienced the magic when it summarized school newsletters and made a Monday morning feel productive, convincing her to pursue a consumer agent.
- Before the pivot, the team was zero-basing Meta AI and had expected consumer agents to arrive around 2026–2027; the OpenClaw experience accelerated the timeline dramatically.
- Despite competitors launching in a couple of months, Muse took about seven months from idea to launch because the team prioritized reliability, safety, and security, driven by the internal motto 'trust is a must'—they feared early issues would permanently destroy user trust.
- The build happened in three phases: first defining the product without worrying about scale or safety; by spring they had an end-to-end product; then they spent months hardening safety, security, and reliability, especially after adding a browser which opened new security vectors.
- The core team was unusually small for Meta—about 10 people initially and under 100 total—and operated without formal reviews or slide decks; they held casual prototype sessions with Mark Zuckerberg, who would often extend meetings beyond scheduled time.
- Nat Friedman was deeply involved as part of the team, contributing to architecture and security design; Alex Wang led model development and later pushed for launch; Mark Zuckerberg shaped the product vision by emphasizing that Muse's purpose is to make people's lives better and help them achieve goals, which led to proactive features and the Goals tab.
- The Jolly avatar was created by lead designer Alex Cornell as a temporary placeholder, named 'Jolly Bot' after his daughter 'Jolly Bug'; multiple attempts to replace it with a corporate logo were resisted, and Mark Zuckerberg personally liked it, reinforcing the principle that a personal agent should not feel like a corporate entity.
- Design choices were intentional: chat bubbles were selected over a canvas interface to mimic familiar messaging patterns and reinforce the personal assistant metaphor, even though it constrained design; the main chat vs side chats debate was highly polarizing among employees, ultimately leading to adding side chats due to demand, though Mona personally prefers a single main chat.
- The team maintains over 100 evals across categories including safety (prompt injection, harm), capability, behavioral, and use-case; notably, the system prompt and behavioral spec must be handwritten by humans, not generated by AI, to ensure quality.
- Agent swarms were used to collect and synthesize feedback and synthetic data, providing deep insight into multi-turn conversations and specific user pain points, which increased empathy and enabled targeted product improvements.
- Activation is the number one problem; word of mouth is the most effective lever—seeing a friend use Muse on their phone led to immediate downloads—and the Ideas tab with starter packs helps users overcome the blank first prompt, while onboarding is being tiered for early vs later adopters.
- Shopping emerged as a top use case unexpectedly, while home automation did not gain as much traction as early adopters predicted; Christina Wad, head of engineering, is a heavy shopping user and generates many bug reports from her purchases.
- An early experiment where two Muses were placed in a group chat revealed discretion issues: one Muse disclosed another user's doctor appointment, and the two AIs got into a passive-aggressive fight, highlighting unresolved challenges for multiplayer agent interactions.
- The future vision for Muse includes moving beyond the app to ambient devices like glasses and charm devices, reducing the need for user babysitting by increasing agent autonomy, and democratizing personal superintelligence to help with financial betterment and side hustles.
- The role of product manager has fundamentally changed: prototyping is now fast and accessible, product taste has become the most important skill, and PMs must maintain a very high bar to avoid shipping 'AI slop'.
- Users can send feedback directly through Muse, which generates a report to the team; Mona encourages this as a way to help improve the product.
