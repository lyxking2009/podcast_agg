# Disney: The Renaissance and the Empire — Acquired

## TL;DR
Ben Gilbert and David Rosenthal tell the story of how Disney went from the brink of breakup in 1984 — corporate raiders circling, the animation division a 'rotting carcass,' and only ~$2M of $250M profits coming from film/TV — to the diversified global behemoth of today. The episode covers the Bass family rescue, the Eisner-Wells-Katzenberg turnaround and their 'singles and doubles' Paramount playbook, Roy E. Disney's fight to save animation, the CalArts pipeline that produced the Renaissance generation, and the empire-era expansion through acquisitions (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, Fox, ESPN, ABC) into streaming and the innovator's dilemma.

## Key points
- 1984 context: Disney in chaos — animation 'a rotting carcass,' EPCOT an overbudget World's Fair knockoff, stock down from $82 to $52, corporate raiders circling with breakup plans (sell the film library to MGM, sell parks to hotel operators).
- The Bass family (Fort Worth) and Richard Rainwater became Disney's largest shareholders at ~25% — a friendly deal to ward off hostile raiders.
- September 7, 1984 boardroom coup: Roy E. Disney and Stanley Gold forced out CEO Ron Miller; in 14 days they recruited Michael Eisner (chairman/CEO) and Frank Wells (president) — the 'greatest two-person management team in media history' — plus Jeffrey Katzenberg to run the studios.
- Eisner's 'singles and doubles' / high-concept strategy from Paramount: low production costs, no A-list stars, quality scripts first — 'celebrity can open a film, but celebrity can't carry a film' (Katzenberg memo).
- First moves: raise park ticket/parking prices (parking was still $1!) — pure incremental profit — and use the cash to fund the live-action slate (27 of first 33 films profitable; financed via Silver Screen partners).
- Animation exile: Eisner moved animation out of the Burbank lot to a rundown Glendale building — the animators thought the flywheel core was dead.
- The CalArts pipeline: Walt created CalArts and left half his estate to it; Classroom A113 produced John Lasseter, Brad Bird, Tim Burton, John Musker, Andrew Stanton, Brenda Chapman, Pete Docter — Disney hired them, then fired them all; Roy E. Disney became chairman of animation to protect Walt's vision.
- The flywheel: make great characters/universally relatable stories, then pump them through consumer products, parks, re-releases — 'the most successful enterprise ever created for monetizing human nostalgia.'
- The Renaissance (The Little Mermaid, Aladdin, Lion King) and the Empire era (Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, Fox, ESPN, ABC, streaming) — attaching the classic flywheel to a massive TV/streaming business.
- The modern question: can Disney fight the innovator's dilemma — old profitable businesses (cable, theatrical, DVDs) fading while Disney+ loses billions — and keep the future as bright as the past?

## Overview
This Acquired episode (Fall 2026 season) is the story of Disney from near-death to empire. It opens in 1984 with the company in dire straits — a hostile-takeover atmosphere, a broken animation division, and a flywheel generating essentially all its profit from parks and consumer products. The rescue comes through the Bass family's 25% stake and a boardroom coup that installs Michael Eisner, Frank Wells, and Jeffrey Katzenberg. The hosts detail how the Eisner-Wells team applied the Paramount 'singles and doubles' playbook — cheap, script-first live-action hits financed through Silver Screen partnerships — while Roy E. Disney fought to preserve the animation flywheel, protecting the CalArts-bred talent who would ignite the Renaissance. The second half covers how Disney attached that classic flywheel to increasingly large businesses — ESPN, ABC, Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm, Fox — and the modern era's central tension: the transition to streaming (Disney+, Hulu, ESPN+) that lost billions while legacy cash cows (cable, theatrical, home video) decline, framing Disney as the ultimate case study in the innovator's dilemma.

## Implications
- For business historians/investors: Disney is the canonical case of the flywheel model — IP → characters → parks/products/re-releases — and its power to compound across a century.
- For strategists: the singles-and-doubles playbook (cheap, script-first production) shows how to run a hits business without star-power dependence.
- For today's media companies: the streaming transition is the innovator's dilemma in real time — cannibalizing profitable legacy businesses for an unproven future.
- For founders: talent pipelines matter — Walt built CalArts as a self-renewing source of creative talent, a lesson in institutionalizing your moat.
