# RWH071: Risk, Ruin, Reinvention & Resilience w/ Victor Haghani — The Investor's Podcast (We Study Billionaires)  - The Investor’s Podcast Network

## TL;DR
William Green interviews Victor Haghani — founder and CIO of Elm Wealth, co-author of 'The Missing Billionaires', and the youngest co-founding partner of Long-Term Capital Management — about a career defined by giddy highs and devastating lows. Haghani traces his path from Salomon Brothers' legendary government arbitrage desk (and its Liars Poker culture of edge-based betting) through the founding and 1998 collapse of LTCM, and reflects on what he learned about risk, position sizing, and low-probability high-consequence events. He argues LTCM's positions were not irresponsibly large relative to Wall Street norms, that the deeper lessons were about the intrinsic uncertainty of life, and that the distinction between a leveraged institutional pool of capital and personal financial decisions is critical.

## Key points
- Background: born in New York in 1962 to an Iranian Jewish father and American mother; moved to Iran at 14, fled after the 1979 revolution (his father lost most of his wealth), finished school in London, studied at LSE — an experience that shaped a lifelong focus on risk, uncertainty, and impermanence.
- Father's money lessons: 'it's harder to hold on to money than to make money', and don't push spending too far in good times because cutting your standard of living is brutally painful.
- Salomon Brothers (1984-93): started in bond research, moved to the government arbitrage desk; a flat organization where junior people met the CEO; built layered convergence trades (on-the-run vs off-the-run bonds, bond futures, OTC option volatility) that spanned desks — emblematic of edge-based, non-speculative trading.
- Liars Poker was played for fun, camaraderie, and as an outlet — but reflected the desk's core principle: only take risk where there is an observable, measurable edge, and size bets accordingly.
- LTCM co-founder (youngest partner, ~32): consensus-based portfolio/risk/executive committees; the Italy trade (CCTs vs fixed-rate BTPs, ending in a positive-carry, credit-hedged swap) showed careful tail-risk thinking; the fund never lost money two months in a row through 1997, averaging ~31.2%/year.
- The 1998 collapse: Russia defaulted on domestic/ruble debt (not dollar debt), triggering a global risk-off move; LTCM's losses came from the cascade (balance-sheet pullbacks everywhere, then positions pushed further once liquidation was anticipated) — not from a large direct Russia position.
- Against 'When Genius Failed': Haghani disputes Loewenstein's portrayal (no partner interviews, written too soon); he argues position sizes were comparable to other major firms' risk-taking, decisions were consensus-based, and the real lessons took until ~2010 to crystallize.
- Key frameworks: low-probability high-consequence events are part of investing (90% drawdown 1929-32, six 40-50% episodes since 1900, the 1987 22% one-day plunge); diversification is 'the only free lunch in finance' (spread assets across redundant options); and LTCM-style leverage logic does not transfer to personal finance.

## Overview
The conversation is part memoir, part investment philosophy. Haghani narrates a life shaped by displacement — an Iranian-Jewish father who lost his fortune in the revolution, an opera-singer mother, a youth spent between New York, Iran, and London — which instilled both optimism about progress and a visceral sense that everything can change. At Salomon Brothers he rose through bond research to the fixed-income arbitrage desk, where the culture of edge-driven trading (and Liars Poker, which taught probabilistic decision-making under uncertainty) was forged. He then co-founded LTCM, describing the extraordinary first years (convergence trades, the Italy trade, returns that exceeded expectations) before the Russian default sparked the 1998 cascade that nearly wiped out the fund. Crucially, he pushes back on the popular narrative: he argues the positions were defensible relative to industry norms, decisions were made by consensus committees, and the 'genius failed' framing misses the real lessons. He then pivots to what those lessons are: the importance of position sizing, the reality of low-probability high-consequence events, the need for flexibility and redundancy in personal finances, and the sharp distinction between a leveraged institutional pool and an individual's wealth. He closes with practical guidance on probabilistic thinking, spending flexibility, and why the goal was never to die with the most money.

## Implications
- For investors: position sizing matters more than edge — even great investments become bad investments at too much scale; size for survival of low-probability, high-consequence events.
- For practitioners: treat personal financial decisions differently from institutional leveraged strategies — diversification across redundant options is the only free lunch.
- For students of finance: LTCM's failure was a liquidity/cascade event (Russia's unexpected defaulting pattern, forced liquidations), not simply over-leverage — risk models must include the behavior of other market participants.
- For founders/leaders: Haghani's family story illustrates how exogenous shocks (revolution, default, regime change) hit concentrated wealth — redundancy and humility about the future are survival traits.

- "Regular way bank accounts are the only free lunch in finance — diversification. Always have multiply redundant options; don't leave your stuff in one place."
