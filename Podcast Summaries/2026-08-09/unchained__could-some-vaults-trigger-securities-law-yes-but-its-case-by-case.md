# Could Some Vaults Trigger Securities Law? Yes, but It's Case by Case — Unchained

## TL;DR
Laura Shin interviews Sun Ragupathi, co-founder and CEO of Veda (onchain vault infrastructure provider), about the $67B onchain vault market, how vaults work, the layers of risk users accept, and the SEC's recent signals that some vaults could implicate federal securities law. Ragupathi breaks down the three dimensions of vault design (access, control, verifiability), explains why risk has shifted from smart-contract exploits to operational/key-management failures (Kelp, Drift, Stream Finance), and reads SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce's statement as a nuanced, case-by-case Howey-test question rather than a death blow — arguing regulatory clarity is exactly what institutional entrants need to jump in. He also shares Veda's Kraken partnership results: $600M+ in assets across 80,000+ users, many new to DeFi.

## Key points
- Vaults are onchain vehicles that give users simple exposure to complex DeFi markets — 'infrastructure for creating financial products on top of onchain markets' (lending, RWA baskets, trading vaults); TVL is now ~$67B.
- Three design dimensions: access (widest range of chains/assets/strategies), control (guard rails and compliance/risk policies for fintech distributors), and verifiability (predictability of withdrawals, transparency into where assets are at all times — the anti-FTX/BlockFi promise).
- Distribution is shifting from DeFi-native 'degens' to fintech platforms (Robinhood Earn, Coinbase, Kraken, MetaMask) — vaults abstract the complexity of peer-to-peer protocols into simple user preferences.
- Risk layers: smart-contract risk (down sharply over 5 years; most 2021 exploits were code flaws) vs operational/key-management risk (now the dominant layer — governance upgrades, key roles; Kelp and Drift incidents) vs economic/collateral risk (collateral crashing faster than liquidation).
- Stream Finance (Nov incident): ~$285M of exposure in vaults where curators chased high yields; post-Stream, onchain risk-taking has scaled back and users pulled funds, but Ragupathi expects similar events again — DeFi's core value prop is financing long-tail assets, which entails risk; the key is users knowing the risk they're taking.
- SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce's statement: some vaults 'could be a common enterprise in which users invest money with a reasonable expectation of profits to be derived from the vault deployers' and curators' efforts' — the Howey test. Ragupathi's read: nuanced and case-by-case; Aave/Morpho lending vaults don't take exposure to securities, but vaults that hold tokenized securities clearly implicate securities law; clarity is a prerequisite for institutions to fully enter.
- Veda's model: three parties — infrastructure provider (Veda), curator (risk management, e.g. Centaur for Kraken products), and distributor (owns the user relationship); Veda is one of the oldest vault providers, at $1B+ scale with no security incidents.
- Kraken DeFi Earn partnership (launched Jan): 3 stablecoin products, expanded to BTC earn, now $600M+ in assets across 80,000+ users, many of whom never used DeFi before — evidence that enterprise distribution is how DeFi reaches the mainstream.
- Competition: the category still has 'a thousandx to go', so cooperation (grow the pie) beats knives-out competition; insurance is emerging but no major claim has been processed yet — skepticism warranted.

## Overview
This episode is a fundamentals-plus-regulation deep dive on onchain vaults. Sun Ragupathi explains what vaults are — abstraction layers that let normal users express simple preferences (yield, liquidity, risk appetite) over complex DeFi markets — and the three things vaults must solve: access, control, and verifiability. He describes the industry's evolution from an 'onchain casino' of 2021-era experiments to institutional-grade infrastructure now distributed through fintech apps, and he maps the risk stack from smart contracts to operations to collateral economics, arguing that operational risk is where the industry now needs to focus. The second half centers on regulation: Ragupathi contextualizes SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce's statement — which quoted the Howey test — as an inevitable and welcome development now that vault products are reaching mainstream users, and reads it as a call for nuanced, facts-and-circumstances analysis rather than a blanket ban. He emphasizes that Veda has been engaging regulators to educate them on vault design space. The episode closes with the Kraken case study ($600M+/80K users), Veda's differentiators (five years of operating history, enterprise scale, zero incidents), and the view that the biggest unlock for the category is regulatory clarity.

## Implications
- For DeFi builders: expect vault products to be scrutinized under the Howey test case-by-case — vaults holding tokenized securities will clearly implicate securities law; lending vaults over non-security protocols (Aave, Morpho) are on firmer ground.
- For users: understand the layers of risk (smart contract, operational, economic) before putting money in a vault; 'users shouldn't think they're in a safe insured savings account when they're getting exposure to what is basically an onchain hedge fund'.
- For fintechs/institutions: regulatory clarity, not technology, is the main gating factor for full entry; rating agencies (S&P interest, Credora) are beginning to evaluate vault risk.
- For the industry: operational standards for key management (multisigs, time locks, hardware keys) and third-party risk ratings are the next frontier as smart-contract risk declines.
