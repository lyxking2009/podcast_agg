# The Bank of England's Megan Greene on Monetary Policy in a World of Supply Shocks

## Key Points

- The UK economy has been weak for years with both demand and supply-side weaknesses, making it vulnerable to inflationary pressures even with modest growth
- Multiple successive supply shocks (COVID, Ukraine war, energy crisis) have created inflation persistence that goes beyond typical 'transitory' effects
- Households and businesses have become more sensitive to inflation and now engage in state-dependent pricing, changing prices more frequently when inflation rises
- Central banks must focus on second-round effects from supply shocks, where price increases change wage and price-setting behavior, potentially creating spirals
- The traditional central banking approach of 'looking through' supply shocks doesn't work when they occur repeatedly wave after wave
- UK mortgage market structure (mostly 2-5 year fixed rates) means monetary policy transmits differently than in the US, with ongoing impacts as people roll off old rates
- Significant spillovers from US monetary policy affect UK financial conditions, with US inflation and jobs data being key drivers of UK bond markets
- Central banks lack proper tools to address supply-side problems since their instruments are designed for demand-side management
- AI represents the one potential positive supply shock on the horizon, but timing and magnitude remain highly uncertain
- The era of precise forecasting has ended; central banking now requires scenario analysis and risk management in an age of radical uncertainty

## Overview

The conversation explores how central banking has fundamentally changed in an era of repeated supply shocks, with Megan Greene providing insights from her transition from financial analyst to Bank of England policymaker. Greene argues that the traditional framework of 'looking through' temporary supply disruptions breaks down when economies face successive shocks from COVID, war, energy crises, and emerging economic statecraft. The discussion reveals how these repeated disruptions have altered business and household behavior around pricing and inflation expectations, creating persistence effects that monetary policy must address. Greene emphasizes that central banks are entering a new era where scenario planning and risk management replace precise forecasting, as the old economic frameworks no longer adequately explain how the global economy functions. The conversation culminates with Greene defending the Bank's recent decision to hold rates despite inflationary risks, citing the need for more information about energy price developments and second-round effects before acting.

## Implications

For practitioners, this signals a fundamental shift toward managing businesses and products in an environment of persistent uncertainty rather than predictable cycles. Founders should build resilience against repeated supply disruptions and consider AI as both opportunity and wildcard. PMs need to factor inflation sensitivity into product pricing strategies, as customers and businesses now react more quickly to price changes. Engineers working on global systems should design for supply chain volatility and consider geopolitical risks as permanent features rather than temporary disruptions. The move away from precise forecasting toward scenario planning suggests practitioners should adopt similar approaches in strategic planning, building multiple contingency plans rather than relying on single predicted outcomes.
