# What It Would Actually Take for a Machine to Have Agency (Michael Tomasello) — Machine Learning Street Talk (MLST)

## TL;DR
Michael Tomasello, developmental psychologist and author of The Evolution of Agency, argues that agency evolved as a response to environmental unpredictability: predictable niches favor fixed behaviors, while unpredictable ones require decision-makers. He distinguishes levels from reptiles' simple control loops, to squirrels that imagine themselves falling, to great apes that seek missing information, and then to human shared agency built on joint goals, joint attention, and perspective-taking. Tomasello contends that large language models began at the endpoint of linguistic convention and skipped the shared social infrastructure, so they lack the agency humans have.

## Key points
- Tomasello's core thesis: agency exists because the world is unpredictable; when environments are predictable, evolution builds behaviors directly into organisms, but unpredictability selects for a decision-making apparatus.
- He defines agency through control loops, will, and causal power, and discusses how to evaluate simple machines: a traffic light, thermostat, and robot vacuum lack the flexible, goal-directed decision-making that characterizes biological agents.
- The discussion explores whether a machine could have more agency than humans; Tomasello indicates that genuine agency requires infrastructure like goals, emotions, and social perspective that current AI lacks, rather than just more computational power.
- Tomasello uses Darwinian emergence and the limits of learning to explain where goals come from: evolution builds in goals and emotional biases ('nature's finger on the scale') rather than leaving everything to general learning.
- He references Chomsky and selfish genes to argue that goals aren't learned from scratch but are inherited as evolved modules and motivational systems.
- Tomasello avoids the word 'intelligence' because it is too vague and loaded; he prefers to analyze agency as the organizing principle of behavior.
- He outlines four levels of agency: reptiles running simple control loops; squirrels that can imagine potential outcomes (like falling) and choose alternatives; great apes that seek additional information before committing; and humans who add shared agency.
- Shared agency in humans involves forming joint goals, attending to the same things together, and understanding that one object can be seen from different perspectives.
- Children who lose sound on a video call spontaneously invent gestures and turn them into shared conventions, demonstrating the bottom-up creation of social coordination and language-like systems.
- Large language models, by contrast, start from the endpoint—existing linguistic conventions—and skip the infrastructure of joint attention, shared goals, and perspective-taking, so they do not create or ground conventions the way humans do.
- The stag hunt illustrates conventions as coordination devices; lying only works among cooperators, revealing the dark side of cooperation and why trust and gullibility are connected.
- Humans create status functions like money and presidents through collective intentionality; Tomasello discusses whether capital can be an agent, considering emergence and the invisible hand.
- Dennett's intentional stance is not sufficient for agency: treating something as an agent doesn't make it one; genuine agency requires the internal causal organization described earlier.
