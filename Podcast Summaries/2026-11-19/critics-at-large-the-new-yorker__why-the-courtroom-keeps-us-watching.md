# Why the Courtroom Keeps Us Watching — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
In this episode of Critics at Large, hosts Alex Schwartz, Naomi Fry, and Vinson Cunningham explore why legal dramas—from The Good Wife to Cupertino and War—continue to captivate audiences. They speak with New Yorker general counsel Fabio Bertoni, who argues that the best legal stories capture lawyers' terror of inadequacy and the redemptive power of winning, and that My Cousin Vinny gets courtroom procedure right. The hosts then review the new CBS show Cupertino (mostly critical, calling it didactic and procedurally implausible) and the HBO divorce drama War (which Cunningham praises), while also touching on Anatomy of a Fall and real-world trials like Lindsay Clancy, Russell Brand, and Johnny Depp v. Amber Heard.

## Key points
- Hosts identify core elements of legal drama: courtroom scenes with objections, eccentric lawyers, ripped-from-headlines stories, and a built-in antagonism between prosecution and defense that creates narrative tension.
- Fabio Bertoni, New Yorker general counsel, argues compelling legal dramas capture the lawyer's constant terror of not being diligent, thorough, or smart enough, and the redemptive feeling of winning; he cites The Verdict with Paul Newman as a film that gets this arc right.
- Bertoni praises My Cousin Vinny (1992) for accurately depicting courtroom procedure, especially expert witness testimony, despite being a comedy; he says most legal shows botch procedure and can be cartoonish.
- The hosts discuss their favorite screen lawyers: Alex Schwartz cites Will Gardner (Josh Charles) on The Good Wife for his appealing arrogance and clarity; Naomi Fry cites L.A. Law for making law seem sexy; Vinson Cunningham cites Ben Matlock for the genteel Southern lawyer trope.
- Cupertino, created by Robert and Michelle King, follows lawyer Michael Price (Mike Colter) and associate Olivia (Rachel Keller) after they are fired from an AI startup and start a firm representing underdogs against Silicon Valley Goliaths.
- Alex Schwartz finds Cupertino "not good," overly didactic in its "tech sucks" message, and implausible: it skips procedural steps and lacks the texture and character depth of The Good Wife, also by the Kings.
- Naomi Fry criticizes Cupertino for feeling wooden and highly accelerated, with plot resolutions arriving too quickly; she argues the AI theme heightens the problem because we don't understand AI in real life, making the show's pat resolutions less believable.
- Vinson Cunningham is more positive about Cupertino, noting its formal surrealism—overhead shots, CCTV-style framing, unusual color grading—and the fact that one of the firm's clients is an AGI (artificial general intelligence), which gives the show a distinct, absurdist relationship to Silicon Valley.
- The HBO drama War, starring Dominic West and Sienna Miller, depicts a high-profile divorce triggered by a honeypot the wife sends to catch her husband cheating; Cunningham likes it as an ensemble piece about lawyering and the effect of immense wealth on a case.
- Alex Schwartz highlights Anatomy of a Fall (2023) as a legal drama that lives in ambiguity: the French courtroom procedure differs from American, the defendant is allowed to speak, and the film focuses on how each side constructs competing narratives about the marriage rather than delivering clear justice.
- Naomi Fry, who has reported on the Ghislaine Maxwell, Weinstein, and Stormy Daniels trials, says real-world trials appeal because they promise order and resolution in chaotic times, and audiences watch to see whether the rule of law will be upheld.
- The hosts connect the legal drama to real trials: the O.J. Simpson trial crystallized race, policing, gender, and violence; recent cases like Lindsay Clancy, Russell Brand, Johnny Depp v. Amber Heard, and E. Jean Carroll serve similar functions, sometimes offering catharsis or a forum for cultural antagonism.
