# Mike Milken: Access to Capital, the American Dream, and Why Risk Is Misunderstood — In Good Company with Nicolai Tangen

## TL;DR
Mike Milken tells Nicolai Tangen how a single encounter after the Watts Riots sent him to study credit history — and how that led him to build the high-yield bond market on the finding that the best risk-adjusted returns came from the debt everyone had written off.

## Key points
- Origin story: as a 19-year-old back from Berkeley, Milken met a young Black man in Los Angeles after the Watts Riots who told him he would be denied access to capital because of his skin colour. That conversation redirected his career toward credit.
- Studying centuries of credit data, he concluded that almost everything conventional wisdom held was wrong — including the ranking of sovereign, individual and corporate credit quality.
- The empirical finding: during the 1930s depression the highest returns came from the lowest-quality debt, meaning risk premiums were too large. And the worst credit in history was sovereign debt, which was treated as riskless.
- “99.99% of every company in the world is non-investment grade” — the structural fact that made high yield a market rather than a niche.
- On persuasion, Milken describes questioning as the only method that works: people don’t change their views when you raise your voice, they change when they discover the facts themselves (crediting Hans Rosling’s Factfulness technique).
- Capital access as the thesis of his second career: cable, mobile and media industries exist in their current form because of high-yield financing.
- He also reflects on surviving a cancer he was told would kill him, and on the American Dream centre he opened in Washington.

## Overview
A career-spanning interview for the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund’s podcast, from the Watts Riots to Drexel to philanthropy. Tangen’s framing — “my guest changed how the world raises money” — sets up Milken’s own account of how an empirical anomaly became an industry.

## Implications
The investing lesson is uncomfortable and still live: systematic mispricing usually survives because it is embedded in institutional rules (reserve requirements, ratings, mandate constraints), not because nobody has seen the data. Milken’s edge was reading research everyone else had access to.
