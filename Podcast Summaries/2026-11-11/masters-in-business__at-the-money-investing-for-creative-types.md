# At the Money: Investing for Creative Types — Masters in Business


## TL;DR
In this episode of At the Money, host Barry Ritholtz interviews AJ Ayers, co-founder of Brooklyn Fi and author of Creative Money, about financial planning for artists and creative professionals with irregular incomes. Ayers argues that traditional advice fails creatives, who need to treat their income like a business, save first, plan for taxes, and use frameworks like her 'spend 1% on something awesome' and 'always be saving' rules. She also discusses pricing creative work, when to quit a day job, and using family money without shame.

## Key points
- AJ Ayers transitioned from book publishing and music editing to financial advisory after noticing a gap: young creatives making serious money from record deals or book advances need tax advice and financial planning, not just portfolio returns, and communication matters more than Wall Street's approach.
- Traditional financial advice misses the fact that income is irregular for many people, not just artists but also consultants and other professionals; without a regular paycheck, these earners must be more disciplined about savings and take money management more seriously.
- Instead of a monthly budget, creatives should 'trick yourself into thinking you have a salary': set up a checking account, pay yourself every two weeks, and save first before spending, because an irregular windfall often represents a front-loaded salary for the next several years.
- For windfalls, Ayers recommends a step-by-step framework: first spend 1% on something awesome, then do nothing for a while, then pay taxes, and only after that save and invest before buying houses or funding new creative projects; skipping tax planning is a common path to debt.
- When in doubt about taxes, save 50% of every dollar earned; if that sounds too aggressive, save at least 30%, because unexpected tax bills can derail a creative career after a big check.
- Successful artists are often shielded by business managers who give them allowances and pay all bills, which can infantilize them and leave them at age 40 never having looked at a bank account; Ayers wrote the book to show creatives they can manage their own finances.
- Pricing creative work is subjective and may not generate a living; Ayers advises thinking of a well-paying day job as a 'patron' similar to the Medici family, allowing artists to create on weekends without financial pressure.
- Creatives should invest $10,000 as soon as possible to overcome fear of the stock market; Ayers argues that using capitalism to your advantage can help you do more art and help more people.
- To quit a day job and pursue creative work full-time, Ayers recommends having at least one year of living expenses saved, but realistically most people need three to five years of runway; risk tolerance varies by individual.
- If an artist has access to family money, Ayers says to use it without shame, as the only barrier is personal shame; being open about financial support may affect how communities perceive you, but it should not prevent you from pursuing your work.
- For retirement, creatives should bring the timeline forward into their 30s and 40s rather than waiting for their 60s or 70s; after a windfall or company liquidity event, they can start a business or pursue long-held passions.
- For creatives who have avoided finances and lack family money, Ayers recommends starting with 'Always Be Saving' (ABS): even saving $1 a week builds the habit, so when income increases or a windfall arrives, they can ratchet up savings.
