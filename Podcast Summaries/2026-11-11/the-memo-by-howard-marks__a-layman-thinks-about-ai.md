# A Layman Thinks About AI — The Memo by Howard Marks


## TL;DR
Howard Marks, a self-described non-tech investor, examines how to approach investing in artificial intelligence, which he calls the most consequential and least predictable development in a generation. He outlines key uncertainties about AI technology, competition, profitability, data centers, market concentration, credit risks, and bubble potential, using Claude's predictability ratings (overall 3/10). Marks advises calibrated humility and acting despite uncertainty, focusing on which questions you can answer and how price and portfolio risk shape intelligent participation.

## Key points
- Howard Marks frames AI as the most consequential and least predictable development for investors in a generation, acknowledging his limited technical expertise but arguing investors cannot abstain from considering it.
- Investing in AI requires being at least somewhat right about technology evolution, demand for compute, competitive structure, beneficiaries, financing, and timing; with 60% probability on each of four independent variables, combined chance is 13% (0.6^4), making AI theses unusually challenging.
- He consulted Anthropic's Claude, which explained that AI systems are 'grown rather than designed': we know the training recipe (predict next token, nudge billions of parameters) but cannot fully explain internal workings, and models' self-explanations may be unreliable.
- AI capabilities have progressed from pattern recognition and extrapolation to reasoning, raising questions about values, honesty, skepticism, sycophancy, creativity, intuition, second-level thinking, and whether AGI means average, 90th percentile, or world's smartest human.
- On who profits, Howard notes transformative technology doesn't guarantee investor returns; if AI models become undifferentiated commodities, profits may flow to users rather than vendors, but chip producers Nvidia and TSMC currently capture large profits due to strong competitive positions.
- Competitive landscape: today's leaders OpenAI and Anthropic were unknown a few years ago; incumbents Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle have vast resources, but history (Napster, Yahoo, Netscape vs Microsoft, Apple, Google) shows leadership can shift rapidly in technology.
- Value investing requires estimating future unit sales, price, cost, reinvestment, and ROI; AI introduces novel uncertainties like product capabilities, pricing as commodity vs essential good, chip lifespan, depreciation, and duration of breakneck spending, making 2030 earnings for hyperscalers and frontier labs hard to project.
- Data centers are analogized to picks and shovels for gold miners, potentially insulated from picking winners, but risks include high cost of GPUs/chips, obsolescence, power and cooling bottlenecks, regulatory hurdles, community opposition, fiber connectivity, and financing constraints; construction moratoriums may increase value of existing centers.
- AI represents a small share of US economy but an outsized share of growth, driven by capex on data centers; the Magnificent Seven's earnings grew faster than rest of S&P for 14 consecutive quarters through June 30, and Alphabet and Amazon booked $151 billion in equity gains on Anthropic and SpaceX in Q2.
- JPMorgan reported 42 AI-related companies generated about two-thirds of S&P 500 price gains since January 2024, leaving the index highly concentrated and vulnerable to negative AI developments, a 'fault line' analogous to over-reliance on a single factor.
- In credit markets, AI-related debt issuance totaled nearly $600 billion globally in 2024, with $400 billion in US investment grade (over 1/5 of IG supply) and AI-related bonds now ~12% of Bloomberg USD IG corporate index; high yield exposure is ~6%.
- AI is expected to require an average of $1 trillion of new capital per year, with hundreds of billions in debt issuance; hyperscalers are rate-insensitive borrowers, potentially crowding out government debt and elevating interest rates; traditional US IPOs raised below $50 billion in 8 of last 10 years, suggesting equity capacity limits.
- Howard highlights circular transactions among chip makers, hyperscalers, frontier labs, and data center builders (money from A to B back to A), raising concerns about arms-length terms, vendor-subsidized demand, off-balance-sheet SPVs, and risk interdependence that lenders must scrutinize.
- He asked Claude to rate AI predictability on a 1-10 scale: overall AI scored 3, with direction 6-7 but profits around 2; sub-scores include regulated utilities 6-7, data centers leased long-term 5-6, hyperscalers 5, chips 4, speculative data centers 2-3, and frontier labs 2 (practically unknowable) despite premium private valuations.
- Claude noted frontier labs are close to binary, with huge capital needs, falling prices per unit of capability, and unclear moats; this least predictable sector contributed to Q2 S&P earnings gains via equity investments, raising questions about durability.
- Geopolitical risks center on China: TSMC produces nearly all leading-edge chips, US restricts sales to China, China may offer low-cost models, and future shifts could affect US competitive position and safety guardrails.
- On bubble definition, Howard defines it as irrational exuberance leading to imprudent behavior, excessive investment, and too high prices; he separates three questions: technology overhyped, spending excessive, investor prices too high. He believes AI technology is not an illusion but spending and valuation are uncertain.
- Key unresolved question: will AI provide sufficient value to users to warrant sales and prices for compute high enough to produce adequate returns on trillions invested, and for which companies? He quotes Stewart Chase: 'For those who believe, no proof is necessary. For those who don't believe, no proof is possible.'
- Investment approach: high uncertainty can be acceptable if price is low relative to intrinsic value; sectors rated lower on predictability might be better if sufficiently cheaper. Investors should practice calibrated humility, act despite uncertainty, and consider which questions they are suited to answer.
- He lists portfolio-level questions: how many things must go right for acceptable return, potential loss if assumptions wrong, which risks are compensated, optimism in price, and whether investment amplifies or diversifies existing risk. He closes with Warren Buffett's 1979 quote that uncertainty is the friend of long-term value buyers.

## Notable quotes
> "Investing consists of just one thing, positioning capital to benefit from future events, and most investors cope by relying on forecasts. But not everything is equally predictable and not all forecasters are equally skilled." — Howard Marks
> "We know what AI is made of and how it was almost perfectly. But we know surprisingly little about how it actually does what it does." — Claude
> "The future is never clear. You pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus. Uncertainty actually is the friend of the buyer of long-term values." — Warren Buffett
