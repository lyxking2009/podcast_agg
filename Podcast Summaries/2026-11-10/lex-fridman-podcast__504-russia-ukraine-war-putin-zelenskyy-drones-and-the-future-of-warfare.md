# #504 – Russia-Ukraine War: Putin, Zelenskyy, Drones and the Future of Warfare — Lex Fridman Podcast


## TL;DR
In this episode, Lex Fridman speaks with military analysts Preston Stewart and Michael Kofman about the state of the Russia-Ukraine war in early 2026. They describe an inflection point where Ukraine is performing better on the ground while Russia's offensive has stagnated, but Russia's strategic bombardment of civilian infrastructure threatens Ukraine's winter resilience. The conversation covers drone warfare, tactical innovations, the Bakhmut battle and Prigozhin mutiny, leadership dynamics, and the difficult prospects for a durable peace, including the risk that a ceasefire could become a prelude to a third war.

## Key points
- As of early 2026, the war has reached an inflection point: Ukraine is achieving its best ground combat performance since the summer 2023 offensive, while Russia is experiencing its worst year since winter 2022-2023. Russia's ground offensive has diminishing returns, but its expansive strategic bombardment campaign against Ukrainian energy and civilian infrastructure—now including schools, malls, and data centers—poses significant uncertainty for Ukraine's winter and economic viability.
- The 2023 summer offensive marked a high-profile failed Ukrainian counteroffensive using Western-trained and equipped units, lasting June-November 2023; before that, Russia's winter offensive failed except for the grinding Battle of Bakhmut. Since then, Russia held the initiative through 2024-2025 but began losing it by fall 2025, with Ukraine launching localized offensives in 2026 that have gained more terrain than the 2023 summer operation in some areas.
- Drones inflict an estimated 80% of combat losses, but the battlefield remains a combined-arms fight: artillery forces dispersion and slows assaults, minefields block mechanized breakthroughs, and drones exploit the gray zone—a 15+ km contested band between front lines where Ukrainian pickets and drone units form a zonal defense. Russian forces use infiltration tactics with individual infantry, motorcycles, and ATVs to bypass positions, suffering 90-95% casualty rates in some attacks.
- FPV drone usage is massive, with an estimated 8,000-10,000 per day per side, but they are not autonomous: each requires a 2-3 person team for piloting, navigation, and maintenance. Fiber-optic cable drones, initially deployed by Russia in Kursk and adopted by Ukraine, are immune to electronic warfare jamming and can fly up to 50 km, though they remain a small but growing percentage of total drones.
- Ukraine's innovation advantage stems from decentralized bottom-up experimentation, with units like CODE 9.2 and the First Separate Assault Regiment pioneering drone-enabled mechanized assaults and three-man fire teams; Russia's centralized top-down command culture leads to falsified territorial reports, causing Russian maps to claim positions 10-15 km ahead of reality and resulting in costly, misinformed operations.
- The Battle of Bakhmut (August 2022-May 2023) was a grinding siege where Wagner, using convict-staffed assault groups supported by Russian artillery and airborne troops, eventually took a destroyed city at enormous cost; Prigozhin claimed 20,000 Wagner KIA. Its political significance far exceeded its strategic value, leading to 'Bakhmut syndrome'—over-defending symbolic cities past the point of military sense.
- The June 2023 Prigozhin mutiny was triggered by the Russian Ministry of Defense's ultimatum that Wagner forces sign contracts, ending Prigozhin's autonomy. Wagner columns advanced from Rostov toward Moscow, shooting down Russian aircraft, but lacked elite support and never threatened regime change. Prigozhin negotiated a stand-down, but he and top Wagner commanders were killed in a mid-air plane explosion two months later; the Russian military subsequently absorbed Wagner's convict assault tactics ("Wagnerization").
- Vladimir Putin's maximalist war aims—including full control of Donbas, parts of Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia, Dnipropetrovsk, limits on Ukraine's military and sovereignty—remain far beyond Russia's current military capacity, while Zelenskyy's goals are tied to realistic military achievements. Russia's economy is spending 44% of the government budget and 9-10% of GDP on the war, with growing shadow mobilization, but still has resources to fight into 2027.
- Peace prospects are dim because Russia prefers fighting to negotiating and may wait for winter infrastructure effects or European elections; possible partial ceasefires (energy strikes, Black Sea shipping) may emerge. However, a ceasefire without security guarantees risks becoming a rearmament period, raising the danger of a third Russian-Ukrainian war, similar to the failed Minsk agreements.
- US policy under the Trump administration has been inconsistent: it gave up leverage by coercing Ukraine initially, failed to compel Russia, distracted by the Iran war, and created doubt about NATO's cohesion. The Biden administration had no plan beyond 'as long as it takes,' and European allies lacked a proactive peace plan, though the Trump push, however flawed, forced clarification of positions.
