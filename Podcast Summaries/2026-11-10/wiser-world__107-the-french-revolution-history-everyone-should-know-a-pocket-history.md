# 107. The French Revolution: History Everyone Should Know | A Pocket History — Wiser World


## TL;DR
In this Pocket History episode, host Ellie Roper explains the French Revolution's causes, key events, and legacy. France's absolute monarchy, rigid three-estate social structure, Enlightenment ideas, and financial crisis—worsened by supporting the American Revolution—led to the summoning of the Estates-General in 1789. The Third Estate formed the National Assembly, the Bastille fell, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen proclaimed equality and popular sovereignty. The revolution radicalized: King Louis XVI was overthrown and executed, the Reign of Terror killed thousands under Robespierre, and Napoleon Bonaparte ultimately seized power in 1799, ending the revolutionary period and reshaping France's political future.

## Key points
- Pre-revolution France was an absolute monarchy under King Louis XVI, with society divided into three estates: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and everyone else (Third Estate, about 97% of the population). The clergy and nobility held privileges such as tax exemptions and special legal rights, while the Third Estate—including wealthy bourgeoisie, workers, and farmers—bore heavy tax burdens and faced inequality, fueling resentment.
- Enlightenment thinkers like Montesquieu, Voltaire, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau popularized questions about legitimate government and individual rights, influencing French reformers; France's costly support for the American Revolution to weaken Britain worsened its financial crisis, and by the late 1780s the government was nearly bankrupt after years of war debt.
- Poor harvests in 1788 caused bread prices to soar, hitting ordinary families hard; royal extravagance at Versailles further angered the public, although war costs and debt—not royal spending—were the primary cause of the fiscal crisis.
- In 1789, Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General for the first time since 1614. The Third Estate, granted twice as many representatives but still outvoted by the other two orders under traditional one-vote-per-estate rules, demanded individual voting; after weeks of deadlock, it declared itself the National Assembly and swore the Tennis Court Oath to not disband until France had a constitution.
- On July 14, 1789, Paris crowds stormed the Bastille, a fortress-prison holding only seven prisoners but symbolizing royal authority and storing gunpowder; this marked the revolution spreading beyond politicians to the broader population. Meanwhile, rural uprisings known as the Great Fear saw peasants attack manor houses and destroy feudal records.
- In August 1789, the National Assembly abolished many feudal privileges of clergy and nobility and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, asserting that people are born free and equal in rights, that liberty, property, security, and freedom of expression are protected, and that sovereignty resides in the nation rather than the king.
- The revolution initially sought a constitutional monarchy; in October 1789, thousands of women, angered by bread prices and demanding political reforms, marched from Paris to Versailles and forced the royal family to move to Paris, effectively placing the king under revolutionary watch.
- The National Assembly's reforms included seizing Catholic Church lands and requiring priests to swear loyalty to the new government, causing deep religious divisions and eventually armed resistance in regions like the Vendée.
- In June 1791, King Louis XVI and his family attempted to flee Paris to join loyalist troops near the eastern border but were captured and returned, destroying trust in the monarchy and undermining the constitutional monarchy plan. In April 1792, France declared war on Austria, with Prussia joining against France; Louis supported war hoping a French defeat might restore his authority.
- In August 1792, crowds attacked the royal palace and Louis was arrested; the new National Convention abolished the monarchy and declared France a republic. Louis XVI was tried for conspiring against France, found guilty, and guillotined in January 1793; Marie Antoinette was executed later that year.
- The Reign of Terror (1793–1794) saw the revolutionary government, led increasingly by the Committee of Public Safety and Maximilien Robespierre, use the Law of Suspects and special courts to arrest and execute tens of thousands perceived as enemies of the Revolution. Former revolutionary Jacques Brissot and 20 allies were guillotined in October 1793, illustrating how the revolution turned on its own supporters.
- The Terror ended when Robespierre was arrested and executed by guillotine in July 1794. In 1795, the Directory was established, a five-man executive designed to prevent concentration of power, but it remained unstable and relied on the military, paving the way for Napoleon Bonaparte to seize power in a 1799 coup.
- Napoleon Bonaparte, a popular general, overthrew the Directory and became first consul, later first consul for life (1802) and emperor (1804). He preserved some revolutionary legal changes, such as ending inherited privileges and introducing a new civil code, but concentrated power, restricted press freedom, and gave citizens little meaningful control.
- The French Revolution did not lead directly to modern French democracy. After Napoleon's defeat in 1815, France restored the monarchy and cycled through monarchies, empires, and republics before establishing a more lasting republic in 1870 and the current Fifth Republic in 1958.
- Key lasting lessons: the revolution transformed political expectations by challenging birth-based privilege and declaring universal rights; it demonstrated that major events have multiple causes; it showed how fear can drive governments to abandon their own principles; and it revealed the difficulty of building stable institutions after overthrowing an old regime.
