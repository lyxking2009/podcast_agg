# There's a Mind-Boggling Number of Rich People in America — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal dig into pass-through entities, S-corps and the US tax code — arguing that the tax breaks routinely sold as help for 'small business' have in fact built a broad, under-discussed layer of very wealthy Americans who are neither Fortune 500 chiefs nor tech billionaires.

## Key points

- The episode's premise is that the inequality conversation over-indexes on the 1%/0.1% and on poverty, while missing the fat middle layer of genuinely rich people who are not on the Forbes 400.
- Weisenthal's field observation from Southern California — Orange County and Huntington Beach, boats and private wine lockers at steakhouses — is used as evidence that this layer is visible and large.
- Tracy Alloway frames the mechanism: since the 1980s, and most recently under the current administration, the big tax cuts have gone to S-corps and pass-through entities, which are politically packaged as small businesses.
- In reality many of these entities are phenomenally large and very wealthy — the legal form, not the size, is what earns the treatment.
- The hosts connect this directly to private markets: when Odd Lots discusses private credit, private equity and private markets, it is often discussing exactly these business structures.
- The 'small business' label survives because it is politically and aesthetically appealing — Tocqueville's nation of entrepreneurs is a durable American self-image.
- The closing exchange argues that salaried W-2 workers are the only class with no obvious route to minimising tax, since every other structure offers one.
- Erica Nguyen's book 'The Everywhere Millionaire' gets a plug during the outro; the episode was recorded on September 9.
