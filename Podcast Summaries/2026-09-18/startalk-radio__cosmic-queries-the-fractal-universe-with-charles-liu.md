# Cosmic Queries – The Fractal Universe with Charles Liu — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR

Neil deGrasse Tyson and co-hosts Chuck Nice and Gary O'Reilly welcome back 'Geek-in-Chief' Charles Liu for a grab-bag Cosmic Queries episode spanning deep space travel, fractal mathematics, multiverse theory, and the boundary of what science may never know.

## Key points

- The episode opens on deep space travel framed as fundamentally an energy problem — including whether something like Iron Man's arc reactor would even solve it.
- Discussion covers generational spaceships, robotic exploration, and a daisy-chain network of probes relaying signals back to Earth.
- That leads into the Fermi Paradox: if infinite energy makes interstellar travel feasible, why is there no evidence of civilisations that already worked it out — hiding their tracks, following a Prime Directive, or exploring somewhere we are not looking?
- The centrepiece is the resemblance between the cosmic web and the brain's neural network — Liu's point is that both are fractal without being the same fractal, which speaks to the universality of mathematics.
- The conversation asks whether there is something fundamental about complexity emerging from simple rules in nature, and whether fractal commonality could offer a bridge between relativity and quantum mechanics.
- They examine whether fractals being common in nature supports simulation theory, and whether the multiverse is a product of apophenia — humans seeing patterns where none exist.
- Liu unpacks the many-worlds interpretation, arguing the equations are open-ended rather than pattern-suggestive, and describes the Planck length as the ultimate perimeter of scientific ignorance.
- Rapid-fire queries include how fast a pulsar can spin before the laws of physics intervene, whether aliens could have seeded life across an expanding universe, and how we know galaxies are embedded in stretching spacetime rather than merely moving through it.
- The episode closes on the idea that civilisations born too late in cosmic history might develop a fundamentally wrong picture of the universe.
