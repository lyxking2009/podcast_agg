# Joseph Moore: How to Get Rich in American History: 300 Years of Financial Advice That Worked (& Didn't) | #639

**Show:** The Meb Faber Show - Better Investing

**Guest(s):** Joseph Moore (historian, author, investor), Meb Faber (host)

## Key Points

- Joseph Moore studied 300 years of American financial advice and tested the strategies himself, uncovering surprising patterns
- Compound interest only recently became powerful — people didn't live long enough historically to benefit from it
- Stocks didn't reliably beat bonds until after WWII, contradicting the common belief that equities always win long-term
- Real estate stayed flat for a century in most cities — the idea that property always goes up is a modern myth
- Crypto mirrors the 10,000+ self-issued currencies before the Civil War — 'all self-issued currencies eventually go to zero'
- The 'latte factor' advice dates to the 1800s — personal finance wisdom recycles old ideas in new packaging
- Upward mobility data shows that today, 60% escape the bottom income quintile, and 10% go all the way to the top — more economic mobility than ever before
- Women were unsung financial heroes throughout American history — 'Capitalism is a team sport. Marriage is a superpower.'
- CFPB studies found that a positive attitude plus saving habit outpredicted income and inheritance for financial wellness
- The FIRE movement is the 'CrossFit of personal finance' — financially independent people only thrived when they found meaningful work
- Generational wealth doesn't last — 90% of top 1%'s grandchildren are not wealthy. 'Tutors outperform trust funds.'
- Human capital is 30x the value of the stock market — investing in yourself matters more than picking stocks

## Overview

Joseph Moore, a historian who spent a decade studying 300 years of American financial advice, joins Meb Faber to share the patterns he discovered. The conversation debunks common financial myths: compound interest is historically recent, stocks didn't beat bonds until after WWII, real estate was flat for a century, and crypto mirrors pre-Civil War self-issued currencies. Moore emphasizes that human capital (education, skills, marriage) is 30x more valuable than stock market wealth, and that generational wealth almost never lasts.

## Implications

The most important 'investment' isn't in stocks or real estate — it's in human capital, relationships, and habits. The historical perspective shows that most financial advice recycles old ideas, and that the fundamentals of wealth building haven't changed in 300 years: save consistently, invest in yourself, and maintain a positive outlook.
