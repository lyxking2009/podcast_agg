# At The Money: Considering a Career Change? How About FINFLUENCER? — Masters in Business

## TL;DR

Bloomberg's At The Money turns to the practical question of whether financial advice is a viable second career, with Barry Ritholtz interviewing Tyler Gardner — a former economics teacher who pivoted to financial advising and then to short-form financial media, building a following of more than six million. Gardner's story is a case study in why the profession is misunderstood: clients mostly don't want to be taught, and audience-building is a multi-year grind rather than a viral event.

## Key points

- Gardner's pivot began in education, not finance: he was teaching at a Connecticut school when a TIAA-CREF representative visited and not one of roughly 110 highly educated faculty members understood basic terms like expense ratio or target-date fund. He decided the real gap was financial education, and moved into advising and portfolio management to fill it.
- He joined a small Vermont RIA specifically because it seemed to value teaching — and was surprised to find that most high-net-worth clients did not want to be educated about their wealth. They wanted the convenience of handing over assets; 'don't call me, I'll call you' was the dominant client posture.
- The social media experiment started inside the RIA as a marketing effort, initially mis-positioned: videos went on the firm's own website and occasionally LinkedIn and went nowhere. The shift came when the firm began distributing to TikTok, Instagram and Facebook, where the distribution was free and reach was disproportionate.
- Gardner's advice to would-be finfluencers is explicitly anti-hype: he describes the work as four years of daily grinding rather than an overnight blow-up, and compares quitting after two weeks to abandoning a New Year's gym resolution when the followers don't hit a million immediately.
- He argues that social platforms offer a currently free distribution channel for professional expertise, and that businesses not experimenting with it are leaving an opportunity that may not exist in a decade — while flagging that the volume/creator business model (books, courses, brand sponsorships) is what turns audience into economics.
- Ritholtz frames the episode around a broader theme he returns to often: the complexity and jargon of finance is often defended as a feature, because opacity makes consumers pay for expertise. Gardner's counter-position is that education and transparency can themselves be the product.
- The show closes with a career-change checklist: if a job offers no challenge, agency or enthusiasm, and the person is creative and a good communicator, adding 'social media influencer' to the repertoire can become a new career rather than a side project.
