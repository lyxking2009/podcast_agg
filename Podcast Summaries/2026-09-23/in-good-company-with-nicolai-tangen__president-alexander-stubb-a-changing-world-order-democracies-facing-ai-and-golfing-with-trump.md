# President Alexander Stubb: A Changing World Order, Democracies Facing AI, and Golfing with Trump — In Good Company with Nicolai Tangen

## TL;DR
Finland’s president tells Nicolai Tangen that the old world order is ending and the Global South — neither the West nor the East — will decide what replaces it. The conversation ranges across AI’s threat to slow-moving democracies, defence and the war in Ukraine, UN reform, and why Finland has been the world’s happiest country nine years running.

## Key points
- Stubb’s thesis: the world order is changing the way it did in 1918, 1945 and 1989, and the deciders this time are the Global South. The choice is between multilateral norms, institutions and cooperation, or multipolar transactional chaos and conflict.
- “The end of western dominance is over” — but he does not think the East will dominate either; instead a set of new players emerges. His interest, as a leader of a small country, is a reformed UN-based multilateral system with genuine agency for the South.
- Concrete UN reform proposal: the Security Council needs at least one seat from Latin America, two from Africa and one more from Asia, to move beyond the five veto-wielding permanent members.
- On China in the Global South: the Chinese “have played the long strategic game much better than we Europeans.” He cites the cliché of a Western leader landing at a Chinese-built airport, driving a Chinese-built highway and then holding a press conference in a Chinese-built hotel to say Africans should not cooperate with China. His answer is a more “dignified” policy: humbler, less finger-wagging, more concrete cooperation.
- What the West got wrong: “innate arrogance” — the assumption that history ended, the West would dominate and everyone would follow. He argues policymakers are “prisoners of our own identity.”
- On the power blocs: he would rename his book’s framework the “rectangle of power,” because the US may no longer want to lead the Western bloc. AI is the force that changes work and the economy, politics and communication, war and science — and access to it runs through compute and electricity.
- Democracies are adapting to technology “badly.” Democracy was designed in the 17th and 18th centuries to be slow and cumbersome; AI demands constant reaction. The original working title of his book was “Digital Democracy or Digital Dictatorship.”
- Big tech versus states: he asks whether Anthropic or Facebook is more powerful than Norway or Finland, noting that some companies’ market capitalisations now exceed many countries’.
- AI as equaliser or amplifier of injustice: both paths are open. The biology-technolog﻿y mix — genome sequencing and manipulation — could create different kinds of human beings, and he raises Harari’s “useless class” scenario. He is critical of big-tech leaders’ limited grasp of societal impact, naming Demis Hassabis as a notable exception.
- Regulation will always trail technology by one to three years, so he favours dedicated control bodies — starting with the US, then Europe and China, and ultimately a UN-level body. He thinks US-China cooperation is feasible “out of necessity,” given the balance of power in critical minerals, compute and chips.
- His foreign-policy doctrine is “values-based realism”: hold to democracy, rule of law and human rights, but accept that not everyone shares them and that global problems require compromise. Diplomacy seeks win-win; business is often zero-sum — the world will not work on transactions alone.
- Three scenarios for the new order: (1) continued chaotic transactional conflict with unholy ad hoc alliances; (2) a multipolar world with iffy norms and pure national sovereignty; (3) regional integration — EU, ASEAN, Mercosur, African Union — feeding into a reformed UN, which is his ideal.
- On Finland’s nine-year run as the happiest country: basics met Maslow-style, a deep social commitment to equality and justice, a strong welfare state alongside an open economy, education and reading as sources of meaning, and a close attachment to nature. He notes Finns are happy but “don’t walk around the streets and smile all the time.”
- The biggest threat to that model is polarisation and inequality. He calls the US “probably the most polarized liberal democracy in the world right now,” driven by identity politics and the broken promise of globalisation, and argues for equal opportunity while accepting no society is a utopia.
- On Finland’s split executive: the president leads foreign policy and the military but has no constitutional domestic role — yet as a “value leader” the office carries a megaphone on values, ethics and well-being, which he says he must use without meddling in budgets or resource allocation.
