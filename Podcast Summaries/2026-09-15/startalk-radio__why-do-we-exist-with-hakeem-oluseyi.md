# Why Do We Exist with Hakeem Oluseyi — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR
Neil deGrasse Tyson and comic co-host Chuck Nice welcome astrophysicist Hakeem Oluseyi back to StarTalk to unpack his new book, Why Do We Exist: The Nine Realms of the Universe That Make You Possible. The episode ranges across plasma physics, emergence and abiogenesis, the superhorizon and the inflation field, and whether there is something more fundamental than fields and geometry. Oluseyi introduces the Oluseyi Equation, his differently-motivated take on the Drake Equation, and the conversation closes on Earth's liquid metal outer core, JWST's ‘little red dots’ and what an indestructible bowling ball on a pulsar would do. ## Key points
- Oluseyi's book builds a cosmological framework treating the universe as a single unfolding story across ‘nine realms of the universe that make you possible’ — the episode walks through that ladder from fundamental physics to brains.
- What is a plasma? The episode breaks down plasma physics and how magnetism is used to contain it, with fusion reactor plasma as the practical case.
- Emergence and abiogenesis: how organisation arises from simple rules, from chordate filter feeders and the origin of vertebrates to the ‘energy ladder’ that leads to a brain like ours.
- The superhorizon and the inflation field mark the edge of what we can observe — Oluseyi discusses what may lie beneath energy and spacetime, and whether something more fundamental than fields and geometry exists.
- The Oluseyi Equation is his own take on the Drake Equation, differently motivated and built around a broader hypothesis about interstellar species that he chose to publish rather than keep to himself.
- Thought experiment: could we move the Earth to another star system instead of building ships to carry humanity there?
- Relativistic exotica: what two astronauts at opposite ends of a rapidly spinning millisecond pulsar would actually see when looking at each other.
- Black hole physics: could the earliest black holes leave a topological remnant — a kind of Cheshire cat smile in spacetime — after they evaporate, and which came first, the supermassive black hole or the galaxy around it?
- Closing segment covers Earth's liquid metal outer core and its magnetic field, the little red dots JWST found at the edge of the observable universe, and the behaviour of an indestructible bowling ball on a pulsar.
- Oluseyi's day job context appears via his role at the Astronomical Society of the Pacific and work on NASA's IMAP satellite, which studies the boundary of the heliosphere.
