# Box's Aaron Levie: On Reinventing Yourself in the AI Age and Enterprise Diffusion — Training Data

## TL;DR
Aaron Levie, co-founder and CEO of Box, argues that the application layer — not the frontier labs — captures the bulk of AI value in the enterprise, because the gap between a model's capability and an actual business workflow is vast and requires domain expertise, systems-of-record access, human-in-the-loop steps and change management. Box, twenty years old and sitting on hundreds of billions of files, has rebuilt itself around agents that read, extract and act on unstructured corporate content. The conversation ranges over agentic harnesses, open-weight adoption, memory and continual learning, ‘work slop’, evals, token subsidisation, and why model providers face an unavoidable strategic tension between owning the customer and keeping a trusted ecosystem.

## Key points
- Levie's core thesis: the bridge between model capability and enterprise workflow is vast, not narrow — roughly a trillion dollars is effectively bet on which of those two outcomes is true. He is long the application tier, and says plainly he is biased, with a concentrated bet and limited diversification.
- The infrastructure/application analogy: AWS, GCP and Azure created trillions in infrastructure value, but also trillions in software value that no one predicted — Snowflake and Databricks did not look inevitable from early cloud primitives. Intelligence will follow the same pattern.
- Why applied companies are the hottest ‘Neolabs’: open source makes it viable to wrap models, and the depth of enterprise workflows means applied players accumulate data and domain expertise that becomes its own flywheel — at which point doing your own model becomes attractive. Box Labs exists but is still largely under wraps; near-term focus is making the agent excellent on any model.
- The ‘fox guarding the hen house’ argument is possibly the biggest single reason the application layer must exist: a buyer wants tasks cost-optimised across 10 models with accuracy held constant, so definitionally they want a vendor with no model preference.
- The countervailing force is model-provider subsidisation of tokens. Levie expects that to be temporary — once these companies are public they face the same laws of capitalism, and at tens of billions in capex they must also pay for training runs.
- Non-economic actors change the equilibrium: Meta, SpaceX, China and even Nvidia do not need the same inference margin structure as Anthropic or OpenAI. Levie suggests some could tolerate 10% margins to pay for massive compute clusters, which pushes cost per token down on a like-for-like basis and accrues more value to applications.
- He doubts one or two labs capture 95% of value creation; a more dynamic environment is likely, and he argues the largest labs should prefer that outcome too — at some point being the only thing that exists as intelligence invites nationalisation, so a little healthy competition is in their interest.
- Software history as precedent: the law firm, the pharma company and the bank all need something that bridges core technology to their workflow and business process. AI has not changed the need or the shape of that requirement.
- Chatbot versus agentic workflow is the key demarcation: a chatbot can be universal and horizontal, but a workflow needs something that pings at the right point in the process or reaches data the chatbot cannot natively access — which requires setup work and domain expertise inside the organisation.
- Diffusion is the underappreciated market: unless you underwrite the big labs as literally 100,000-employee organisations, every company — from a 50-person firm to a multi-thousand-person enterprise — needs an army of people to help with transformation and change management. That is a very large services and software opportunity.
- Levie's advice to students and early-career people: get wired into the right information flow, because being a year ahead or behind is largely a function of your feed — he still meets twenty-year-olds who rely on articles arriving by luck rather than a deliberate pipeline.
- Box's origin: a simple idea — securely store and share data in the cloud — that struck a nerve as enterprises moved from on-premises systems to the cloud and needed a better way to store, share, collaborate on and manage unstructured data (corporate documents, financial documents, marketing assets, research materials).
- The tension Levie expects model providers to wrestle with over the coming years: getting closer to the customer versus maintaining an ecosystem that people trust. He notes the ‘existential dread’ of a VC choosing between another billion into Anthropic and finding out what the applied layer does.
- On pricing: API revenue is the high-margin line subsidising the applied product, but if all customers move to the applied product there is no API revenue left — so an equilibrium has to be struck.
