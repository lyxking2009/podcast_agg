# An 86-minute guide to finding PMF | Rob Snyder (Author, The Power of Pull) — Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth

## TL;DR
Rob Snyder, author of The Power of Pull, explains his 'pull' framework for finding product-market fit: a person who would be 'weird not to buy' your product. He details how to discover pull through hands-on customer research, test hypotheses via sales calls, and avoid common traps like design partnerships. The episode covers idea generation, cold outreach, sales call structure, pricing, retention, and a 'case study factory' mental model for scaling.

## Key points
- Rob Snyder developed the 'pull' framework after his own startup struggled for two years; he has since helped ~50 startups go from zero to over $1M ARR, and wrote The Power of Pull. Pull describes a person who would be 'weird not to buy' your product because they have an urgent project they cannot complete with existing options.
- The PULL acronym stands for Project (a job-to-be-done on their to-do list), Unavoidable (must be done now), List of options considered, and Limitations (why those options aren't good enough). All four elements must be present simultaneously for genuine pull; missing any one leads to no purchase.
- Example: Jump, an AI notetaker for financial advisors, found pull because advisors have an unavoidable project (minimizing note-taking time for CRM compliance due to SEC jail risk), and generic AI notetakers lack CRM integration and compliance, so they ripped the product out of their hands.
- For idea generation, founders should not brainstorm or rely on whiteboarding; they should get hands-on, shadowing potential customers and feeling the pain firsthand. Example: a friend shadowed independent physical therapists, worked alongside them, and identified a cost-cutting project with urgent need.
- Create a 'pull hypothesis' using a two-slide pitch: first slide describes the person's project, why it's unavoidable, their options, and limitations; second slide shows your conceptual solution. Show it to prospects and look for them trying to buy ('How do we get started?') versus lukewarm responses.
- Cold outreach should use 'founder magic': a short, interesting personal fact to get a meeting, not a pitch. Example: 'Snoop Dogg invested in us.' Benchmarks: aim for 30-40% LinkedIn connection acceptance and 30-50% reply rate.
- Sales calls should start by asking why they took the call, then agree on an agenda, and ask discovery questions to surface the pull. If no pull emerges, ask the 'Hail Mary' question: 'Is there anything on your to-do list that you would pay me to do right now?' This can lead to pivots; one startup pivoted from ATS to job description software and took off.
- Avoid design partners because they don't have pull; they give unhelpful feedback and rarely buy. Instead, sell directly, even if the product is service-shaped at first; you can automate later.
- For pricing, use the pull framework to set bounds: the value of the project or cost of current alternatives. Present pricing via a 'case study' approach: describe how others priced similarly and ask how they think about it, rather than stating a price.
- After first sales calls, analyze whether pull existed. If not, iterate on the hypothesis, possibly entering 'goblin mode' (extreme measures like flying to a conference or going door-to-door) to find real pull. Most startups need several iterations.
- Retention requires identifying a leading indicator: for Slack it was sending a certain number of messages; for Rob's restaurant retention startup it was receiving a preventable quit notification within two weeks. Design onboarding to hit that moment so churn becomes 'weird'.
- If many customers churn, analyze which ones stay and what they have in common; pivot to that niche. Example: a horizontal AI workflows product pivoted to freight forwarders after noticing they used one specific workflow heavily.
- Use the 'case study factory' mental model: your startup's job is to repeat one successful case study infinitely. Map pipeline, sales, and delivery as stages; use theory of constraints to find and fix the bottleneck.
- This approach applies to larger companies launching new products: founders or product leaders should sell before building, using the same pull hypothesis and sales call methods. Ideas that come from sales calls asking for additional features tend to work better than whiteboarded ideas.
- Avoid tarpit ideas: going after a market with a clear winner without a differentiated unblocking for a specific customer segment. Example: a 100x better printer won't sell if the person isn't blocked.

## Notable quotes
> "One of the main mistakes people make is thinking that pull is a thing that a company has. It's a thing a person has. You're not selling to SpaceX. You're selling to the head of legal at SpaceX who has a project on her to-do list that they can't do themselves." — Rob Snyder
> "Most people most of the time are like fine. It would be weird if they bought our product given what's on their to-do list, given what they have, given their options, like so it'd be weird if they bought. But a person with pull is weird not to buy." — Rob Snyder
> "We're seeing if they try to buy. If they don't, cool. We've learned something. We can kind of keep iterating from there. If they do, okay, great. Now we might have something. Let's see if it repeats." — Rob Snyder
> "The theory behind a sales call is if you have pull, you're going to rip the thing out of my hands. So, I have a very specific structure of a sales call for getting into discovery and then transitioning out of discovery." — Rob Snyder
