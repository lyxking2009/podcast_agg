# What Chess.com Teaches US About Superhuman Capabilities, with CEO Erik Allebest — No Priors: Artificial Intelligence | Technology | Startups

## TL;DR
Chess.com has grown to 10 million daily active users, 250 million registered members, and over $200 million in annual revenue, driven by waves from COVID, The Queen's Gambit, and school adoption. CEO Eric Allebest explains how the platform bootstrapped for years, later taking private equity investment from General Atlantic and CVC, and how AI has made chess more exciting and accessible. The company is expanding into poker and other classic games with skill-based ratings, while investing in AI-powered coaching and anti-cheating tools.

## Key points
- Chess.com has over 10 million daily active users and 40-50 million monthly active users, with 250 million registered members. The company generates over $200 million in annual revenue and employs 650 people in a fully remote setup.
- Eric Allebest bought the chess.com domain from a bankruptcy auction in 2005 for $56,000. He and a friend bootstrapped the company, launching in 2007 and becoming profitable within 18 months. Early venture capitalists dismissed it as uninvestable.
- Growth accelerated in 2020 due to COVID-19 and The Queen's Gambit, then again in 2023 from school adoption, short-form content, the Mittens bot, and cheating scandal attention. The user base stabilized at a higher baseline after each wave.
- Private equity involvement began with General Atlantic in 2020-2021 buying secondary shares. In a recent transaction, CVC came in to buy out some shareholders while General Atlantic reinvested. No new capital was needed for operations, but investors brought operational expertise.
- Chess's cultural relevance has shifted from nerdy to mainstream. Celebrities like Lewis Hamilton play, fashion brands like Louis Vuitton feature chess, and schools have chess programs. Chess.com redefined what it means to be a chess player, celebrating all ratings.
- Competitors like FICS, ICC, and Yahoo Chess faded due to Chess.com's focus on a superior free, browser-based user experience. Lichess remains an open-source alternative, but the two coexist. Chess is not patentable, so product and community determine success.
- AI has transformed chess. Early engines like Stockfish made play boring as players mimicked perfect computer moves, but neural net engines like Leela Chess Zero introduced creative, aggressive play that made chess exciting again. AI tools now provide game review, puzzles, and coaching.
- Humans want to compete against humans, not machines. Even though computers have been superhuman at chess for 30 years, chess popularity has grown. Machines push humans to improve, and AI can accelerate learning through personalized tools.
- Skill acquisition in chess is fundamentally about repetition: doing puzzles, reviewing mistakes, and practicing openings. There are no shortcuts, but AI tools can lower barriers and provide on-demand coaching.
- Chess.com invests heavily in anti-cheating using statistical models, machine learning, and player behavior data to distinguish human from computer play. Cheating cannot be prevented entirely, but they ban and educate offenders to protect game integrity.
- The company launched Gambit, a poker platform, and is bringing the chess rating playbook to poker, measuring skill independent of money or chip buying. They plan to roll out other classic games with the same approach of pure, skill-based ratings.
- AI is used across Chess.com's operations: support automation, data analysis, internal knowledge systems, martech, and software development to shorten release cycles. Product-facing AI includes personalized game analysis and a conversational chess coach.
- Allebest advises founders to follow their vision and not over-index on market size or funding playbooks. He bootstrapped against conventional wisdom and focused on building a product he wanted to use.
- On AGI, Allebest believes superhuman intelligence is coming and will require guardrails. He is optimistic about its potential to solve global problems but worried about power concentration. He notes that chess's growth demonstrates continued demand for human skill despite machine superiority.
