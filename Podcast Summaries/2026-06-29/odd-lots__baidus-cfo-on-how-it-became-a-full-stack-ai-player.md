## Key Points

- Baidu is a full-stack AI company spanning chips (Kunlun), cloud, foundation models (Ernie), and applications — Henry Hoo identifies cloud as the single most critical layer because it can host both Baidu's own models and third-party models, and it connects inference hardware to real-world monetization.
- The AI industry has shifted from infrastructure and training toward inference and agents: roughly 80% of incremental token demand today is inference-related, not training.
- Baidu's measure of AI success has moved from DAU (daily active users) to DAA (daily active agents), reflecting a shift from AI as a tool to AI as an autonomous task-completer.
- Baidu resolved its "impossible triangle" — growth, profitability, and capital discipline — in a recent quarter: operating profit nearly doubled QoQ, cloud revenue grew ~79% YoY (roughly double China's market rate), and operating cash flow turned positive since Q3 last year.
- Apollo Go (Baidu's robotaxi unit) delivered ~350,000 trips across 27 cities in a recent quarter, roughly 25% behind Waymo's ~500,000 weekly trips, and is expanding to London in partnership with Uber and Lyft.
- Baidu's Ernie 5.1 is ranked #1 globally for text in the Chatbot Arena leaderboard and #5 globally for search-skill capabilities, and Baidu became a search-skill provider for the Open Claw (open-source Claude alternative) ecosystem after its founder Peter reached out.
- Baidu is proceeding with a confidential Hong Kong IPO filing to spin off its Kunlun chip business, aiming to give the chip unit a more neutral, independent identity that can attract a broader customer and developer ecosystem.
- The revenue model for agents is shifting toward outcome-based payment: Baidu's enterprise agent deployed at a major Chinese port shares in the port's profit improvement, and its "digital employees" product charges based on e-commerce sales results generated by AI live-streamers.

## Overview

Recorded live at the Bloomberg Asia Invest Conference in Hong Kong, this episode features Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway interviewing Henry Hoo, CFO of Baidu. The conversation opens with the hosts framing the current moment as the first real head-to-head clash between Chinese and American internet giants — not in separate markets but on the same turf, particularly in AI and autonomous vehicles. Hoo argues that Baidu's full-stack position (chips, cloud, models, applications) is its defining competitive advantage, and that if forced to pick one layer to win, he would choose cloud — because cloud is the platform that integrates everything else and enables monetization.

A large portion of the discussion centers on how Baidu thinks about capital allocation and productivity in an era of rapid AI cost deflation. Hoo describes a "token budget" philosophy that favors openness and speed over rigid per-employee limits, noting that unit costs can drop by half in a matter of weeks. He is candid about the "impossible triangle" tension between aggressive AI investment, shareholder returns, and profitability, but points to recent quarterly results — nearly doubled operating profit, 79% cloud revenue growth, and positive operating cash flow — as evidence that Baidu is threading that needle better than peers.

The conversation also covers AI safety and China's regulatory environment. Hoo frames AI alignment as an engineering problem rather than a theoretical one, arguing that China's tech ecosystem has cost-efficient talent, data-labeling infrastructure, and post-training capabilities that make safety work tractable. On the regulatory side, he describes the policy environment as transparent and supportive, with policymakers drawing on two decades of cloud-era data governance experience. The episode closes with discussion of Apollo Go's robotaxi ambitions (London launch, Uber/Lyft partnerships), the spin-off of Kunlun chips, and Baidu's "digital employee" products — AI live-streamers that sell goods on behalf of e-commerce merchants around the clock.

## Implications

Baidu's trajectory suggests that the competitive frontier in AI is shifting decisively from model benchmarks to system-level integration — whoever can close the loop from data to chips to inference to agent monetization at scale will set the terms of competition, and Baidu is positioning itself as the Chinese company with all those pieces in place. The robotaxi head-to-head between Apollo Go and Waymo in London will be the first real-world test of whether Chinese and American AI giants can compete for the same consumers in the same city, making it a bellwether for broader AI geopolitical competition. The move toward outcome-based agent pricing (revenue-sharing with ports, performance fees from e-commerce merchants) points to a new SaaS-like model for enterprise AI that could redefine how AI companies are valued — less by token consumption and more by measurable business outcomes delivered.
