## Key Points
- Scaramucci advocates paying yourself first by investing consistently each month and ignoring short-term market fluctuations — a compounding habit he credits as foundational to building wealth.
- He missed out on significant Amazon gains, paying roughly $14 million for a position that never materialized as hoped; he uses this as a lesson in not letting ego override research.
- Scaramucci evolved from a crypto skeptic to an institutional blockchain believer, now arguing that 90% of investors who do serious homework on Bitcoin will choose to own it.
- He is a committed long-term holder of SpaceX and argues investors should not bet against Elon Musk, citing the compounding effect of owning transformational private companies.
- Fear of deflation should outweigh fear of inflation over the long run — innovation systematically drives prices down, and investors who confuse the two misallocate capital.
- Radical self-awareness is the most underrated quality in investing and in life: knowing what you don't know prevents catastrophic overconfidence.
- Entrepreneurs and investors must expect failure; the differentiator is refusing to play victim and instead learning to iterate after setbacks.
- His forthcoming book *All the Wrong Moves* (Penguin Books) documents both personal mistakes and broader political and financial miscalculations over three decades.

## Overview
Anthony Scaramucci joined Wilfred Frost for a wide-ranging conversation spanning his 38-year career in finance, from his early days on Wall Street through founding SkyBridge Capital and his brief, high-profile stint as White House Communications Director. Recorded June 23, 2026, the episode is framed around the twin themes of wealth creation and resilience after failure. Scaramucci is candid about costly mistakes — including a missed Amazon opportunity that cost his investors roughly $14 million in foregone gains — and uses these episodes to illustrate how overconfidence and short-termism sabotage even experienced investors.

The conversation moves through several investing lenses. On digital assets, Scaramucci recounts his skepticism toward crypto giving way to conviction as he studied the underlying blockchain technology and watched institutional adoption accelerate. He now believes Bitcoin is systematically under-owned and that the majority of rigorous investors who study the asset will conclude they need an allocation. On private markets, he flags SpaceX as the clearest example of a transformational company that rewards patient, long-horizon holders — the kind of conviction-driven position that public-market investors rarely maintain long enough to capture full upside. Throughout, his macro thesis is that innovation is inherently deflationary, so investors should fear a low-growth, low-innovation environment far more than conventional inflation.

The episode's second major arc is about character and leadership. Scaramucci draws on his White House experience and his own public failures to argue that servant leadership — putting the mission and the team ahead of ego — is the most durable leadership model. He emphasizes "radical self-awareness" as the quality that separates investors and executives who grow from failure from those who repeat it. The discussion closes with a note on the UK, where he pushes back on what he sees as excessive national self-criticism, arguing that London and British institutions remain globally competitive despite the prevailing pessimism.

## Implications
Scaramucci's conversion from crypto skeptic to institutional Bitcoin advocate is notable given his credibility as a traditional alternatives manager, and his 90%-conviction claim on research-driven Bitcoin adoption is a data point in the ongoing debate about when institutional allocation to digital assets becomes consensus. His deflationary macro framing — that innovation makes long-run inflation fears overblown — cuts against a persistent narrative in markets and has practical implications for how investors position duration and real assets. The servant leadership and anti-victimhood messages reflect a broader cultural moment in which public figures with high-profile failures are reassessing how to narrate setbacks constructively.
