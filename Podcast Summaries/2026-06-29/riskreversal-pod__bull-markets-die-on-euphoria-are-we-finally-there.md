## Overview

Dan Nathan and Guy Adami open with a pointed question implied by the episode title: after years of the bull market adage "bull markets die on euphoria," have markets finally reached that terminal stage? The exhibit A is Micron Technology, which just delivered a historic earnings report — $41.5 billion in revenue versus $35.9 billion expected, gross margins of nearly 85%, and guidance of $50 billion for the next quarter — fueling a run to record highs. The hosts dig into Micron's new Strategic Customer Agreements with hyperscalers, take-or-pay contracts covering 2026–2030 that include $22 billion in deposits and letters of credit and peg prices to floors well above Micron's best historical margins. Bulls argue this transforms memory from a commodity cyclical into a durable secular growth story; bears point to the risk of Chinese competitors, recession, and the same complacency that comes with long-term contracts masking future inventory buildups. The central tension: at current valuations, who's actually left to buy?

The episode then pivots to the collateral damage of the AI-driven bull: Microsoft, the Nasdaq's largest weight for much of the past decade, has shed over 20% in June alone — its worst monthly performance since December 2000 — as the market has stopped valuing it on earnings and instead punishes its $190 billion capex projection that consumes nearly 100% of operating cash flow. Palantir, once a market darling, has been cut in half. Nathan and Adami frame this divergence — semiconductors ripping higher while hyperscalers and high-multiple software names crater — as either a healthy rotation acknowledging different AI winners and losers, or the early warning signs of broader distribution before a larger selloff.

The second half covers the week's other major flashpoints. SpaceX's massive $25 billion bond offering, raised within two weeks of the company's historic IPO, is trading badly in the secondary market with paper losses above $300 million, underscoring how quickly retail enthusiasm has cooled after SpaceX's stock declined sharply from its opening price. OpenAI's rumored IPO delay to 2027 is discussed as both a market signal and a strategic choice. On macro, new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh held rates steady at his first FOMC meeting (at 3.50–3.75%) but the dot plot shifted hawkish, with officials hinting at potential rate hikes later in 2026 tied to Iran-war inflation; the hosts flag the upcoming jobs report as the next key inflection. Crude oil at roughly $70/barrel is discussed as shrugging off very real geopolitical risk — US-Iran peace talks in Doha, a supertanker struck near the Strait of Hormuz — suggesting markets may be underpricing the tail risk. Bitcoin below $60K rounds out the picture of a market repricing risk assets away from speculative crypto and toward AI infrastructure. Nike's earnings the following day serve as a check on the consumer and a litmus test for whether the AI boom is masking a weaker economy beneath the surface.

## Implications

The episode captures a market at a genuine inflection point: euphoria-level valuations in AI memory and semiconductors are coexisting with dramatic underperformance in hyperscalers, software, crypto, and consumer names — a breadth divergence that historically resolves badly. The hawkish turn from Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, the SpaceX and OpenAI IPO turbulence, and Bitcoin's slide below $60K collectively suggest liquidity is tightening at the edges of the risk spectrum even as headline indices hold up. If Micron's blowout print proves to be a "sell the news" peak for the semiconductor trade rather than the start of a secular rerating, the lack of new buyers at current levels could accelerate a broader correction.
