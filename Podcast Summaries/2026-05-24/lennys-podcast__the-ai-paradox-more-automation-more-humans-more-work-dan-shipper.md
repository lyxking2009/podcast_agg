## Key Points

- **The AI paradox**: The more Every (Dan's company) automates, the more human work there is to do — they're nearly 30 people and haven't replaced anyone with agents.
- **Two modes of agent work**: (1) Agent employees — coworker agents (tagged in Slack) and embedded agents in workflows; (2) Human-agent collaboration tools like Claude Code and Codex where humans and AI work simultaneously.
- **AI commoditizes residue of expertise**: Models train on the "exhaust" of successfully completed tasks, making previously rare skills (coding, writing, thumbnail design) broadly available — but this collapses default output value toward slop.
- **Slop → demand for difference**: When everyone produces similar AI output, differentiated work commands a premium. Demand for difference is demand for human experts.
- **Benchmarks measure frames, not framers**: Benchmarks freeze problems into static frames. When models saturate a frame, changing the frame zeros them out again. The fractal nature of benchmark saturation mirrors the paradox in chart form.
- **Every agent needs a human**: The further agents get from active human oversight, the worse they perform. Personal agents at Every were abandoned when employees stopped maintaining them.
- **Second-order effect**: When AI makes a task cheap, more people attempt it, producing an abundance of mediocre output that creates demand for experts to review, refine, and decide.
- **AGI definition**: AGI arrives when it makes economic sense to keep an agent running continuously 24/7 — we're not there yet.
- **Frames vs. framers**: AI saturates the frame but not the framer. Humans know what *needs* to be done right now; models only know what *has* been done.
- **Agency vs. agents**: Current AI models are agents (act on behalf of others) but lack agency (pursuing their own ends). A toddler, worse at every measurable task, is infinitely ahead in this respect.

## Overview

Dan Shipper, CEO of Every, presents a paradox at the heart of AI adoption: despite automating everything possible — coding via Claude Code, customer service via Fin, email via AI assistants — his team has grown rather than shrunk, and there is *more* work to do than ever. The companion essay "After Automation" (every.to, May 21, 2026) articulates the structural reason: AI commoditizes the residue of human expertise (whatever is explicit enough to train on), which collapses the value of default model output and simultaneously creates demand for what is *different*. Demand for difference is demand for human experts.

Shipper walks through Every's internal agent infrastructure — coworker agents like Claudie (consulting), Andy (editorial nuggets), and Viktor (analytics), plus embedded agents like Fin (customer service) — to show how the pattern repeats: agents handle the stable, repeatable layer, but every output needs a human to frame, judge, and extend it. This is the "human sandwich": humans set the frame → AI collapses the task → humans judge and extend.

The benchmark argument is particularly sharp. Any benchmark freezes a problem into a frame. Once saturated, the frame can be trivially zeroed out by changing it. The "Senior Engineer benchmark" Shipper describes — giving a coding agent a vibe-coded mess and asking for a first-principles rewrite — shows that GPT-5.5 reached 62/100 (humans in the high 80s). But if the frame changes to "should we rewrite at all, and if so, what scope, migration plan, and rollback?", the score drops to near zero. The benchmark measures the frame, not the framer.

Shipper ends with a philosophical argument: current AI is pure agent (acts on behalf of humans) with no genuine agency (ends of its own). A toddler, far worse at every measurable task, is infinitely ahead because the toddler has wants. Until AI pursues its own ends — which alignment actively suppresses — the gap between models and humans regenerates perpetually.

## Implications

- Knowledge workers should stop worrying about AI replacing them and start worrying about AI replacing the *generic* version of their work — the differentiated, expert judgment layer is structurally safe.
- Organizations building AI-native workflows will need more human reviewers, not fewer — review queues, evals, harnesses, and CI pipelines are the growth area.
- Benchmark-driven AI panic confuses frame saturation with human replacement; the frame is always changeable.
- The most valuable skill in an AI-saturated world is knowing *what* to do, not *how* to do it.
- Founders should treat their company as an early-adopter lab: the workflows emerging now at AI-native companies will spread broadly within 12-24 months.

> "Slop is visible sameness, repeated ad nauseam."

> "The score tells us how well the model operates inside a frame we supplied; it does not tell us that the model has become us."

> "AI makes yesterday's human competence cheap... Cheap competence gets rapidly adopted... Abundance creates sameness... Sameness creates a demand for difference... Demand for difference is new demand for experts."

> "Frames are necessary; they let us get traction on the world. But they are frozen, partial, and therefore optimizable. Framers are different."

> "The toddler is not waiting for a prompt. He is not optimizing against a benchmark, except whatever seems, to him, worth doing."
