## Key Points

- Complexity is hardwired into human psychology — we associate elaborate strategies with competence, even when simplicity outperforms.
- Financial independence starts with savings — everything else in financial planning is downstream of this single variable.
- A Canadian bank portfolio manager admitted he "couldn't manage a multimillion-dollar portfolio using just a few ETFs, because it would look too simple" — status incentives drive unnecessary complexity.
- A personal spending plan is more effective than a budget because it reframes spending as intentional allocation rather than restriction.
- Occam's Razor and the concept of irreducibility apply directly to investing: the simplest explanation (or strategy) that works is usually the best.
- Simplicity gives clarity and peace of mind — psychological benefits compound alongside financial returns.
- Incentive structures in the financial industry create systemic bias toward complexity: advisors and funds are compensated for activity, not simplicity.

## Overview

Host Stig Brodersen sits down with David Fagan, Managing Partner at MBF Chartered Professional Accountants (Nova Scotia, Canada) and co-creator of the free resource *Compounding Simplicity*, to make the case that simple investing strategies consistently beat complex ones — and explore why investors keep choosing complexity anyway.

The conversation traces the psychology of complexity: people conflate sophistication with competence, and the financial industry profits from this confusion. Fagan's anecdote about the portfolio manager who couldn't recommend a simple ETF portfolio because it would "look too simple" encapsulates the structural problem — advisors face social and professional pressure to appear busy and expert.

The hosts argue that the foundational variable in wealth-building is savings rate, not investment selection. Once savings are established, simplicity in portfolio construction — low-cost index funds, minimal rebalancing, long time horizons — tends to outperform active complexity on a risk-adjusted basis. Occam's Razor and the principle of irreducibility (no further simplification is possible without losing explanatory power) are applied as frameworks for portfolio design.

The episode concludes with the distinction between a "budget" and a "personal spending plan": budgets feel like restriction and tend to be abandoned, while a spending plan frames every dollar as a deliberate choice aligned with values, making adherence more durable.

## Implications

- Individual investors should default to the simplest portfolio that meets their goals; complexity should require a compelling justification, not the reverse.
- The financial services industry has structural incentives misaligned with investor interests — simplicity is an advantage for individuals precisely because it is not profitable for institutions to recommend.
- Savings rate is the lever most within individual control and has the largest impact on long-term outcomes.
- Psychological benefits of simple investing (clarity, reduced anxiety) may compound returns by preventing panic selling and unnecessary trading.
