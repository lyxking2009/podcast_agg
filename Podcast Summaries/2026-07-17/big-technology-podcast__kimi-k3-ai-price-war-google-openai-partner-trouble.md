# Kimi K3 & AI's Price War, What Happened To Google?, OpenAI's Partner Trouble

**Show:** Big Technology Podcast  
**Date:** 2026-07-17  
**Duration:** 61 min

## Key Points

- China's Moonshot AI released Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter model that rivals frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic on most benchmarks, triggering what analysts are calling a new "DeepSeek moment." Alex Kantrowitz and Ranjan Roy (of Margins) discussed how the model shook global markets, with Bloomberg reporting AI and semiconductor stocks sold off sharply after the release.
- Kimi K3's real significance isn't outright superiority but affordability: it's priced at $3/million input tokens and $15/million output tokens — roughly 40% cheaper than GPT-5.6 and 70% cheaper than Claude — while trailing Fable 5 on most benchmarks but beating it on select tasks like SWE Marathon. Roy argued this undercuts the idea that frontier models are a durable moat.
- Within days of Kimi K3's release, Meta shipped Muse Spark 1.1 and SpaceX launched Grok 4.5, both priced 25-50% below frontier levels, reinforcing a broader commoditization trend. Investor Gavin Baker (Arthis Management) argued this is a negative inflection for OpenAI and Anthropic specifically but a net positive for nearly every other layer of the stack — infrastructure providers, hyperscalers, and software builders — since a market dominated by two or three labs with ~90% inference margins is bad for everyone else.
- Roy described a dramatic shift in industry conversation: six months ago the consensus was "frontier models are unstoppable"; now the conversation has moved to "what is the best model for the best task," reflecting a reorientation toward model interoperability and away from single-vendor lock-in.
- Google's Gemini 3.5 Pro flagship model is reportedly months behind schedule, per Bloomberg, having missed internal goals particularly around coding capability. The hosts attributed this to competing AI initiatives spread across Cloud, DeepMind, and Android teams, "purist" engineers resistant to AI-written code, compute allocation conflicts between the cloud business and AI development, and disappointing retraining results in late June — a sharp reversal from Gemini 3's competitive parity at its November 2025 launch. Google's one clear remaining edge is multimodality (image and video), where competitors still lag.
- OpenAI is losing partners across the board: Apple sued OpenAI for trade secret theft, with allegations that Apple employees discussed exfiltrating data over Slack using messages as blunt as "LOL, I found all the network files"; Apple has sent legal preservation letters to dozens of OpenAI employees and says this is "the tip of the iceberg." OpenAI says it takes the allegation seriously but isn't aware of evidence it has merit. Kantrowitz traced a broader pattern of OpenAI's deteriorating relationships — Elon Musk (co-founder turned adversary), Dario Amodei (early employee turned Anthropic competitor), Microsoft/Satya Nadella (largest funder turned public critic), and now Apple (integration partner turned litigant) — arguing that in tech "you need friends to win," and OpenAI's accumulating losses of allies will compound into real damage.
- Microsoft executives raised what they call the "reverse information paradox": buyers of frontier AI models risk giving away proprietary knowledge just to get the model to perform well, since the better you want the model to work, the more of your own data/knowledge you have to feed it — a dynamic that complicates enterprise trust in frontier-model vendors.
- As raw model intelligence commoditizes, both hosts agreed OpenAI and Anthropic need to compete on product rather than capability alone. Roy framed this as competition shifting to "the harness and the process and the data." Anthropic was cited as further along here, with Claude Code and other developer tools now meaningfully monetizing beyond the raw API — a vertical-integration advantage OpenAI hasn't matched to the same degree.
- On the geopolitical/regulatory side, former Trump AI advisor David Sacks argued the US is "tying itself in knots" with data center bans and state-level regulation piling up, even as Moonshot achieved Kimi K3's results despite export restrictions and sanctions on advanced chips — prompting online mockery of the idea that a model trained on lower-end Huawei chips could beat well-resourced US labs running on Blackwell-class hardware. Kantrowitz also noted the irony of Sacks criticizing overregulation while having backed the "Fable ban" under the Trump administration, calling that restriction "poorly thought out over-regulating."

## Overview

This episode is Alex Kantrowitz's weekly news roundup with Ranjan Roy of Margins, covering three linked stories: the shockwave from Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 release and what it means for the economics of frontier AI; reports that Google's next flagship model, Gemini 3.5 Pro, has fallen badly behind schedule after a strong Gemini 3 launch; and a cascade of partner and legal trouble for OpenAI, capped by Apple's trade-secret lawsuit. The throughline across all three segments is that raw model capability is commoditizing fast — new competitive entrants (Kimi K3, Meta's Muse Spark 1.1, SpaceX's Grok 4.5) are matching or nearly matching frontier labs at a fraction of the price, which is reshaping how investors, enterprises, and the labs themselves think about durable competitive advantage. The hosts move from market reaction and analyst takes (Gavin Baker, David Sacks) into internal execution problems at Google, then into OpenAI's unraveling partnerships, before landing on the shared conclusion that product and ecosystem differentiation — not model quality alone — will decide who wins as intelligence becomes a commodity.

## Implications

For practitioners and investors, the episode argues that betting on any single frontier lab's model moat is increasingly risky — cost and interoperability are becoming as important as raw benchmark performance, and teams should expect to route across multiple models rather than standardize on one vendor. The commoditization trend is framed as bad specifically for OpenAI and Anthropic's premium-margin business models but good for nearly everyone else in the stack (infrastructure, hyperscalers, application builders), suggesting downstream software and infra plays may be relatively better positioned than pure model labs. Google's execution stumble is a reminder that even well-resourced, technically capable organizations can lose momentum through internal coordination failures and compute misallocation — a risk factor distinct from pure model capability. OpenAI's collapsing partner relationships (Apple, Microsoft, Musk, Amodei) signal that as intelligence commoditizes, the "friends and ecosystem" layer becomes a critical, under-priced strategic asset, and companies without it may struggle to defend their valuations even if their models remain competitive.
