# You're About to See the Real AI Winners Stand Up

**Show:** The Compound and Friends  
**Date:** 2026-07-17  
**Duration:** 88 min

## Key Points

- Josh Brown hosts alongside Sean Rousseau (filling in for Michael Batnick, who was away) and welcomes Jonathan Thomas, CEO and Chair of American Century Investments, a roughly $350 billion asset manager. The conversation opens on American Century's factor-investing subsidiary Avantis Investors, which launched in 2019 and reached about $150 billion in AUM in just 6.5 years — one of the fastest-growing ETF franchises in the industry. Thomas credits the growth to a fully transparent, low-cost, academically-grounded factor approach: of the Avantis products with five-year track records, all outperformed their benchmarks, averaging roughly 280 basis points of annual outperformance while charging only about 7 basis points more than comparable passive index funds.
- Thomas explains American Century's unusual corporate structure: because the firm isn't beholden to quarterly earnings pressure from public shareholders, it can invest patiently in long-term product buildouts like Avantis rather than chasing short-term flows. Sean Rousseau and Josh Brown discuss how "active is dead" narratives from a decade ago missed that what actually mattered to investors wasn't strictly active vs. passive, but fees, transparency, and the underlying building blocks of a strategy — which is why factor-based, rules-driven "active" ETFs have thrived even as traditional stock-picking active mutual funds kept bleeding assets.
- On AI and market valuations, the group digs into whether current earnings growth reflects a genuine productivity boom or an unsustainable capex-driven bubble. Consensus estimates call for about 23.2% year-over-year S&P 500 earnings growth, with the tech sector alone projected near 65% earnings growth. Thomas references Jensen Huang's "five-layer AI cake" framework (infrastructure, chips, data centers, models, applications) to argue that value capture will differ sharply by layer, and that the true test of whether the AI buildout is justified is whether "adopters" — the companies buying and deploying AI tools — actually realize the productivity gains analysts are pricing in.
- The hosts discuss market breadth broadening well beyond the "Magnificent Seven": roughly half of S&P 500 constituents are up more than 10% year-to-date, and the other 493 non-Mag7 stocks have been outperforming the mega-cap group as a whole. They note Nvidia's valuation multiple has compressed even amid strong earnings, which they read as the market pricing in future competitive pressure rather than doubting near-term AI demand.
- A major segment covers the Stowers Institute for Medical Research, funded by American Century's unusual ownership structure: because founder Jim Stowers Jr. and his wife directed the bulk of company ownership to a medical research foundation, over 40% of American Century's annual dividends — an estimated $2–2.3 billion over the past 20 years — has flowed to the Institute. Stowers employs roughly 500 scientists who take an unconventional approach, studying organisms that have evolved natural resistance to diseases like cancer, diabetes, and osteoporosis that afflict humans, then publishing both positive and negative results openly (including failed approaches) so other researchers worldwide don't waste time repeating dead ends. Thomas cites a recent Stowers-originated osteoporosis discovery that was licensed to Amgen as an example of the model working.
- Thomas recounts attending the Taylor Swift–Travis Kelce wedding in June 2026 at Madison Square Garden as a personal aside, describing extreme security (watermarked digital invitations, NDAs, a last-minute venue reveal), Adam Sandler officiating, and performances by Paul McCartney and Stevie Nicks, framing it as an example of the kind of relationship-driven, high-touch culture that also shows up in American Century's approach to advisors and clients (including its long-running celebrity-studded charity golf tournament).
- Closing out, Thomas traces how asset management distribution has evolved across his career: direct-to-consumer relationships in the 1980s–90s, a shift to advisor-centric distribution in the 2000s–2010s, and now an emerging platform-based/model-portfolio distribution era. He emphasizes that regardless of the era, the enduring value of a human advisor is behavioral guidance — keeping clients invested and rational during volatility — rather than pure product selection.

## Overview

Josh Brown, joined by Sean Rousseau in for Michael Batnick, hosts Jonathan Thomas, CEO and Chair of American Century Investments, for a wide-ranging conversation spanning the rise of American Century's factor-ETF subsidiary Avantis Investors (from launch to $150 billion in 6.5 years), the debate over whether AI-driven earnings growth is a bubble or a genuine productivity story, and the broadening of market leadership beyond the Magnificent Seven. Thomas explains how American Century's ownership structure — insulated from quarterly public-market pressure and tied to a charitable mission — enabled the long-term investment needed to build Avantis into a low-cost, high-outperformance franchise, and connects that same patient-capital structure to the firm's extraordinary philanthropic footprint: over 40% of its dividends fund the Stowers Institute for Medical Research, a roughly 500-scientist research operation using an evolutionary-biology approach to cancer and genetic-disease research that openly publishes its findings, including failures, to accelerate global research. The episode closes with lighter material, including Thomas's account of attending the Taylor Swift–Travis Kelce wedding, and his reflections on how advisor distribution models have shifted over his career while the core value of human financial guidance has stayed constant.

## Implications

- The Avantis growth story (and American Century's structural insulation from quarterly earnings pressure) suggests that low-cost, transparent, rules-based factor ETFs may continue taking share from both traditional active mutual funds and pure passive index products, reinforcing the industry shift toward "active-but-transparent" ETF wrappers.
- Whether the AI trade is justified now hinges, per this discussion, on whether AI "adopters" (not just AI infrastructure suppliers) start showing up in productivity and earnings data — a signal investors should watch for in coming quarters rather than assuming capex growth alone validates current valuations.
- Market breadth improving beyond the Magnificent Seven, combined with Nvidia's multiple compression despite strong results, points to a market that is starting to price differentiated winners and losers within the AI value chain (Jensen Huang's "five-layer cake") rather than treating AI exposure as monolithic.
- American Century's ownership-to-philanthropy pipeline is presented as a structural model other closely-held asset managers could emulate, and underscores how a firm's capital structure (not just its investment process) can shape both its product strategy and its non-financial public benefit.
- The discussion of shifting advisor distribution models (direct-to-consumer to advisor-centric to platform/model-portfolio) signals continuing structural change in how asset managers reach end investors, with implications for how firms like American Century allocate marketing and relationship-building resources going forward.
