# How to Profit off a Presidency

**Show:** Everybody's Business  
**Date:** 2026-07-17  
**Duration:** 34 min

## Key Points

- Bloomberg Opinion executive editor Tim O'Brien — a longtime Trump biographer who was sued by Trump over his reported net-worth estimate in his book *TrumpNation* — argues Donald Trump earned more money in the first year and a half of his second term than in any single year of his prior sixty-year business career, and attributes this entirely to the leverage of the presidency itself rather than to renewed business acumen.
- O'Brien frames the issue as a break from precedent: prior presidents treated the office's ethical "veil" as something not to be pierced, whereas Trump has turned the White House into what O'Brien calls "a Walmart," monetizing the presidency to the tune of billions of dollars. O'Brien insists this is a good-government issue, not a partisan one.
- The starkest example O'Brien cites: shortly after Trump's inauguration, the UAE's chief spymaster invested $500 million in the Trump family's crypto company; about two months later the federal government lifted a Biden-era export ban on high-end AI computer chips to the UAE that had been imposed for national-security reasons. O'Brien argues the administration traded national security and public safety for a $500 million family payday.
- O'Brien situates the grift discussion within a broader Businessweek issue on "grift" in the economy, tying it to consumer sentiment — Bloomberg producer Jasmine Green's street interviews found ordinary Americans feeling "scammed" by health insurance, rising food and cookout-tray prices, baby/parenting product marketing, and rent/taxes eating their paychecks.
- O'Brien warns the deeper stakes are institutional: the American experiment depends on the rule of law and good-faith actors in government and markets; when people at the top bend the system toward personal financial gain, public trust — and the experiment itself — is put at risk.
- In a lighter World Cup segment, Bloomberg sports reporter Randall Williams and the hosts discuss the tournament's business success (huge TV ratings — roughly 40 million peak viewers on Fox for the US-Belgium game, plus 10 million on Telemundo) despite pre-tournament worries about ticket prices and attendance, and note FIFA's continued commercialization (added mid-game commercial "water breaks," expansion from 32 to 48 teams, discussion of expanding further to 64).
- The World Cup discussion also covers a controversial VAR (video assistant referee) reversal of a red card against US player Folarin Balogun in the US-Belgium match, which followed a phone call from President Trump to FIFA president Gianni Infantino invoking a previously obscure rule (Article 27); Belgium went on to win 3-1 regardless.
- A recurring "Everybody's Business" mystery-solving bit follows up on a listener question (from "Julio") about why store-brand Triscuit-style crackers vanished from Aldi, Publix, Trader Joe's, and Walmart. Businessweek food writer Dina Shanker reports via a source that a single third-party manufacturer had been producing all the private-label Triscuit knockoffs and recently exited that line of business for reasons unrelated to wheat prices or IP disputes (private-label recipes aren't copyrightable), leaving no clear replacement.

## Overview

This episode of Bloomberg's *Everybody's Business*, hosted by Stacey Vanek Smith and Max Chafkin, centers on a Businessweek "grift" issue and features Bloomberg Opinion's Tim O'Brien — a Trump biographer with decades of history covering him, including a defamation lawsuit Trump filed against O'Brien over disputed net-worth reporting. O'Brien argues Trump has monetized the presidency more aggressively and successfully than any predecessor, citing his personal earnings during his second term and the UAE crypto-investment-for-chip-export-relief episode as the clearest example of the presidency being used for private family gain at the expense of national security and the public interest. The hosts also run consumer vox-pop interviews showing broad public perception of being "scammed" across health insurance, food prices, and baby products, which O'Brien connects to a broader erosion of institutional trust. A separate, lighter segment with Bloomberg sports reporter Randall Williams covers the business and controversy of the ongoing World Cup, including record US TV ratings, FIFA's revenue-maximizing tournament expansion, and a Trump-influenced VAR controversy in the US-Belgium match. The episode closes with the resolution (partial) of a running listener-submitted "mystery" about disappearing store-brand Triscuit crackers, investigated by Businessweek food writer Dina Shanker.

## Implications

- O'Brien's core claim — that presidential self-enrichment via crypto and business ties has become normalized rather than treated as scandal — suggests continued regulatory and diplomatic decisions (e.g., export controls, licensing) may increasingly be scrutinized for ties to the Trump family's financial interests, particularly in crypto and foreign investment.
- The UAE chip-export reversal, if the causal link O'Brien describes holds, signals that national-security policy can be reshaped by financial arrangements benefiting the president's family, a precedent with implications for future administrations and for allied/rival states seeking influence.
- Consumer sentiment data (persistent feelings of being "scammed" across everyday spending categories) points to a broader erosion of trust in both private markets and government that could shape political and consumer behavior independent of formal economic indicators like GDP or unemployment.
- FIFA's continued expansion (48 teams now, discussion of 64) and monetization (in-game commercial breaks) signals TV/ad revenue will keep driving tournament format changes, with future World Cup hosting increasingly limited to nations with the stadium capacity to support it (US, Saudi Arabia, and Spain/Portugal/Morocco cited as hosts through 2030s).
- The Trump-FIFA VAR intervention illustrates how political figures can now directly influence in-game officiating decisions in high-profile international sport, raising questions about the integrity and independence of sports governance bodies.
- The private-label Triscuit shortage — one manufacturer exiting a category, no immediate replacement — is a small but illustrative case of supply-chain concentration risk in retail store brands: a single point of failure caused several major retailers to lose an entire product line simultaneously.
