# World Models, Explained

**Show:** Y Combinator Startup Podcast  
**Date:** 2026-07-17  
**Duration:** 74 min

## Key Points

- The episode (part of YC's "Decoded" series) opens with the central puzzle motivating world models research: why do state-of-the-art AI systems need tens of thousands of examples to learn a skill a human can pick up in a handful of tries? Ankit Gupta and Francois Chaubard frame world models — internal simulations that predict how an environment's state changes in response to actions — as one of the most promising paths to closing that sample-efficiency gap.
- Perfect sample efficiency requires a perfect world model with zero training data, illustrated by Newtonian physics: SpaceX can land a rocket on an ocean platform, and NASA can plan an asteroid intercept, using model-predictive control derived purely from known physics equations rather than learned data. The core formalism is a state-transition function S_(T+1) = f(S_T, A_T, θ), paired with a policy π(A_T | S_T) and a value function tracking discounted future reward.
- Differentiability determines whether classical optimization (SGD, convex optimization) is even possible. Physical systems (a falling object, a steering rocket) are differentiable and can be planned against directly. Adversarial or human-involving systems break down because, as Francois puts it, "I can't backprop through your brain to say what you're going to do" — forcing a shift to stochastic, learned approaches.
- The hosts rank problem domains by tractability using state-space and action-space cardinality: chess (huge state space, small ~8-move action space) is tractable via tree search; Go (19x19 board, 3^361 states, ~361 actions) needed AlphaGo's Monte Carlo Tree Search (roughly 800 simulations and ~24,000 model calls per move) and would become computationally intractable on a larger board; self-driving (near-infinite state space, ~365,000 discretized actions, non-deterministic due to other agents) and robotics (16+ degrees of freedom, 10^16+ action space, scarce and expensive teleoperation data) are progressively harder, with robotics identified as the hardest current frontier.
- Self-driving also illustrates an "embodiment gap" problem: physics learned from one vehicle doesn't transfer cleanly to another (a Model 3 brakes differently than a heavier, more aerodynamically distinct Model X), so naive physics-based world models must still be learned/calibrated per platform rather than assumed universal.
- Model-free RL (learning a policy directly, e.g., vision-language-action models and behavior cloning on state-action sequences) is contrasted with model-based RL (learning an explicit world model plus policy). Model-based approaches yield stronger policies but are far more expensive at inference time because they require full test-time planning/rollouts rather than a single forward pass.
- The hosts trace the lineage of working world models: Jürgen Schmidhuber's early RNN-based world models trained purely on synthetic "imagined" rollouts and still performed in real environments (OpenAI Gym games like Car Racing and Doom); Danijar Hafner's Dreamer series trains a state-transition model on large amounts of unlabeled video, then injects action-conditioning from a much smaller labeled dataset, eventually becoming the first system to mine diamonds in Minecraft using only synthetic rollouts for policy training.
- Modern world models increasingly reuse large pretrained video diffusion models (e.g., Sora-like systems) and add a comparatively small amount of action-conditioning data on top, rather than training world models from scratch. Real examples cited include Wayve's GAIA for self-driving (built on roughly 500 hours of teleoperation data) and a robotics paper achieving cross-embodiment manipulation with limited data by instantiating an open-source video diffusion model.
- JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) is presented as the solution to the failure mode of predicting raw future pixels directly, which is computationally expensive, sample-inefficient, and prone to representational collapse (the model just learns to output near-zero). JEPA instead encodes frames into a latent space (S_T → Z_T), predicts the next latent state (Z_(T+1) = f(Z_T, A_T)), and minimizes distance in latent space — with regularization techniques like VicReg, SigReg, and the newer LEWM used to prevent collapse. The hosts draw an analogy to extending this idea to LLMs: predicting future embeddings as a cheaper proxy loss instead of pure next-token cross-entropy.
- Neuroscience is invoked as supporting evidence: a University of Washington paper found that each cortical area estimates both latent sensory states and actions, and the cortex as a whole predicts the consequences of actions — which Francois says maps almost exactly onto the two-equation (policy + world model) formalism discussed earlier. The hosts also cite the evolutionary expansion of the neocortex as being fundamentally about improving world-modeling capability, and a classic 1967 basketball study where mental rehearsal alone produced roughly 23% performance improvement versus a control group, as evidence that internal simulation is a core feature of biological intelligence.
- Several open problems are discussed at length: (1) physics fidelity — models trained mostly on "normal" driving data extrapolate incorrectly in rare scenarios (e.g., driving toward a house may get hallucinated back into a normal road) because SGD never drives training loss to true zero; (2) lack of fast test-time adaptation comparable to how quickly a human recalibrates to a new opponent or surface; (3) real-time performance, since MCTS-style planning remains slow even with pretrained synthetic rollouts; (4) missing sensor modalities — robots lack rich tactile/thermal/shear feedback, illustrated by the fact that numbing a human's hands makes it impossible to tie shoes despite intact motor control; (5) absence of a sleep/consolidation-like offline learning phase, noting that no known intelligent species goes without sleep, and that hippocampal replay during sleep runs sequences in reverse as part of memory consolidation.
- The episode closes on Yann LeCun's "squint test" — his rebuttal to the idea that AI needn't mimic biology, made using the analogy that flight didn't require flapping wings but did require wings. Applying the squint test to current world models, the hosts conclude they look "much, much closer" to how the brain seems to operate than autoregressive LLMs do, even though key pieces (offline consolidation, fast adaptation, rich sensing) are still missing.
- On near-term outlook, the hosts think self-driving could approach full autonomy mostly through more compute and data (with edge cases like unusual parking situations remaining hardest), while general-purpose robotics ("Rosey the Robot"-style systems) is closer than commonly assumed but bottlenecked by the expense of collecting teleoperation data needed for the Dreamer-style training sequence. They note YC is seeing startups across the entire stack — collecting egocentric data, collecting teleoperation data, and training proprietary world models — and that this is the first year demos in this space look like they're "actually starting to go somewhere."

## Overview

Y Combinator General Partner Ankit Gupta and YC Visiting Partner Francois Chaubard (a Stanford PhD candidate researching diffusion-based world models) walk through what world models are, why they matter for sample-efficient AI, and where the field currently stands. Starting from the observation that humans learn from a handful of examples while AI models typically need tens of thousands, they build up the formal framework of world models (state-transition functions, policies, value functions) using control-theory examples like rocket landings, then use it to compare the difficulty of chess, Go, self-driving, and robotics based on state- and action-space size. They trace the technical history from Schmidhuber's early synthetic-rollout RNNs through Hafner's Dreamer series to modern approaches that fine-tune large pretrained video diffusion models with small amounts of action-conditioning data, and explain JEPA as a fix for the inefficiency and collapse problems of predicting raw pixels. Neuroscience findings and a mental-rehearsal basketball study are used to argue that world models are a biologically plausible account of intelligence, applying LeCun's "squint test" to argue current world models are closer to how brains work than LLMs are. The episode closes with a survey of unsolved problems — physics fidelity in rare scenarios, fast test-time adaptation, real-time planning speed, missing tactile sensing, and the absence of a sleep-like consolidation phase — and a cautiously optimistic read on near-term progress in self-driving and robotics.

## Implications

For founders and researchers, the episode suggests that the next major unlock in embodied AI (self-driving, robotics) is less likely to come from bigger LLMs and more likely to come from better world models built on top of existing large pretrained video/diffusion backbones plus modest amounts of action-labeled data — lowering the data-collection bar relative to training world models from scratch. The framing of problem tractability by state/action-space cardinality gives a concrete lens for evaluating which embodied-AI problems are near-term tractable (self-driving, arguably) versus still fundamentally hard (general robotics, due to enormous continuous action spaces and expensive teleoperation data). The emphasis on JEPA-style latent-space prediction over raw pixel prediction is a signal that architecture choices reducing computational cost and avoiding representational collapse will matter more than raw model or data scale. The neuroscience framing (cortical prediction, sleep consolidation, mental rehearsal) suggests that missing pieces like offline consolidation and fast online adaptation are promising research directions rather than solved problems, and YC's observation that startups are now emerging across the full stack (data collection, teleoperation, world-model training) implies the sector is nearing a point where productized world models could become a distinct, investable infrastructure layer rather than something every robotics or autonomy company must build in-house.
