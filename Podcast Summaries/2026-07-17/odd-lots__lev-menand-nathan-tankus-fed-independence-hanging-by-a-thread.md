# Lev Menand and Nathan Tankus on Why Fed Independence Is Now Hanging by a Thread

**Show:** Odd Lots  
**Date:** 2026-07-17  
**Duration:** 66 min

## Key Points

- The Supreme Court's *Trump v. Slaughter* decision effectively overturned the logic of *Humphrey's Executor* (1935), the case that had protected heads of independent multi-member agencies (FTC, FCC, SEC, etc.) from at-will presidential removal. The Court instead revived the reasoning of the 1926 *Myers v. United States* decision, which favored near-total presidential removal power over executive officers.
- In the companion *Cook* case (over Trump's attempt to fire Fed Governor Lisa Cook), the Court carved out a special exception for the Federal Reserve, ruled 5-4, invoking "history and tradition" language borrowed directly from *United States v. Rahimi*, a Second Amendment case. The Court analogized to Founding-era historical practice the way Rahimi looks for analogous gun regulations at the time of the Second Amendment's ratification.
- Menand argues this analogy is factually wrong: the "history and tradition" the Court points to is Alexander Hamilton's First Bank of the United States, which was a privately-owned commercial bank with no government executive officers and no regulatory authority — not a precedent for insulating a government banking regulator from removal. Hamilton's writings support independent monetary policy, not independent bank regulation, which didn't exist as a federal function at the time.
- Only two of nine justices (Kavanaugh and Roberts) actually endorse the Fed carve-out as coherent; three (Kagan, Sotomayor, Jackson) think the Fed should have no special protection beyond the FTC (i.e., wanted Slaughter's logic to apply everywhere, so they'd have let Cook be reinstated but for different reasons), and others (notably Thomas) think the Fed should get no protection at all and should have been just as removable as the FTC. This makes the current 5-4 "Fed exception" outcome extremely fragile and dependent on two justices who won't serve forever.
- The Fed Board of Governors is a government regulatory body (not the operationally separate, investor-owned Federal Reserve Banks) that carries out monetary policy chiefly through regulation — most notably capital requirements on banks. The Trump administration is currently loosening bank capital rules, which is functionally monetary stimulus, even though it isn't formally "monetary policy" and thus isn't shielded the same way. Footnote six of the Cook opinion tries to preempt this by saying the Fed exception applies to the Fed "as currently constituted," avoiding a power-by-power analysis.
- Tankus details how the Fed Board's total independence from congressional appropriations — funded by self-set "assessments" on the regionally-owned Federal Reserve Banks, which are themselves money-creating institutions — is what actually insulates it from White House and congressional budget control, not any special statutory removal protection (which historically was actually the weakest of any independent agency, since the Federal Reserve Act never explicitly defined removal "cause").
- Menand and Tankus trace the "independent central bank" ideology to post-Volcker-shock economics literature and IMF advocacy for central bank independence worldwide, arguing this created a contradiction: the U.S. spent decades promoting central bank independence abroad while lacking a clear constitutional basis for it domestically. They contrast this with the CDC, which had no comparable legal insulation despite arguably needing similar credibility protections during COVID.
- Discussion of Congress's "power of the purse" as the deeper constitutional logic for insulating the Fed (per a 1980s case, *Bowsher v. Synar*, striking down a similar arrangement for the Comptroller General), and why the Court avoided embracing that broader principle — it would require protecting many other agencies whose funding functions also implicate the purse.
- Both guests describe the broader political backdrop: a "unitary executive" legal movement, aligned with parts of the tech right (reacting to Gary Gensler and Lena Khan), pushing to dismantle the New Deal-era "fourth branch" of independent, expertise-driven administrative agencies, with the Fed now standing out awkwardly as the sole surviving exception.
- The episode closes on rule-of-law concerns: Menand connects the discussion to *Marbury v. Madison* (courts can compel executive officers to follow the law) and warns that eroding removal protections, combined with agencies not being forced to comply with court orders, risks a broader constitutional crisis where "the law is what Trump says."

## Overview

Bloomberg's Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal host Columbia law professor Lev Menand and Nathan Tankus (Notes on the Crises) to unpack two linked Supreme Court decisions — *Trump v. Slaughter* and the Lisa Cook case — that reshape presidential power to remove agency heads. Slaughter effectively guts Humphrey's Executor, giving presidents broad removal power over independent agency leaders (FTC, FCC, SEC), while a separate ruling carves out the Federal Reserve as uniquely protected, citing "history and tradition" reasoning imported from a Second Amendment case (Rahimi) and pointing to Alexander Hamilton's advocacy for the First Bank of the United States. Menand argues this historical analogy is a fundamental misreading, since Hamilton's bank was a private commercial institution, not a government regulator, and Hamilton never articulated a case for independent bank regulation as opposed to independent monetary policy. The conversation traces the deeper history of Humphrey's Executor and Myers, the origins of independent multi-member regulatory commissions (starting with the Interstate Commerce Commission in 1887), and how post-1980s economics orthodoxy about central bank independence created a legal-versus-ideological mismatch that only recently reached a breaking point. Tankus adds detail on the Fed's unique budgetary autonomy (self-funded via assessments on Federal Reserve Banks, outside congressional appropriations) and warns how thin the current arrangement is — only two justices actually endorse the carve-out's logic, making it a fragile, contingent equilibrium. The episode ends with a discussion of the political forces (unitary executive theorists, tech-right disillusionment with Gensler/Khan-era regulation) driving the broader assault on independent agencies, and closes with concerns about rule of law and constitutional crisis if agencies stop being compellable to follow the law.

## Implications

- Fed independence now rests on an unstable 5-4 majority built around two justices (Roberts, Kavanaugh) rather than a durable legal principle; a future case or court composition change could remove the Fed's special protection entirely.
- The Court's reasoning explicitly covers only Fed "monetary policy" as currently structured — leaving the door open for future challenges to the Fed's bank regulatory and supervisory powers (capital rules, stress tests), which Menand and Tankus note are already being targeted rhetorically.
- Because the Fed's insulation is grounded in a shaky historical analogy rather than a coherent purse-power theory, other historically independent functions (e.g., IRS enforcement, NIH/NSF grantmaking) have no comparable protection, raising the specter of executive-directed de facto fiscal policy (e.g., altering bank capital rules for stimulus, or non-recourse lending used as a policy tool).
- The episode frames this as part of a broader "unitary executive" push to dismantle the New Deal administrative state, with potential downstream effects on regulatory stability, capital markets predictability, and the rule of law if agencies become non-compellable by courts.
- Markets and international observers who rely on the credibility of U.S. central bank independence (a norm the U.S. spent decades promoting abroad) now face genuine uncertainty about whether that independence is legally durable domestically.
