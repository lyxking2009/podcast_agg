# HIGHLIGHTS: Eliot Higgins

## Key Points

- Eliot Higgins, founder of Bellingcat, describes how the organization does "open source investigation" — using publicly available material (social media, satellite imagery, marine/flight tracking data) to investigate chemical weapons attacks in Syria, the downing of MH17, US strikes on Iran, and ICE/DHS raids. Bellingcat has grown from Higgins blogging alone in 2012 to a 35-person core organization, a trained volunteer network of about 200, and a 43,000-member Discord community, all built around the value of "never prejudge a situation" — gather evidence before forming an opinion.
- Higgins recounts the origin story: in 2012 he was arguing with people online about the Syrian war from his kitchen table in Leicester, not out of noble intent but to "win arguments," and discovered he could geolocate YouTube videos using satellite imagery. He launched Bellingcat in July 2014, and three days later MH17 was shot down, catapulting the fledgling site into finding details (like the missile launcher's origin point) that no one else had found.
- He details the Skripal and Navalny poisoning investigations: Bellingcat used Russia's "gray market" of purchasable government data (passport registrations, phone records with cell-tower location history) to unmask the Skripal poisoning suspects as Russian GRU agents, and later used similar phone-record tracking to identify the FSB chemical-weapons team behind Navalny's poisoning — including Navalny's own prank call where an FSB operative described the poisoning method in detail.
- Russia has escalated retaliation against Bellingcat over the years: state media smear campaigns since 2015, hacking attempts, and six Bulgarian nationals jailed for spying on a Bellingcat colleague on behalf of the FSB. Higgins also personally receives occasional visits from UK counterterrorism police and avoids travel to Russia and now the US, citing the "censorship industrial complex" conspiracy theory (which falsely ties Bellingcat to the Biden administration) and being publicly labeled a "scoop" — sic — by Elon Musk.
- On disinformation, Higgins describes an elaborate Russian campaign that sent Bellingcat fake news videos (including a 3D avatar of him) containing numbered jigsaw-puzzle pieces that, once assembled, formed an insulting image of him — which he treated as an amusing hobby rather than a threat. He argues disinformation only works when audiences want to engage with it, citing a failed Russian campaign targeting Armenia that got no traction because English-language audiences don't care about Armenian politics.
- Speed matters in the "verification vs. false narrative" race: Higgins says Bellingcat typically has "two or three hours" to get verified facts into public discourse before false narratives calcify — as in the Alex Prey/Minnesota shooting case (synchronizing multiple videos to show the true sequence of events) and an Iranian school-bombing case where Bellingcat's footage of Tomahawk missile strikes forced Donald Trump into a public (and false) denial within 48 hours.
- Higgins frames the deeper problem as a shift from a top-down, institution-mediated information system (verification, deliberation, accountability via media/courts/parliaments) to a "peer-to-peer many-to-many" system where platforms like X reward engagement over truth, letting populist and conspiratorial content outperform slower, procedurally-verified reporting — a dynamic he calls "authoritarian capture" driven by loyalty tests rather than truth-seeking.
- On AI, Higgins says it mostly hasn't transformed Bellingcat's core investigative process yet (though tools like Claude have automated tedious scraping tasks), but it has given people a "permission structure to deny reality" — dismissing real evidence as "AI-generated" while embracing AI-fabricated content (e.g., a fully AI-generated far-right UK rapper) that confirms existing beliefs. He also notes Bellingcat's funding model (crowdfunding, foundations, workshops) has been badly destabilized by US aid funding cuts, which wiped out 25-40% of global investigative-journalism NGO funding overnight.

## Implications

- Bellingcat's reliance on purchasable "gray market" government data in Russia (passport records, cell-tower-linked phone records) illustrates both a powerful investigative technique and a fragile one — Russia has already moved to criminalize soldiers posting geolocatable content, suggesting adversaries will keep closing off the specific data channels that enabled past breakthroughs.
- Higgins's "two-to-three-hour window" framing suggests that for any actor (investors, policymakers, media) trying to act on fast-breaking, contested events, waiting for institutional verification may mean acting on an already-calcified false narrative; open-source/verified-first reporting increasingly serves as the practical alternative to slower institutional channels.
- His diagnosis that engagement-optimized platforms structurally favor populist/conspiratorial content over procedurally verified information implies that institutions (including investment firms making decisions in fast-moving, high-disinformation environments like Iran) cannot assume "more information" equates to "more truth," and may need dedicated verification capability rather than relying on ambient media coverage.
- The abrupt loss of 25-40% of global investigative-journalism NGO funding from US aid cuts signals a broader contraction in independent verification capacity worldwide, particularly in the Global South, at the same time as disinformation volumes are rising — a widening gap between supply of verified information and demand.
- Higgins's observation that AI mainly provides a "permission structure to deny reality" (dismissing real evidence as fake, embracing fake content that confirms priors) suggests that as generative AI content proliferates, the practical challenge shifts from producing verification to getting people to accept verified evidence at all.
