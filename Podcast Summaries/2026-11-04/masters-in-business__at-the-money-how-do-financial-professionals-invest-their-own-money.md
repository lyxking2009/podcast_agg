# At The Money: How Do Financial Professionals Invest Their Own Money? — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
In this episode of Bloomberg's At The Money, host Barry Ritholtz interviews Brian Portnoy, CEO of Shaping Wealth and co-editor of 'How I Invest My Money' volumes one and two, about why financial professionals rarely reveal how they invest their own money. Portnoy explains the book's genesis from Josh Brown's blog, how all 50 contributors (25 per volume) eagerly shared personal stories, and the key themes that emerged—such as prioritizing psychological comfort over mathematically optimal strategies, paying off mortgages early, and holding extra cash. The conversation highlights that financial planning is highly personal and that the best plan is one you can stick with.

## Key points
- Brian Portnoy co-edited two volumes of 'How I Invest My Money' with Josh Brown; the project began after Josh Brown's 2019 blog 'How I Invest My Money' received strong feedback, leading to a book contract with Harriman House within 72 hours.
- Host Barry Ritholtz reveals that he contributed a chapter to the second volume, noting he is one of the 25 contributors.
- Each volume contains 25 chapters from different financial experts; remarkably, all 50 contributors (25 per volume) agreed immediately when asked, with no one declining, according to Portnoy.
- Portnoy emphasizes that the project gave contributors 'permission' to share their personal investment approaches, revealing that even professional fund managers diversify across equities, bonds, real estate, charitable giving, and insurance beyond their specialized roles.
- The books intentionally feature diverse voices—men, women, people of color, early-career and veteran professionals—so readers can find relatable stories and perspectives that resonate with their own life experiences.
- Contributors were given a completely blank page with only the instruction 'Answer the question, how do you invest your money?'; most chose to answer 'why' they invest, sharing life stories about family, priorities, hopes, fears, and past experiences.
- Host Barry Ritholtz notes that the books' royalties are not a money grab; contributors received no compensation, and Portnoy jokes that each contributor could receive 1/25th of proceeds or a Starbucks gift card for a large coffee.
- A recurring theme across all 50 chapters is that financial experts often prioritize behavioral comfort over mathematical optimality; many contributors paid off mortgages early despite the potential for higher market returns, because it provided a sense of safety and security.
- Similarly, many contributors held more cash than theoretical models suggest, choosing psychological well-being over potential long-term returns, echoing the principle that the best financial plan is the one you can stick with.
- Daniel Crosby's line 'there's a difference between spreadsheet optimal and behaviorally optimal' is referenced to explain why experts choose plans that may be suboptimal on paper but more sustainable in practice.
- Portnoy highlights Tim Maurer's observation that 'personal finance is more personal than finance,' noting that different risk tolerances and life circumstances justify different portfolios even among seasoned professionals.
- The host and guest agree that optimizing for human needs—like using credit card points for a luxury purchase rather than cash back—can be more satisfying than purely mathematical decisions.
- Portnoy says editing the books did not change his own investment approach, which is anchored in simplicity, diversification, and compounding, and he has long practiced self-reflection in his financial planning.
- The episode concludes with Ritholtz recommending the books to anyone who consumes financial media and wonders what experts actually do with their own money.
