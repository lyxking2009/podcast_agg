# #195 - Lisa McKenzie & Keith Gildart - Who Is Britain For? The Destruction of the Working Class

## Key Points

- Britain's working class was not simply "left behind" — political and economic decisions deliberately dismantled the jobs, institutions, culture, and communities that once gave working-class life security, identity, and meaning.
- Keith Gildart draws on his seven years as an underground coal miner to illustrate how pit closures destroyed not just jobs but entire community structures and identities in mining towns.
- The Labour Party progressively abandoned its traditional working-class base, stopping saying "working class" and reframing class purely in income terms rather than cultural and structural terms.
- Immigration and low wages are interconnected issues — employers exploit immigrant labor to undercut wages, while politicians use immigration as a scapepist to avoid addressing structural inequality.
- Working-class teenagers today want opportunity, dignity, and genuine social mobility — not dependency or handouts — but Britain's education and economic systems fail to provide pathways.
- Britain has a deep, pervasive class prejudice that operates differently from racism or sexism — it's socially acceptable to mock and dismiss working-class people, their tastes, and their communities.
- The rise of Reform UK reflects working-class voters' justified belief that Westminster no longer represents them — Labour, Conservatives, and even the left have all failed to address their material concerns.
- The working and middle classes are both being destroyed by the same forces: asset inflation, housing unaffordability, precarious employment, and the concentration of wealth among a small elite.

## Overview

Sociologist Lisa McKenzie and historian/former coal miner Keith Gildart join Peter McCormack for a sweeping examination of how Britain systematically dismantled its working class. The conversation traces the arc from the destruction of industrial Britain through Thatcher-era pit closures, Labour's New Labour transformation, the disappearance of secure work and affordable housing, and the rise of Reform UK as a protest vehicle. McKenzie and Gildart argue that class is about far more than income — it encompasses culture, identity, community, and belonging. They examine how education systems perpetuate inequality, how immigration policy serves elite interests while blaming immigrants, and how both major parties have abandoned working-class voters. The episode builds to the central question: who is Britain actually for now?

## Implications

The conversation suggests Britain faces a genuine crisis of democratic representation — working-class voters have been failed by every major party, driving them toward populist alternatives. The structural destruction of working-class communities has created conditions ripe for further political instability. Any party that genuinely addresses housing, wages, and community investment could unlock a large, currently alienated voter base.
