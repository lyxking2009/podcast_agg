# S2 E7: It’s Up to Us — Sold a Story

## TL;DR
In the Season 2 finale of Sold a Story, Emily Hanford explores why Direct Instruction (DI)—Zig Engelmann’s highly scripted, teacher-led method—remains rejected by many educators despite strong evidence. The episode examines cultural and philosophical resistance, teacher experiences, and concerns about rigidity, while also highlighting DI’s potential to accelerate learning for struggling students. Hanford concludes that the bigger problem is a lack of agreed-upon professional knowledge in teaching, and she cautions against mandating DI, urging instead a deeper understanding of effective instruction.

## Key points
- A middle school science teacher (anonymized as Teacher 3) was fired after openly advocating for direct instruction over inquiry learning, illustrating the career risks teachers face when challenging prevailing progressive pedagogy.
- Cathy Watkins recounts a family member saying, 'I would rather a child never learn to read than learn to read like that,' demonstrating deep philosophical opposition to Direct Instruction that persists even when evidence supports it.
- Carl Hendrick traces skepticism about direct teaching to Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s 18th-century book Emile, which argued that children should learn through natural curiosity and discovery, an idea that later underpinned progressive education.
- Direct Instruction acquired a reputation as conservative or right-wing, but Jerry Silbert, a liberal and Civil Rights Movement participant, notes that many liberal friends hated DI, particularly its scripted lessons.
- Marty Siegel says Direct Instruction’s scripts are both its greatest strength and its Achilles’ heel, because they ensure consistent, carefully sequenced teaching across thousands of teachers but also invite dismissal as overly rigid.
- Hailey McCarthy, a Florida charter network principal, believes strongly in DI but criticizes the National Institute for Direct Instruction for being 'rigid and unyielding,' which turns off schools with different contexts.
- Greg Ashman, a former science teacher, argues that teaching’s superpower is transmitting hard-won knowledge—such as why the falling moon never hits Earth—so students don’t have to start from zero.
- Carl Hendrick observes that unlike medicine or dentistry, teaching lacks an agreed-upon professional body of knowledge, and the phrase 'teaching is an art' implies only a tiny fraction can succeed, whereas millions of teachers must be effective.
- Teachers often blame students when methods fail; Carrie Chee and Adrienne Allen both admit they blamed children for not learning, while Jean Stockard insists that learning problems require teaching solutions, not just addressing poverty or hunger.
- Emily Hanford explicitly does not want lawmakers to mandate Direct Instruction, arguing that understanding and commitment cannot be legislated; the season’s goal is to clear misconceptions and ask why an effective method is rejected.
- Zig Engelmann maintained until his death that no child is unteachable, stating in a 2018 speech, 'There aren’t any kids out there that can’t be taught. None.'
- Zig Engelmann died at 87 about six months after that speech; his sons Owen and Kurt say he was satisfied with his accomplishments, having checked off all his goals even though his programs were not widely adopted.
- Kurt Engelmann estimates fewer than 200 U.S. schools use Zig’s programs schoolwide, and only four states—Alaska, Arizona, Colorado, and Pennsylvania—list Zig’s reading program for core instruction, despite the science of reading movement.
- Doug Carnine, a close collaborator of Zig, stopped trying to convince schools to adopt DI, arguing the bigger problem is that teaching lacks an agreed-upon professional knowledge base, similar to medicine before handwashing became standard.
- Hanford warns that a teacher talking while students passively listen is not Direct Instruction; real DI involves carefully designed sequences, active student responding, and immediate correction of errors.
- In his final recorded speech, Zig Engelmann described how a child starting at the 18th percentile could improve to the 43rd percentile over several years with effective teaching, declaring that no doctor can achieve such transformation—only teachers can.
