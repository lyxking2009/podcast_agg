# At The Money: Shopping in an Era of Surveillance Capitalism — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
Lindsay Owens, CEO of Groundwork Collaborative and author of 'Gouged,' discusses how surveillance capitalism and algorithmic pricing are eroding the 150-year-old fixed price tag, leading to personalized pricing, loyalty penalties, drip pricing, and repair restrictions. She offers consumer strategies like comparing prices across devices and highlights the importance of public backlash, while also examining emerging AI shopping agents and the need for legislative protections like click-to-cancel and right-to-repair.

## Key points
- Fixed price tags, introduced about 150 years ago by Quakers and popularized by John Wanamaker, are now under threat from algorithmic and personalized pricing that can vary prices per individual based on collected data.
- Companies run pricing experiments on consumers; Owens advises consumers to run experiments back by comparing prices simultaneously with friends or a spouse on different devices to get the lower price, especially for airline tickets and rideshares.
- Loyalty programs can result in a 'loyalty penalty': companies use purchase history to determine you're a sure thing and stop offering discounts or perks. Examples include a Washington Post reporter's Starbucks data showing diminishing perks and a Wired reporter's McDonald's app data revealing a 'lifetime attrition score' of zero.
- Kroger, where 95% of transactions are tied to loyalty programs, derives 30% of its net operating income from alternative profit streams including selling customer data, illustrating how loyalty data is monetized.
- Drip pricing and junk fees involve luring customers with low teaser rates and then adding fees; the FTC rule at the end of the Biden administration now requires upfront all-in pricing for short-term lodging and event ticketing.
- Subscription traps and 'roach motels' are common; Owens's organization released model legislation called the Shopper's Bill of Rights including the right to a refund on the same payment method and click-to-cancel. New York City implemented a click-to-cancel rule about a week before the recording.
- Right to repair is a form of price gouging; companies like John Deere, car companies, and Taylor Commercial Food Services (McFlurry machines) restrict repairs to increase profits. Nathan Proctor at US PIRG leads the right-to-repair movement, with wins including MOUs with John Deere and FTC actions.
- Consumer backlash is powerful: examples include Coca-Cola abandoning a 1990s plan to vary vending machine prices by temperature after public outrage, and Delta walking back its partnership with Fetcher after exposure by Groundwork Collaborative; Instacart also pledged not to use certain practices after a study and backlash.
- Costco maintains a cap on markup (around 14%) and avoids dynamic or personalized pricing, contributing to consumer loyalty; the Savannah Bananas use fan-first pricing with a lottery system instead of dynamic pricing.
- AI shopping assistants and agentic commerce have potential, but early meta-analyses show that AI assistants often return higher prices even when asked to find lower ones, raising concerns about agents working for companies rather than consumers.
