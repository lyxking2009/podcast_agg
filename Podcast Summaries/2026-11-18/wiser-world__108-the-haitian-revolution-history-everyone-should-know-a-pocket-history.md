# 108. The Haitian Revolution: History Everyone Should Know | A Pocket History — Wiser World

## TL;DR
This episode offers a pocket history of the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), detailing how enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose against one of the world's most brutal slave systems, defeated French, Spanish, and British forces, and founded Haiti as the first nation born of a successful slave revolt and the second independent country in the Americas. It covers key figures like Toussaint Louverture and Jean-Jacques Dessalines, the revolution's global impact including the Louisiana Purchase, and the lasting economic damage caused by the indemnity France forced Haiti to pay.

## Key points
- Saint-Domingue, the French colony on the western third of Hispaniola, was among the wealthiest colonies in the world in the 1780s because of sugar and coffee exports, but this wealth depended on enslaved labor. Enslaved people made up roughly 90% of the population—about 500,000 enslaved people compared with 30,000 white colonists and 25,000–30,000 free people of color. Conditions were so brutal that plantation owners often calculated it was cheaper to work enslaved people to death and buy new captives than to improve conditions, so the enslaved population did not grow naturally and was maintained by constant importation from Africa, with many captives arriving from western and central Africa and some with military experience from wars in places like the Kingdom of Congo.
- The colony had a complex social hierarchy: wealthy white planters (grands blancs), poorer white colonists (petits blancs), and free people of color (gens de couleur libres), who included people of mixed ancestry and some wealthy Black people. Many free people of color owned plantations and enslaved people themselves, but they faced growing legal discrimination and were denied political rights, creating tensions that would later influence the revolution.
- Resistance to slavery existed long before 1791. Enslaved people slowed work, broke tools, escaped to form maroon communities, and in the 1750s François Mackandal was accused of organizing a poisoning plot against planters before being captured and executed in 1758. Some free men of color from Saint-Domingue gained military experience fighting for France at the siege of Savannah in 1779 during the American Revolution, later playing roles in the Haitian Revolution.
- The French Revolution of 1789 and its Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen cracked open Saint-Domingue's political order. Wealthy planters wanted more local control but not equality for others, poor whites wanted more political power, free people of color demanded equal rights, and enslaved people heard news of the revolution and began to question slavery itself.
- In 1790, free man of color Vincent Ogé returned from France and demanded political rights for free men of color; when colonial authorities refused, he and a few hundred supporters took up arms, but the uprising was defeated and Ogé was brutally executed in early 1791. However, France's revolutionary legislature later granted full political rights to free people of color in 1792.
- The main slave uprising began on August 22, 1791, in the northern plain, after secret organizing across plantations through personal connections, religious gatherings, and traveling workers. According to Haitian tradition, a Vodou ceremony at Bois Caïman helped unite rebels. Within weeks, tens of thousands of enslaved people had joined, destroying hundreds of plantations, burning sugar fields and mills, and killing planters; colonial forces responded with horrific reprisals.
- Toussaint Louverture, born enslaved in the early 1740s and freed in the 1770s, was literate and in his late 40s when the uprising began. He emerged as a brilliant military commander and politician, initially fighting alongside Spain against France, but in 1794 he switched to the French Republic after France abolished slavery in its colonies.
- Facing war with Britain and Spain and the massive rebellion, French officials desperate for allies promised freedom to enslaved rebels who fought for France in June 1793, then abolished slavery in northern Saint-Domingue in August 1793. In February 1794, the revolutionary government in Paris abolished slavery throughout all French colonies, a major turning point that shifted many rebels, including Toussaint, to the French side.
- Toussaint Louverture helped expel Spanish forces (Spain made peace in 1795) and British forces (who withdrew by 1798 after enormous losses from fighting and yellow fever). By 1801 he had defeated his main internal rival André Rigaud in the War of Knives, taken control of the formerly Spanish eastern side of Hispaniola, and introduced a constitution that permanently abolished slavery and named him governor for life.
- Toussaint's labor policies created deep resentment: to rebuild the devastated economy and fund the army, he required many formerly enslaved people to continue working on plantations with wages or a share of crops, but severely restricted their ability to leave or choose other work, enforced by the military. Many felt they had fought for freedom only to be forced back onto the same plantations, illustrating the conflict between protecting national independence and individual freedom.
- Napoleon Bonaparte, who took power in France in 1799, aimed to rebuild a French empire in the Americas and saw Saint-Domingue as central to that plan. In late 1801 he sent an expedition of more than 20,000 soldiers under his brother-in-law to restore French control and remove Toussaint. After fierce fighting, Toussaint agreed to stop fighting in May 1802 after being promised retirement, but French officers arrested him weeks later and sent him to a fortress in France, where he died in April 1803.
- After Toussaint's removal, Napoleon's government began restoring slavery in parts of the French Caribbean, including Guadeloupe, which intensified resistance in Saint-Domingue. General Jean-Jacques Dessalines and other leaders who had briefly sided with the French turned against them; combined with yellow fever killing thousands of French soldiers, the French were eventually defeated and surrendered in November 1803.
- On January 1, 1804, Jean-Jacques Dessalines officially declared independence, naming the new country Haiti after the indigenous Taíno name Aayiti (meaning 'land of mountains'). Haiti became the second independent country in the Americas after the United States, the first in Latin America and the Caribbean, and the first nation founded by people who had freed themselves from slavery.
- In early 1804, Dessalines ordered the killing of much of the remaining French white population, an event known as the 1804 Haitian massacre, driven by fear of French reinvasion and desire for revenge. This massacre was used by opponents to portray Haiti as dangerous and contributed to international hostility and isolation.
- After independence, Haiti faced enormous challenges: more than a decade of war had destroyed plantations, towns, and trade; leaders disagreed on how to rebuild without recreating slavery. Dessalines declared himself emperor in 1804 but was assassinated in 1806; the country split into rival governments under Henri Christophe in the north and Alexandre Pétion in the south, and was not reunified until 1820.
- In 1825, France sent warships to Haiti and demanded an indemnity of 150 million francs to compensate former French colonists for lost property, essentially forcing Haiti to pay for the wealth built on enslaved labor. Under threat of invasion, Haiti agreed; the amount was later reduced to 90 million francs in 1838, but Haiti had to borrow from French banks to make payments, and the debt drained resources for generations, hindering development.
- The Haitian Revolution had major global impacts: Napoleon's failure to retake Saint-Domingue led him to sell the Louisiana Territory to the United States in 1803, doubling U.S. size; thousands of refugees from Saint-Domingue settled in New Orleans, shaping its culture; and Cuba expanded its own plantation economy and slavery after Saint-Domingue's sugar production collapsed.
- The revolution changed the debate over slavery: slaveholders across the Americas feared it as a symbol of revolt and tightened control, while enslaved people and abolitionists saw Haiti as proof that enslaved people could defeat European empires and build a nation. The United States refused to recognize Haiti until 1862, partly due to fear of inspiring slave uprisings.
- Haitian anthropologist Michel-Rolph Trouillot argued that Europeans and Americans couldn't imagine enslaved Africans organizing and winning a revolution, which contributed to the revolution being minimized or omitted from histories. The episode argues that racism and fear are key reasons the Haitian Revolution is under-taught despite its importance.
- As of the recording (end of 2026), France has not repaid the indemnity. In April 2025 President Emmanuel Macron acknowledged a 'historical injustice' against Haiti, and in June 2025 the French National Assembly passed a non-binding resolution calling for a study of how the debt might be returned, but no repayment has occurred.
