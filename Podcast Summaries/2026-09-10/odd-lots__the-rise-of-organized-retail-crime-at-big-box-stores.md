# The Rise of Organized Retail Crime at Big Box Stores — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

In this episode of Odd Lots, Home Depot VP of Asset Protection Scott Glenn explains the scope and mechanics of organized retail crime, from boosters and fences to online resale on peer-to-peer platforms. He details how shrink is categorized (roughly 30% operational, 70% malicious), the most stolen items (power drills by volume, copper by dollars), and the shift to supply chain theft like fraudulent pickups and rail heists. Glenn highlights safety-first policies, technology like AI and QR-code lockups, and bipartisan legislation (COORCA) aimed at providing federal resources to combat the problem.

## Key points

- Retail shrink is estimated in the tens of billions of dollars, but exact figures vary due to inconsistent categorization across retailers. Scott Glenn notes that Home Depot splits shrink into operational/administrative (about 30%) and malicious (about 70%), with traditional breakdowns attributing roughly 40% of malicious loss to associates, 35% to external shoplifters, and the rest to operational errors, though these ratios may be drifting upward.
- Organized retail crime operates via a structured chain: boosters (often recruited, given lists, sometimes drugs) steal goods and deliver them to fences, who consolidate, clean, and resell them, primarily online. Since the INFORM Consumers Act took effect in 2023, sales have shifted from large marketplaces like Amazon and eBay to peer-to-peer platforms such as Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist, which are not covered by the law's seller verification requirements.
- At Home Depot, the most commonly stolen item by volume is a power drill, while copper and wire lead in dollar value due to their commodity value. Fences typically price technology-laden goods at 70-80% of retail, while commodity items like deodorant and Tylenol sell for 30-40% of retail.
- To combat in-store theft, Home Depot locks select high-shrink items, using QR codes that summon an employee in about 90 seconds; the company is exploring giving loyal pro customers self-service access to reduce friction. Interestingly, locking items can increase sales in some categories because in-stock availability improves.
- Supply chain theft is growing, with three main modes: diversion of full trailer loads from ports or transload facilities, breaking into parked trailers, and fraudulent pickup using fake bills of lading—sometimes aided by AI-generated documents. Rail theft has also surged, with thieves often having insider intelligence to target specific valuable cars.
- Home Depot uses AI and video analytics to identify suspicious behaviors and web crawling to detect stolen goods sold online, which helps compress a typical organized retail crime case from discovery to closure from around 70 days to a shorter timeframe. The company prioritizes associate and customer safety, training staff to de-escalate rather than confront violent boosters.
- Scott Glenn is bullish on legislative progress, citing the bipartisan Combating Organized Retail Crime Act (COORCA), which passed the House and is awaiting Senate action; it would provide federal resources, prosecutors, and coordination centers to address ORC as interstate commerce, a shift from years of underreporting and decriminalization trends.
