## Key Points

- Susquehanna Investment Group sued 100 anonymous "John Does" for alleged insider trading after a Chinese regulatory crackdown on cross-border brokerage trading caused those firms' share prices to plummet on May 22, and the defendants had bought short-dated out-of-the-money put options just before the announcement.
- The lawsuit is primarily a tactical move to compel brokerages (Interactive Brokers, Futu Holdings, Tiger Brokers) to freeze accounts and disclose the identities of the traders rather than to immediately prove insider trading in court.
- Susquehanna frames this as potentially one of the largest insider trading schemes in recent memory, though Matt Levine notes the evidence of a coordinated conspiracy is probabilistic — some of the 100 may have simply been following observable market signals.
- Options market makers like Susquehanna function similarly to a casino or sportsbook, where the core risk is "sharp betters" — people who win more than they lose — and insider traders are the primary category of such betters in short-dated options.
- Thomas Peterffy of Interactive Brokers argued on a recent Odd Lots appearance that insider trading rules should be abolished, which would allow market makers to widen spreads instead, ultimately passing costs to retail investors.
- The SEC issued a request for public comment on ETF regulation, specifically flagging prediction market and sports gambling ETFs as a category of concern, and exploring whether ETF filings should be confidential to prevent copycat applications.
- Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy) is in financial distress: Bitcoin's decline has pushed its market cap below net asset value (NAV), its STRIFE preferred stock trades in the 80s rather than at par ($100), and it has raised the dividend on STRIFE from 11.5% to 12% while signaling it will no longer try to maintain the par peg.
- Strategy's STRIFE preferred stock was described as a ChatGPT-designed instrument — a monthly-resetting, discretionary-dividend perpetual preferred that functioned like short-term rolling deposits used to buy Bitcoin, now carrying a market-clearing rate around 15%.
- Strategy's path out involves unwinding the trades it made on the way up: selling Bitcoin, buying back stock, and redeeming preferred — all at losses, with significant frictional costs.
- Matt Levine flagged that Strategy's leveraged Bitcoin buying previously drove it to be the dominant corporate and ETF Bitcoin buyer in 2025; that buying has now slowed, removing a key source of upward price pressure on Bitcoin.

## Overview

The episode opens with hosts Matt Levine and Katie Greifeld discussing 4th of July plans before diving into three financial stories. The first and title topic concerns Susquehanna Investment Group's lawsuit against 100 anonymous traders (styled "John Does 1-100") who purchased short-dated out-of-the-money put options on Chinese brokerage stocks just before China announced a major regulatory crackdown on cross-border trading on May 22. The crackdown blindsided markets, sending those stocks sharply lower and leaving Susquehanna, the options market maker, with large losses. Susquehanna alleges the 100 traders either had inside information about the crackdown or were part of a coordinated scheme. The lawsuit targets accounts at Interactive Brokers, Futu Holdings, and Tiger Brokers — notably, many of the same platforms that were themselves targets of the crackdown.

Levine contextualizes Susquehanna's position using a 1992 interview with Jeff Yass in "New Market Wizards," where Yass explained that the ability of market makers to trust regulators to police insider trading allows them to quote tighter option spreads. The lawsuit, Levine argues, is less about proving insider trading in court and more about a probabilistic business decision: by naming all 100 traders in one action, Susquehanna can freeze accounts and extract identities; half may be innocent, but those innocent parties must affirmatively show up in a foreign court to reclaim their money. The discussion also covers Thomas Peterffy's contrarian view that insider trading rules should simply be abolished, since markets would adapt through wider bid-ask spreads.

The second story covers the SEC's request for comment on ETF regulation, specifically around the explosion of novel ETF categories including prediction market and sports gambling funds. Levine has a standing prediction that sports gambling ETFs will exist in the US within two years of March 2025. The SEC is exploring whether ETF applications should be made confidential (to prevent dog-piling copycat filings) and whether it needs better tools to suspend or reject filings for ETFs that hold non-traditional assets like Bitcoin, gold, or sports bets. The third topic is Strategy's worsening financial situation, with its STRIFE preferred stock trading below par, rising effective borrowing costs, and its first steps toward unwinding the leveraged Bitcoin accumulation strategy that defined the company's identity.

## Implications

Susquehanna's lawsuit against 100 John Does signals a notable shift in how sophisticated market makers respond to suspected insider trading: rather than waiting for regulatory enforcement, they are using civil litigation to unilaterally freeze accounts and recover funds. The case highlights a structural feature of options market making — market makers cannot easily screen out "sharp betters" the way casinos can, so civil and regulatory remedies become their primary tools. If this approach succeeds, it could establish a template for other options dealers facing similar cross-border insider trading dynamics, particularly in markets where enforcement cooperation with foreign regulators is limited.

Strategy's distress is a significant data point for the "digital asset treasury" (DAT) model that many other companies attempted to replicate. The failure of STRIFE to hold par — essentially the collapse of what functioned as a leveraged, short-duration Bitcoin funding vehicle — illustrates the fragility of structures that depend on continuous market confidence and perpetually rising asset prices. If Strategy is forced into a prolonged deleveraging, it removes one of the most consistent incremental buyers in the Bitcoin market, which could matter for price formation even independent of broader macro dynamics.
