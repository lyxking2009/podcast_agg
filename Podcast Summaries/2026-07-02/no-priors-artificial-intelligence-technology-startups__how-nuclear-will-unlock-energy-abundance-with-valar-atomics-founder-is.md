## Key Points

- Valar Atomics became the first advanced reactor startup to make nuclear power, turning on reactor W-250 in Utah under Executive Order 14301, which mandated three advanced reactors go critical by July 4th, 2026.
- The company's core philosophy is that nuclear is a hardware execution problem, not a design problem — emphasizing iteration, simplicity, and rapid deployment over elaborate paper designs or modeling and simulation.
- Valar's trico-fueled, graphite-moderated, helium-cooled reactor is designed for passive safety: when all active cooling systems are shut off, the geometry of the reactor ensures decay heat dissipates naturally with no operator input, no moving parts, and no electricity.
- Their concept of "tick rate" — the time between successive reactor startups — started at 2 years 4 months and is now 7 months, with the goal of eventually reaching minutes between new reactor activations.
- Valar invented their own grade of concrete (no rebar, high-density, gamma-ray-blocking) and built modular pre-cast "Citadel" blocks in-house, reducing bioshield construction from 3 months to 42 hours.
- They built their own reactor protection system (RPS) — a $5 million, 2.5-year vendor quote — in 6 weeks for roughly $400,000, exemplifying the vertical integration strategy they apply to any supply chain bottleneck.
- Taylor argues nuclear companies fall into two camps: hardware-execution companies (like Valar) and design/simulation companies — and only the former will actually scale.
- The "gigasite" strategy involves Valar independently building and owning large-scale power sites (targeting gigawatts), then letting data center customers come to the power rather than negotiating complex co-location deals.
- Taylor views energy as the fundamental input to all manufactured goods, arguing that cheap energy combined with AI and robotics will drive costs toward zero — what he calls "hypertechnoindustrialism."
- Valar is venture-backed on an equity basis deliberately, arguing VC is uniquely suited to underwrite technical execution risk while traditional project finance is too risk-averse for early-stage nuclear.

## Overview

Isaiah Taylor, founder and CEO of Valar Atomics, joined No Priors hosts Sarah Guo and Elad Gil at the company's Utah nuclear facility — the site of the W-250 reactor, the first advanced nuclear reactor built and powered by a startup. The episode is part interview, part facility tour, recorded while the reactor was actively splitting atoms and even hosting an Nvidia Blackwell GPU-powered website directly from the reactor's output. Taylor traces his journey from a teenager obsessed with nuclear's untapped potential — inspired by a great-grandfather who was a Manhattan Project physicist — to founding Valar at 24 and, within three years, achieving what no other private company founded after the discovery of fission had accomplished: generating nuclear power.

The conversation covers the technical and philosophical foundations of Valar's approach. Taylor explains why the nuclear industry stalled after Three Mile Island, how the Department of Energy's original mandate as a testing agency (distinct from the NRC's commercial deployment role) provided the regulatory pathway for Valar's reactors, and why the dominant nuclear incumbent strategy of paper designs and modeling has failed. He walks through Valar's specific innovations: the passive safety architecture that removes the need for active cooling post-shutdown, the modular concrete bioshield invented and manufactured in-house, and the custom reactor protection system built in six weeks by a five-person team for a fraction of vendor cost. Throughout, Taylor hammers on two themes: ruthless simplicity in design and an obsessive internal culture of speed.

The episode closes with an expansive vision of what cheap nuclear energy enables. Taylor argues that energy is the fundamental input to all human welfare and manufactured goods — from transportation to materials to everyday objects. As AI and robotics convert human labor into energy consumption in manufacturing, the cost of everything becomes increasingly a function of the cost of energy. Make energy ten times — or eventually a thousand times — cheaper through nuclear, and you unlock a future where consumer goods, travel, and even space exploration become dramatically more accessible.

## Implications

The episode documents a genuine inflection point in the nuclear industry: the first time a venture-backed startup has achieved nuclear power generation. If Valar's tick rate continues to compress — from 28 months to 7 months and falling — it suggests that startup-style hardware iteration can work in nuclear, a domain long assumed to be the exclusive province of governments and century-old utilities. The combination of passive safety architecture, modular manufacturing, and vertical supply chain control (if it scales) could make nuclear cost structures look more like advanced manufacturing than civil infrastructure, which is the central bet of the company. The gigasite strategy — building power capacity speculatively and letting customers come to it — also represents a departure from the co-location deal-making approach most nuclear and energy startups pursue, reducing dependency on any single customer and potentially accelerating deployment timelines.

The broader implication is for AI infrastructure. Data center operators have consistently cited 2031-2035 as the timeline for nuclear to matter to their power strategies. If Valar's approach is credible, that timeline may compress significantly — or at least, the competitive pressure of a functioning private nuclear company may force the industry to reckon with how close deployment actually is. The framing Taylor offers — that energy is the bottleneck to human flourishing, that AI is converting labor into energy demand, and that nuclear is the only path to dramatically cheaper energy — positions nuclear not as a climate play but as an economic and technological necessity.
