- A special episode released to mark the 250th anniversary of the United States, with hosts Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry, and Alexandra Schwartz presenting a listener-curated American playlist
- Listeners submitted songs they felt best encapsulated their feelings about the U.S. today, which the hosts combined with picks of their own

## Overview

To mark America's 250th birthday, the Critics at Large team — Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry, and Alexandra Schwartz — stepped away from their usual cultural commentary format to curate a musical portrait of the nation. Rather than a conventional retrospective, the episode is built around listener participation: the hosts solicited song submissions from the audience, asking what music best captures the state of the United States today. Those responses were woven together with the hosts' own selections to form an 18-track playlist spanning nearly a century of American recorded music.

The resulting tracklist is deliberately eclectic and politically charged. It ranges from Aaron Copland's classical orchestral suite ("Billy The Kid") to Rage Against the Machine's industrial-metal protest anthem ("Sleep Now in the Fire"), from Simon & Garfunkel's wistful American road imagery to Olivia Rodrigo's sardonic feminist critique. The episode is organized around the tension between American mythology and American reality — the ideal of freedom and the persistent failure to extend it equally. Songs like Roberta Flack's "Compared to What" (originally written by Gene McDaniels) and The Isley Brothers' "Ohio/Machine Gun" medley foreground the dissenting tradition, while Tom Petty's "Free Fallin'" and Kim Wilde's "Kids in America" capture a more naive, aspirational register.

The episode closes thematically on Sam Cooke's "A Change Is Gonna Come," which functions as both elegy and promise. The hosts frame the playlist not as a definitive statement about what America is, but as an act of collective witnessing — a way of taking the nation's emotional temperature at a moment of uncertainty. The Spotify playlist was released alongside the episode so listeners can experience the music as they listen.

## Implications

This episode is notable for using music curation as cultural criticism rather than traditional verbal analysis. By soliciting listener submissions, the hosts democratized the critical act and made the episode a kind of real-time cultural survey — the playlist becomes a document of how a particular, likely urban and culturally engaged, listener base feels about the United States at the 250-year mark. The heavy representation of protest music (Rage Against the Machine, Billy Bragg, John Prine, Roberta Flack, The Isley Brothers) alongside more ambivalent or wistful tracks suggests that disillusionment and hope are not mutually exclusive in the current American imagination.

Cunningham's framing — that genuine nation-building happens through creative response, not political structures — positions art as the more durable and honest record of what a society actually is. This is a significant editorial statement for a New Yorker podcast to make in 2026, a year marked by America's semiquincentennial celebrations and considerable political turmoil. The episode implicitly argues that the 250-year story of America is best understood through its music, and that the music tells a more complicated and more truthful story than official commemorations tend to allow.
