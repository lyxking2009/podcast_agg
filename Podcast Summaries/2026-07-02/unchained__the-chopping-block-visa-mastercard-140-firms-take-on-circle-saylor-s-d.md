## Key Points

- A 140-firm consortium including Visa, Mastercard, BlackRock, Coinbase, Google, and Stripe launched Open USD, a no-fee stablecoin designed to challenge Circle's USDC and Tether's dominance; Circle stock dropped 7-8% on the news.
- Open USD distributes yield to consortium members based on contribution rather than keeping it for the issuer — the opposite of Circle's business model — with governance handled by an Open Standard Board and no single controlling entity.
- Michael Saylor unveiled a "Digital Credit" framework in response to MSTR's 30% five-day decline: an 18-month cash cushion of $2.5 billion, a dividend hike on STRC from 11.5% to 12%, up to $1 billion in buybacks, and potential Bitcoin sales of up to 2.5% if needed.
- The panel debated whether MSTR is a "Luna for suits" (Tarun) or a structurally sound Bitcoin accumulation vehicle (Jordi), with Jordi's key distinction being that Bitcoin — unlike LUNA — cannot be infinitely printed.
- Haseeb argued that a 140-member consortium is effectively "a signed petition," questioning whether Open USD can actually dislodge Tether's exchange dominance or Circle's deep DeFi integration without dramatically expanding the total market.
- ENS DAO governance faced a crisis when Nick Johnson, controlling approximately 50% of voting power, unilaterally blocked a treasury restructuring proposal, reigniting the debate about whether DAOs represent genuine decentralization or just obscure concentrated control.
- Tarun suggested AI could make DAOs more viable by acting as delegate representatives — automated voting agents that carry out token holders' stated preferences — improving coordination without centralization.
- The Ansem-named memecoin, which Ansem himself did not launch, reached roughly $100 million market cap, signaling renewed speculative demand after an extended lull in memecoin activity.
- Recurring theme across all three stories: entities claiming decentralization (Open USD consortium, DAOs, Saylor's preferred share structure) face the same tension between distributed governance and the efficiency of centralized decision-making.

## Overview

This episode of The Chopping Block brings together regular hosts Haseeb Qureshi, Tom Schmidt, and Tarun Chitra with guest Jordi Alexander (Selini Capital) to dissect three major crypto stories from the week ending July 2, 2026. The conversation opens with the launch of Open USD, a no-fee stablecoin backed by over 140 firms spanning payments (Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Stripe), finance (BlackRock, BNY Mellon, Standard Chartered), tech (Google, Samsung, Shopify, DoorDash), and crypto infrastructure (Coinbase, Solana, Base, Polygon, Aptos). Unlike USDC, which keeps the yield on backing assets, Open USD distributes that revenue to consortium members. Notably absent: Circle, Tether, and PayPal. The panel is skeptical about the consortium's ability to topple incumbents — Haseeb likens it to a signed petition — but acknowledges the fee-sharing model could attract serious participation if incentives are calibrated correctly.

The second major segment covers Michael Saylor's response to Strategy's deteriorating market position: MSTR fell approximately 30% in five days and the MNAV dropped below one, meaning shares traded below the value of the underlying Bitcoin. Saylor responded by rebranding the capital structure as "Digital Credit," announcing an 18-month dividend cushion, a buyback program, and a potential willingness to sell Bitcoin if conditions demand it. Tarun maintains the "Luna for suits" framing, pointing to problematic marketing around STRC preferred shares; Jordi pushes back, arguing the structural difference from Terra/LUNA is fundamental because Bitcoin supply is fixed. The episode closes with the ENS DAO controversy — Nick Johnson's use of a dominant voting stake to block a governance restructuring — which the panel uses as a lens for the broader question of whether DAO governance is meaningful or theatrical.

## Implications

The Open USD launch represents the most credible institutional challenge to the USDC/Tether duopoly to date, not because of the consortium's size but because the fee-sharing model directly attacks Circle's core revenue structure. If major payment rails route stablecoin volume through Open USD, Circle's business — which depends almost entirely on capturing the yield on USDC reserves — faces structural pressure even without Open USD winning a majority market share. However, the panel's skepticism is well-founded: changing the default settlement layer in DeFi protocols and on exchanges requires deep technical integration and strong network effects, not just a coalition announcement.

The Strategy situation highlights a systemic risk that the crypto market has been slow to price: the degree to which MSTR's Bitcoin accumulation activity has become a market-moving signal in itself. As Haseeb noted, a significant portion of market participants are positioning around what Saylor will buy or sell, creating reflexive dynamics. The Digital Credit reframe may buy time, but the fundamental tension — a leveraged Bitcoin vehicle whose attractiveness depends on Bitcoin appreciation — remains unresolved. Meanwhile, the ENS DAO episode suggests a broader vibe shift in the industry away from decentralization as a terminal goal and toward pragmatic efficiency, with AI-assisted delegation governance as a possible future path.
