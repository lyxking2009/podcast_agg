## Key Points

- A new academic paper ("Ordering Power is Sanctioning Power") from Chinese university researchers shows that stablecoin freezes can be front-run because the freeze itself is a blockchain transaction competing for block space — making sanctions enforcement a market structure problem, not purely a legal one.
- Over eight years, only nine stablecoin freeze transactions successfully intercepted funds before they moved; 7% of sanctioned addresses had a zero balance by the time the freeze landed.
- 94% of on-chain stablecoin sanctions are issuer-driven compliance actions (Tether and Circle), not government-ordered sanctions — raising questions about the constitutionality of delegating enforcement to private actors.
- The SEC and CFTC issued a joint request for comment on harmonizing portfolio margin rules across securities and derivatives markets, a development that could meaningfully reduce collateral requirements for institutional crypto derivatives traders.
- The CLARITY Act has stalled in the Senate calendar; a CBDC-ban provision buried in a housing bill caught in a presidential veto dispute is creating additional political friction for crypto legislation.
- The EU's MiCA transition completed, replacing 27 fragmented national regimes with a unified licensing framework — providing the kind of regulatory predictability that the U.S. still lacks.
- A legal tech startup (Legion) sued after losing access to Anthropic's Fable AI model due to export restrictions, raising concerns that governments may use export law to control who accesses published code rather than what code gets published — a potential First Amendment issue under Bernstein v. Department of Justice precedent.
- AI chatbot conversation logs are increasingly used as evidence in criminal litigation without warrant protections, with users unaware these interactions do not enjoy privacy protections similar to attorney-client privilege or therapy sessions.
- The hosts warn that expanding the third-party doctrine to deputize crypto infrastructure providers for law enforcement carries significant Fourth Amendment constitutional risks.

## Overview

This DEX in the City episode centers on a striking new finding from academic researchers: stablecoin freezes are fundamentally broken as a sanctions enforcement tool. The paper "Ordering Power is Sanctioning Power" demonstrates that freeze transactions must compete for block space just like any other transaction, meaning sophisticated bad actors can front-run the freeze and move funds before it lands. Vy Le walked through the data — only nine successful mid-transfer freezes in eight years of on-chain sanctions history, with 7% of sanctioned addresses already drained by the time the freeze transaction confirmed. The hosts examine what this reveals about the current system, where 94% of on-chain sanctions are private compliance actions by Tether and Circle rather than government orders, and the constitutional concerns about deputizing these issuers without proper process.

The episode also covers a wide range of legal and regulatory news. Katherine Kirkpatrick Bos highlighted the SEC/CFTC joint request for comment on portfolio margin harmonization as a quietly significant institutional unlock. Jessi Brooks dove deep into the Legion lawsuit against the government's use of export restrictions to cut off access to Anthropic's Fable AI model — drawing a parallel to early crypto code distribution battles and warning that "winners and losers are being chosen without a process." The hosts also provided a status update on the CLARITY Act, now mired in Senate procedural complications, and offered the EU's completed MiCA transition as a counterexample of what regulatory certainty looks like in practice.

## Implications

The stablecoin freeze research exposes a structural gap at the heart of crypto enforcement: the tools regulators rely on to seize illicit funds move at the speed of legal process, while crypto moves at the speed of block production. If even Tether and Circle — the most compliance-oriented stablecoin issuers — can only successfully intercept funds in rare cases over an eight-year sample, the practical deterrence value of on-chain sanctions is severely limited. This raises a harder question: whether the regulatory architecture being built around stablecoin issuers as enforcement proxies can ever work at sufficient speed, and whether it is constitutionally appropriate to delegate that power to private companies without due process.

The AI export control story carries separate but equally significant implications for crypto. The argument that governments can use export law to restrict who accesses already-published code — not just what code gets published — would represent a significant doctrinal shift. Crypto code (smart contracts, cryptographic protocols) has long operated under the assumption that open publication is protected. If the Legion case establishes a precedent that access can be controlled post-publication through export mechanisms, the chilling effect on open-source crypto development and decentralized protocol deployment could be substantial.
