## Key Points

- A bond ladder staggers bond maturities across multiple years (e.g., 5, 7, or 10 years) so investors gain control over interest rate risk without needing to predict rate movements.
- iShares iBonds ETFs (pioneered by BlackRock in 2010) allow investors to build diversified ladders holding hundreds of bonds per maturity year, solving the diversification problem of individual bond laddering.
- ETF bond ladders offer superior liquidity versus individual bonds — selling an entire ladder of individual bonds can be costly and complex, while ETF-based ladders allow cheap, fast liquidation.
- ETFs trade with bid-ask spreads of pennies on exchange; individual bonds can have spreads that are multiples of that, making ETF ladders more cost-efficient despite having expense ratios.
- The iShares Ladder Builder tool at ishares.com lets investors configure ladders by dollar amount, bond type (corporates, Treasuries, TIPS, munis, high yield), and maturity year out to 2056 (a 30-year ladder).
- In the final maturity year of a bond ETF, bonds mature at different times throughout the year and proceeds are reinvested in cash equivalents until the ETF is fully liquidated — different from receiving a single par payment at maturity.
- High yield bond ladders offer higher initial yields but come with default risk, meaning total returns often fall short of the headline yield; diversification via ETF mitigates individual issuer risk.
- For investors with known future cash needs (college tuition, major purchases), building a treasury or TIPS ladder matched to that timeline is lower risk than reaching for yield with high yield bonds.
- Perpetual laddering — continuously rolling the maturing rung out to the longest maturity — mathematically resembles holding a broad bond index fund, supported by academic research.
- The key advantage of a ladder over an index fund is control: investors can stop reinvesting at any time and let the remaining rungs mature to par, which is not possible with an open-ended index fund.

## Overview

Barry Ritholtz hosts Stephen Laipply, Managing Director at BlackRock and Global Co-Head of iShares Fixed Income ETFs, to discuss the mechanics, benefits, and practical considerations of building a bond ladder using ETFs. The conversation explains why bond laddering — historically the domain of wealthy investors using separately managed accounts — is now accessible to everyday investors through low-cost, diversified ETF products.

Laipply explains that the fundamental appeal of a bond ladder is control and certainty of cash flows in an unpredictable rate environment. By spacing bond maturities across multiple years, investors avoid the "perpetual loss" problem of open-ended bond funds if rates rise, because they can simply let each rung mature and recover par value. iShares iBonds ETFs replicate this structure but with hundreds of bonds per maturity year, providing diversification that would require millions of dollars to achieve in individual bonds. The ETFs also bring standard ETF benefits: price transparency, intraday liquidity, and low trading costs compared to OTC bond markets.

The discussion covers practical matters such as maturity selection (tied to investor's life events or cash flow needs), credit quality trade-offs, and what to expect when a bond ETF approaches and reaches its maturity date. Ritholtz and Laipply also explore a surprising mathematical finding: continuous perpetual laddering closely approximates holding a broad bond index fund, making laddering intellectually connected to passive indexing strategies.

## Implications

The democratization of bond laddering through ETFs represents a meaningful shift in fixed income investing. Tools like the iShares Ladder Builder eliminate the expertise, capital, and complexity that previously kept retail investors out of structured bond portfolio strategies. Investors now have the ability to match bond cash flows to known future liabilities — education expenses, retirement income, major purchases — with treasury-grade certainty if needed, or higher yield with credit diversification if appropriate.

For advisors and investors choosing between a ladder and an index fund, Laipply's point that perpetual laddering essentially converges to indexing is significant: the differentiator is not return profile but control. In a volatile rate environment, the psychological and practical value of knowing you can stop reinvesting and coast to maturity at any point is substantial, and ETF bond ladders now deliver that benefit at scale and low cost.
