# Richmond Fed’s Tom Barkin on the Surprisingly Resilient Real Economy — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin discusses the resilient U.S. economy, persistent inflation above target, and the impact of the AI data center boom. He explains that consumers are finding creative ways to keep spending despite high prices, and that business investment has surged, partly driven by AI. Barkin also addresses Fed communication, expressing support for transparency while cautioning against over-reliance on forward guidance and the dot plot.

## Key points
- Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech was viewed as hawkish, describing policy as not restrictive and noting inflation has been above target for 65 months. Barkin said he aligned with Warsh's economic assessment but noted Warsh dislikes forward guidance, so the speech should not be taken as a signal for September.
- The economy remains solid: GDP and consumer spending are strong despite rising gas prices and uncertainty, and business investment has almost doubled historically, driven significantly by AI-related spending. The jobs market has stabilized with low unemployment, and the low-hire, low-fire dynamic continues.
- Consumer resilience stems from a post-COVID mindset where people are determined to spend. Financial buffers from stimulus, higher equity and home values, and creative behaviors—such as shifting to Walmart and dollar stores, adult children living at home to avoid rent, paying auto loans just enough to avoid repossession, and delaying gas bill payments—are effectively borrowing from the future, with savings rates down.
- The AI data center construction boom is monumental, with $700 billion announced in one week in early February. Switchgears, transformers, and electricians are in very short supply, and construction activity has shifted away from office and multifamily toward data centers, raising questions about crowding out other construction and increasing costs.
- Barkin sees the main near-term impact of AI as political and hiring-related rather than a broad productivity boom. Town halls and chamber meetings are dominated by questions about data centers, water, and jobs. Productivity gains so far are largely from 2022-era investments in automation and staffing models, while AI is used for call centers, programming, and documentation. Companies are delaying hiring to see if AI can fill roles.
- The housing market is currently soft, but Barkin expects it will tighten again because a generation of buyers wants homes and inventory is insufficient. He cautions against over-parsing inflation components like rent, preferring to view inflation as a total basket of goods.
- On Fed communication, Barkin holds two opposing views: transparency builds credibility and helps markets, but too much forward guidance can box the Fed into suboptimal decisions, as in 2021-22. He values the internal SEP process for sharpening thinking but dislikes the dot plot because it overwhelms the narrative and leads to misinterpretations like 'the Fed promised two rate cuts.'
- Regarding tariffs, business-to-business companies are largely passing through costs, but consumer-facing companies struggle, especially those serving lower-income customers. Big box retailers resist price increases. Tariff refunds following a Supreme Court ruling are positive for earnings, leading to reinvestment in customers, marketing, store refits, and staffing, which is stimulative but not broadly price-increasing.
- Barkin offered two ways to frame inflation: a 65-month view where the Fed has failed for 65 months, or a 47-month view followed by 18 months, where inflation fell to 2.3-2.4% by March 2025 before external shocks (AI, tariffs, oil) pushed it back up. He insists on getting inflation under control but acknowledged the debate is ongoing.
- Data centers bring tax dollars but few permanent jobs, creating no local political base. Citizens worry about water, aesthetics, and AI, while economic developers tout tax benefits. Barkin noted the outcome could be either overbuilding or underbuilding, and the Fed cannot predict the scale of AI's economic impact with confidence.
