# Rogue Planets & Exomoons with David Kipping — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR
Astrophysicist David Kipping discusses the recent candidate exomoon around a brown dwarf, the challenges of biosignature false positives, and his proposed A/B testing strategy for future life-detection missions. The conversation covers surprising exoplanet diversity, rogue planets detectable by microlensing, the possibility of life on tidally heated moons of rogue planets, and the galactic habitable zone. Patreon questions also explore naming conventions, high-gravity planets, and whether technosignatures could be relics from extinct civilizations.

## Key points
- CD-35B candidate exomoon: a brown dwarf orbiting an M-dwarf star shows a companion detected via Doppler wobble after coronagraphic separation; lead author Kevin Hoy, Chilean group, VLT. Mass ratio 1/37; IAU definitions classify anything orbiting a brown dwarf as a planet, but there is no official moon category, creating ambiguity.
- Exoplanet diversity: mini-Neptunes (2-4 Earth radii) are the most common planet type, yet our solar system lacks one; super-Earths are ambiguous between rocky and gaseous; circumbinary planets like Tatooine require close binary stars for stable P-type orbits.
- Rogue planets: the solar system likely once had a fifth giant planet ejected to stabilize Neptune; many rogue planets are expected, perhaps more than bound planets; the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will use microlensing to detect thousands by observing brief brightening of background stars as a rogue planet's gravity bends light.
- Rogue planet life: rogue planets retain internal heat from formation and radioactive decay; moons can have tidal heating like Io/Europa, sustaining subsurface oceans and life without sunlight for billions of years; energy, not sunlight, is the requirement.
- Biosignature false positives: claims like ALH84001, Venus phosphine, and K2-18b dimethyl sulfide have been challenged by abiotic explanations; Kipping proposes A/B testing for future observatories like the Habitable Worlds Observatory, splitting target groups to statistically control confounders.
- LHS 1140b: 48 light-years away, 5.5 Earth masses, 1.7 Earth radii; density suggests not pure rock because self-compression implies a lighter envelope (gas/ocean/ice); in the habitable zone but not Earth-like; biosignature gases like DMS, oxygen, and methane still face false positive problems.
- Habitable zone evolution: M-dwarf habitable zones are closer and narrower; as stars age, the habitable zone moves outward; the Sun was 30% less luminous 4 billion years ago, Earth was on the outer edge then, now inner edge, increasing CO2 sensitivity.
- SETI history: NASA separated the search for intelligent life after Senator William Proxmire's Golden Fleece scrutiny; SETI Institute took over with private funding; a second independent technological civilization would suggest no great filter, intensifying the Fermi paradox.
- Technosignatures vs biosignatures: technosignatures like engineered transmissions or artificial transits (e.g., a giant triangle shading a star) can be unambiguous, while atmospheric gases will be debated for decades; artifact SETI proposes relics like Pioneer plaques for future non-human intelligences.
- Naming exoplanets: catalog coordinates are needed for unambiguous identification; historical precedents include asteroids named after gods, feminized versions, and evil gods for Earth-crossers; common names like Barnard's Star coexist with catalog designations.
- Galactic habitable zone: near Sgr A* radiation, stellar density, and supernova risk make life unlikely; metallicity gradient needed for planet formation; the Sun's circular orbit in galactic suburbs at about two-thirds radius is favorable.
- High-gravity planets: 2-3x Earth gravity would lead to gas giant formation due to gas accretion; super-Earths max around 1.7-1.8 Earth radii; 1.5g is plausible but the rocket equation makes spaceflight extremely difficult, potentially trapping civilizations.
- Distance and extinction: technosignatures could come from civilizations already extinct due to light travel times (tens of thousands to 100,000 light-years), making first contact archaeological; nearby biosignature targets are only tens of light-years away, so extinction during signal travel is less likely.
