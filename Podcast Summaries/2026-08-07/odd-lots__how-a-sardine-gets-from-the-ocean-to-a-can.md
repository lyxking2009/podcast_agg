# How a Sardine Gets From the Ocean to a Can — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Tracy Alloway and Joe Wisenthal talk tinned fish with Becca Milstein, founder and CEO of Fishwife, against the backdrop of a sardine shortage: Morocco — which exports roughly 50% of the world's sardines — has banned sardine exports on overfishing and plankton-decline concerns. The episode traces the full supply chain from ocean to can, the rise of premium tinned fish, and why anchovies are the strangest product in the category.

## Key points
- The news hook: Morocco has imposed a ban on sardine exports (overfishing; not enough plankton for the sardines — which also means penguins elsewhere don't have enough to eat), and ~90% of the raw material in Spanish sardine canneries came from Morocco; the ban is on frozen exports, protecting raw material for Morocco's own canned-sardine industry.
- Fishwife's origin: Milstein was inspired by tapas culture in Granada, Spain and Lisbon's tinned-fish shops; during COVID the canned fish category grew ~10% YoY as people made beautiful tinned-fish meals at home — "America deserves a better product than I think they'll be willing to pay for."
- The anchovy supply chain (the most complex): purse-seine fishing six miles off Santona, Spain — an entire town of anchovy canneries; fish marked with quality numbers at auction; hand-cleaned, salt-cured in barrels 12-36 months, then laid one at a time in tins with olive oil; not heat-treated, so shelf life is ~1 year vs 5-7 years for the rest of the range.
- Zero to one: 2021 started with a tiny micro-cannery in Oregon (sports-fishermen scale, no minimum order quantities), then a Washington State cannery, then a family-owned Spanish cannery (est. 1940s, three generations) — European family firms were baffled by Fishwife's 3-4x YoY volume growth vs their comfortable 5% growth.
- Supply chain V2 (2023): rebuilt for "essentially unlimited scale in the American market" — canneries capable of 80-100M cans a year; the hard part of high-growth brands in long-lead industries is demand planning: "we're trying to tell our canneries what we need a year ahead and six months ahead and three months ahead."
- Why US canneries disappeared: FDA rules ~15 years ago, Pacific sardine/anchovy fishery declines, and cheap processing in Asia (much Alaskan salmon processing goes through China) — the classic American manufacturing hollowing-out story.
- Sourcing: Fishwife uses MSC (Marine Stewardship Council)-certified fisheries — Cornish sardines (the only MSC-certified European fishery when they started), and the Iberian fishery after it got MSC-certified last year; they never sourced from Morocco.

## Overview
The episode is an Odd Lots supply-chain classic: one commodity, from fishery to shelf. Milstein explains how premium tinned fish went from a European romantic aesthetic to a fast-growing American category, how a startup with zero fish experience broke into a centuries-old, family-owned European industry, and how the Moroccan export ban exposes the fragility of a global supply chain where most of Spain's sardine raw material comes from one country.

## Implications
- Supply chains: geographic concentration (Morocco = ~50% of world sardines) means single-country policy shocks move global prices; expect substitution toward MSC-certified alternative fisheries.
- Consumers: the Moroccan ban will squeeze sardine supply; anchovies remain the premium, fragile end of the category.
- Startups: long-lead industries punish high-growth brands — demand planning and multi-year commitments to suppliers are the real moat.
- Sustainability: certification (MSC) is becoming a sourcing standard that both de-risks supply chains and commands premium positioning.
