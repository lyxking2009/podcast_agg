# Cosmic Queries – Life on Other Planets with Aomawa Shields

**Show:** StarTalk Radio
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** web_podscripts_startalkradio

## Key Points

- Neil deGrasse Tyson and Chuck Nice host astrobiologist Dr. Aomawa Shields, an associate professor of physics and astronomy at UC Irvine and founder of Rising Star Girls, to discuss the search for habitable exoplanets and her new memoir, "Life on Other Planets: A Memoir of Finding My Place in the Universe."
- Shields explains that her research begins after exoplanets are discovered — nearly 6,000 known so far — and focuses on modeling which of them could be habitable, meaning warm enough to host liquid water on their surface, since all known Earth life requires it. She notes the field is inherently Earth-biased, and that astrobiologists also study what "life as we do not know it" might look like using non-water solvents or non-carbon chemistry.
- Much of her career centers on M dwarf stars, which are cooler, smaller, and redder than the Sun but make up the majority of stars in the galaxy. Because they are cooler, the habitable ("Goldilocks") zone sits much closer to the star, which increases tidal forces and can lock a planet into synchronous rotation — one permanently sunlit side and one permanently dark side.
- Shields and her team, including postdoc Anna Lobo, published new research showing that even tidally locked planets could support life along the "Terminator" — the twilight boundary between the permanent day and night sides — using Earth climate models (the same models used to forecast climate change) adapted with different stellar spectra, orbital shapes, and atmospheric compositions.
- Neil gives a short tutorial on stellar spectral classification (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) and luminosity classes, explaining the Sun is a G2 star, and Shields notes M dwarfs' long "terrible twos" phase of intense X-ray and UV flares, which could confine surface life to protected niches like ocean depths.
- Shields discusses her unconventional path — leaving her astronomy PhD after a professor suggested she "consider other career options," earning an MFA in acting at UCLA (where she met her husband, also an actor), and eventually returning to finish her doctorate — and how theater training now helps her communicate science and handle audience Q&A.
- She describes Rising Star Girls, a program she started to bring astronomy and creative-arts projects (poetry, constellation myths, designing hypothetical exoplanets) to middle-school girls of color, aiming to build personal ownership of science before girls disengage from STEM.
- In the Patreon "Cosmic Queries" segment, listeners ask about the most promising places for life in the solar system (Shields ranks Europa, then Enceladus, then subsurface Mars), whether starlight color affects habitability (yes — it shapes atmospheric chemistry and surface climate), and panspermia (plausible but underfunded as a research area).
- A listener question about the ethics of scientific discovery leads to a reflective exchange: Shields says she'd want any contact with extraterrestrial life to proceed "from a place of love, kindness, inclusivity, and welcome," drawing a parallel to the harm caused by colonization on Earth, while Neil and Chuck riff on the alien-contact scenario.

## Overview

This episode is a return visit for astrobiologist Dr. Aomawa Shields, who previously appeared on StarTalk to discuss terraforming Mars. This time the conversation centers on her ongoing exoplanet habitability research and her newly published memoir. Shields explains that with nearly 6,000 exoplanets now catalogued, her work — and that of many astrobiologists — begins after discovery, using adapted Earth climate models to estimate which of these worlds could sustain liquid water. She is candid about the field's Earth-centric bias while noting parallel efforts to define "life as we do not know it."

A major thread is her team's research on M dwarf stars, the small, cool, red stars that make up roughly 70% of the galaxy's stellar population. Because their habitable zones sit much closer in, planets there are prone to tidal locking, producing permanent day and night hemispheres. Shields and her postdoc Anna Lobo's new paper, "Terminator Habitable," models a narrow twilight band between the two hemispheres that could stay in a temperature range hospitable to liquid water — an idea the hosts play with at length, including the pun on "the Terminator."

The episode also traces Shields' personal journey, the subject of her memoir. She describes leaving her original astronomy PhD program after feeling alienated as one of few women of color in the department, pursuing an MFA in acting at UCLA, and eventually returning to finish her doctorate with Neil's encouragement. She frames the book as reassurance that pursuing multiple passions, and returning to abandoned dreams, is never "too late." This personal narrative connects directly to her Rising Star Girls program, which uses creative-arts activities to build personal connection to astronomy for middle-school girls of color.

The Patreon cosmic-queries segment covers a range of listener questions: the best solar system candidates for life (Europa, Enceladus, and subsurface Mars), whether stellar color affects habitability, UV radiation risks around M dwarfs, panspermia, and the value of diverse perspectives in science. The episode closes on a more philosophical note, with a listener asking about the ethics of scientific discovery and possible contact with extraterrestrial life, prompting Shields to reflect on humility, kindness, and the lessons of Earth's own colonial history.

## Implications

For listeners interested in astrobiology and exoplanet science, this episode offers a concrete look at how habitability modeling actually works: astronomers don't just discover planets, they retool existing Earth climate models — the same ones used for terrestrial climate-change forecasting — to test hypothetical atmospheres, orbits, and stellar spectra. The "Terminator Habitable" concept is a useful update to popular assumptions that tidally locked planets around red dwarfs are automatically uninhabitable, widening the plausible search space for future biosignature missions. The discussion of M dwarf UV/X-ray activity is also a practical caveat for evaluating headlines about nearby "Earth-like" exoplanets, many of which orbit red dwarfs. More broadly, Shields' framing of astrobiology as inherently interdisciplinary (climate science, glaciology, geology, atmospheric chemistry) illustrates how progress in this area depends on borrowing tools from adjacent fields rather than working in isolation.
