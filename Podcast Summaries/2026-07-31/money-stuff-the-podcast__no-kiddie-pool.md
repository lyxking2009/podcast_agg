# No Kiddie Pool

**Show:** Money Stuff: The Podcast
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points
- **Situational Awareness gets margin called.** Leopold Aschenbrenner's hedge fund, Situational Awareness (named after his 2024 essay series predicting AI would be huge), had returned roughly 1,000% since inception on long AI/memory-chip bets and short software bets. In the last couple of weeks a drawdown in AI-related trades triggered margin calls from his prime brokers, forcing him to offload a large chunk of his public stock positions. Citadel ended up buying much of that stock at a discount; Aschenbrenner still retains a large private stake in Anthropic. Reported assets under management fell to around $10 billion. Notably, the positions he was forced to sell had already recovered in price by the time of recording, meaning Citadel likely made a quick profit simply by having "strong hands" (longer-term, more secure funding) able to absorb stock from a "weak hands" seller facing margin pressure.
- **Leverage and short-term financing, not being "wrong," caused the blowup.** Matt Levine argues Aschenbrenner's underlying bets weren't mistaken — they're up hugely since the start of the year — but that funding them with leverage via short-term margin debt made the position fragile. He contrasts this with Citadel's institutional approach of securing long-term, non-callable funding (e.g., issuing bonds) and cultivating a risk-management reputation so prime brokers extend patience during drawdowns. A ~1,000% two-year return is described as itself a symptom of excess leverage: an unlevered version of the same bets would have returned something like 200%, which would have been "good enough" and left no need to lever up further.
- **Comparison to Sam Bankman-Fried's Anthropic stake.** Levine draws a parallel to SBF, who was effectively an early, savvy venture investor in Anthropic (an ~8% stake now worth tens of billions), but funded that bet with the worst possible short-term financing — customer funds from his crypto exchange — leading to its forced liquidation in bankruptcy. Aschenbrenner, by contrast, borrowed from banks on normal prime brokerage terms, which is far more legitimate, but structurally creates the same risk: a long-duration, high-conviction bet financed with short-duration, recallable money can be blown out before it plays out, regardless of whether the original thesis was correct.
- **Exoma's CVR record-date mix-up.** Exoma, a biotech royalty company involved in ongoing litigation, sold itself to Ligand for $39/share in cash plus a Contingent Value Right (CVR) tied to the litigation outcome (worth an estimated ~$5 in expected value). Days before the merger closed on July 14, Exoma issued a press release stating the CVR "record date" was July 13 — the day before closing — implying that because stock trades settle T+1, buyers on July 13 wouldn't actually hold shares until July 14 and thus wouldn't receive the CVR, only the cash. This contradicted normal merger mechanics (where whoever holds stock at closing gets whatever the merger consideration is) and contradicted the original merger proxy. Nasdaq itself told traders they needed to own the stock by Friday, July 10 to get the CVR, causing the stock to trade around $40 (between the CVR-inclusive and cash-only values) as traders disagreed about the correct interpretation. Two days after closing, Exoma issued a corrective press release walking the record date back, confirming mergers close when they close regardless of any stated "record date." Traders who bought expecting to receive the CVR anyway appear to have gotten it.
- **This is a recurring pattern at Exoma specifically.** Levine notes Exoma previously acquired another biotech company that made a similarly ambiguous, self-contradictory press release about an "X-date" (the date after which a right is not attached to the stock) — the release simultaneously implied investors would and would not get the associated payout depending on how it was read, and the stock traded between the two implied values until the company clarified. Levine speculates the confusion stems from companies conflating merger consideration (received automatically at closing) with a "distribution" (which requires establishing a record date, as with a dividend) — treating the CVR handout like a dividend distribution rather than simple merger consideration.
- **A niche trading strategy: finding drafting flaws.** The hosts discuss how ambiguities in merger documents, press releases, and bond indentures create tradeable opportunities — some investors specialize in reading corporate/legal documents more carefully than the company itself (or than other traders) to bet on how ambiguous terms will ultimately be interpreted, sometimes not resolved until years later in litigation. Levine notes this doesn't require a law degree; some of the best "document flaw finders" in finance never attended law school.
- **Structured notes on SpaceX.** Following SpaceX's IPO about a month prior and subsequent stock decline below its IPO price, banks including Morgan Stanley are marketing structured notes linked to SpaceX to retail/high-net-worth clients. The marketed pitch is "SpaceX exposure with downside protection," but Levine argues the real economic function is the opposite: banks use these notes to source out-of-the-money puts on SpaceX from retail investors, because retail clients will buy an oddly-structured note without realizing they're effectively selling insurance, whereas professional counterparties either won't sell such deep out-of-the-money puts cheaply (since they're low-probability but very costly if triggered) or aren't reliable counterparties for it (a hedge fund selling that insurance might itself blow up in the scenario where it needs to pay out).
- **How the Morgan Stanley SpaceX note works mechanically.** An investor puts in $100; after roughly 18 months, if SpaceX stock hasn't fallen by more than 50%, the investor gets back a flat $140 (a 40% return) regardless of whether SpaceX rose 39% or 100% — the upside is capped. If SpaceX falls more than 50%, the investor loses money proportionally (e.g., a 60% SpaceX decline returns only $40). From Morgan Stanley's perspective this is equivalent to having bought a put option (insurance) against a SpaceX crash, funded by its retail clients, while marketing it to those clients as a capped-but-protected way to get SpaceX exposure.
- **Structured notes as an "inventory-clearing" business.** Levine describes the broader structured-notes business as banks holding a "bucket" of options exposures and volatility positions they want to offload, then packaging them into products with a compelling story so retail clients will buy them — serving simultaneously as marketing, meeting client demand for particular payout profiles, and clearing the bank's own inventory/risk.

## Overview
This episode of the Money Stuff Podcast features Matt Levine hosting Sylvia Killingsworth (editor at large for Bloomberg Ideas and Culture and author of the "On Books" newsletter) as the first in a planned rotation of guest hosts while regular co-host Katie Greifeld is on parental leave. The conversation moves through three main topics drawn from Levine's recent Money Stuff columns.

The episode opens with the story of Situational Awareness, a hedge fund founded by 25-year-old former OpenAI researcher Leopold Aschenbrenner, which had returned roughly 1,000% over two years on aggressive, leveraged bets on AI's rise before getting margin-called this week amid a broader AI-sector drawdown. Levine uses this to make a broader point about the mismatch between long-term investment theses and short-term financing, drawing an analogy to Sam Bankman-Fried's ill-fated (and illegally funded) early Anthropic investment, and to Citadel's institutional strength in securing durable funding, which let it scoop up Aschenbrenner's forced-sold positions at a discount just before they rebounded.

The middle section covers a quirky corporate-actions story: biotech company Exoma's merger with Ligand involved a Contingent Value Right (CVR) tied to pending litigation, and Exoma's confusing press release about the CVR's "record date" (seemingly at odds with normal merger mechanics and its own merger proxy) caused genuine market confusion and mispriced trading in the days around the deal's closing — a mix-up Levine notes Exoma has now made more than once. Killingsworth presses Levine on the psychology behind why a company would issue such a release, and the two work through the likely explanation (confusing merger consideration with a shareholder "distribution").

The final segment examines structured notes newly launched on SpaceX stock following its post-IPO decline, which Levine deconstructs as a mechanism for banks to buy cheap downside insurance from unsuspecting retail clients while marketing the product as SpaceX exposure with protection. The episode closes with standard sign-off and newsletter/podcast promotion.

## Implications
For investors and market-watchers, the Situational Awareness episode is a reminder that thesis correctness and position survivability are separate risks — a "right" long-term bet financed with short-term, recallable leverage can be forcibly liquidated at the worst possible moment, destroying value that would have materialized had the position simply been held. This has particular relevance for anyone evaluating AI-adjacent hedge funds or leveraged long-duration bets more broadly, and underscores why funding structure (bonds vs. margin debt, reputation with prime brokers) matters as much as the investment thesis itself.

For anyone involved in M&A or holding shares through a merger with contingent consideration (CVRs, earnouts, etc.), the Exoma story is a caution that companies themselves can issue ambiguous or simply incorrect guidance about entitlement mechanics, and that "record dates" don't necessarily apply to merger consideration the way they do to dividends — creating real, tradeable uncertainty and potential for shareholders to be misled about their rights.

For retail and high-net-worth investors being pitched structured notes (on SpaceX or otherwise), the episode is a warning to read the fine print and understand that the marketed "protected exposure" narrative may mask the bank's true objective of offloading unwanted volatility risk (i.e., buying puts) onto the client — the capped upside and uncapped-below-a-threshold downside are the tell.
