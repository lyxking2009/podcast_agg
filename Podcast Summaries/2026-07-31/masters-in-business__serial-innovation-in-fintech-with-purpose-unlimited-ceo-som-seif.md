# Serial Innovation in FinTech with Purpose Unlimited CEO Som Seif

**Show:** Masters in Business
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points

- **Early career and self-awareness.** Som Seif originally wanted to be an architect but was talked out of it and studied Industrial and Systems Engineering at the University of Toronto instead, landing a job in RBC's investment banking structured products group in 1998. He worked 80-100 hour weeks and initially chased money, but at 25 he had a breaking-point moment (crying at 2-3am after a long shift) that led him to realize what actually motivated him was seeing his ideas produce tangible outcomes, similar to what drew him to architecture. He gave himself a "put option": if he was still in his investment banking seat on his 30th birthday, he would resign that day.

- **Founding Claymore Investments (2005).** At 28, Seif started Claymore in a Canadian ETF market that had only 14 ETFs (all from Barclays/iShares) and almost no retail awareness ("what's an ETF?"). He was inspired by a 2004 research paper on non-market-cap-weighted ("fundamental") indexing by Rob Arnott and Jason Hsu, met Arnott in Pasadena, and launched the first public fund using RAFI fundamental indexes. Claymore grew from nothing to about $800M by 2008, then despite the financial crisis kept growing (the firm had positive net sales even in September/October 2008), reaching $1.1B by end of 2008, $4.4B in 2009, $5.7B, then $6.8B, and finally $8B (Canadian) when it was sold to BlackRock in early 2012. Growth accelerated because post-crisis investors re-entering markets sought alternatives to traditional funds, coinciding with advisors shifting toward discretionary/fee-based models where ETFs fit well.

- **The BlackRock sale and emotional difficulty.** Seif's financial partner was Guggenheim Partners; after failing to negotiate a buyout of their stake, he ran a sale process while also trying to be a bidder himself. BlackRock ultimately won as the price rose. Seif described the sale (signed January 2012, closed March 2, 2012) as very hard emotionally — "something was being ripped out of me" — despite pushing himself to be rational rather than let his ego/attachment ("holding on too much") drive the decision. He credits ongoing self-reflection ("am I on the right track? am I pursuing the right goals?") as a discipline the finance industry generally lacks because practitioners tend to be self-focused rather than client-focused.

- **Founding Purpose (and co-founding Wealthsimple) almost immediately after the sale.** Rather than resting, Seif took a mandatory personal-motivation gut-check: he consulted for the regulator briefly, then spent three months unreachable in Southeast Asia with his wife, deciding to move forward with Purpose only if his excitement returned. It did, and he registered Purpose right after returning. Two insights drove Purpose's founding thesis: (1) Claymore's flagship RAFI fund technically outperformed benchmarks by 200bps over five years but investors' actual realized returns lagged badly because of behavioral mistiming (buying/selling at the wrong times) — meaning "beating the market" alone doesn't change client outcomes; (2) exposure to Guggenheim's wealth practice (led by Danny Kahneman-influenced behavioral thinking) convinced him that asset management needed to be outcome-oriented and goal-based rather than purely benchmark-beating.

- **Wealthsimple's founding insight.** Seif diagnosed the industry's reliance on "average economics" — profitable in aggregate because large clients subsidize the perception that small clients are unprofitable — as a structural/technology problem, not an inherent economic one (he cites "ABC" activity-based costing from his engineering coursework). Wealthsimple was built to serve young, small-balance clients well via better infrastructure and unit economics, then scale services as clients' wealth grows. He contrasts it with Robinhood: rather than gamified free trading, Wealthsimple starts with disciplined "manage money" glide-path portfolios and layers in optional services (direct trading, crypto, prediction markets) in a "safe and secure" way so customers don't seek those elsewhere unsupervised. As of the interview, Wealthsimple runs roughly $150B and has higher net deposits than RBC, Canada's largest bank.

- **World's first spot Bitcoin ETF and crypto innovation.** Seif developed a "deep thesis" on crypto starting around 2016 after initial skepticism, making a personal investment to force himself to learn. He was especially excited by Ethereum and in 2018 launched Ether Capital, the first publicly traded corporate vehicle holding Ether on its balance sheet. As custody infrastructure matured, Purpose worked with Canadian regulators for nine months to launch the world's first spot Bitcoin ETF (2021), which crossed $1B in its first month — roughly three years ahead of the SEC approving U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs. Seif frames this as a broader principle: registrants should proactively educate and align with regulators on where an industry needs to go, rather than treat regulation adversarially. Ether Capital was later converted from the corporate structure into an ETF once that structure became viable, incorporating staking.

- **Longevity Pension Fund (2021) — reviving pension-style guaranteed income.** Seif argues the defined-benefit pension plan is "the greatest financial product ever created" because it pools savings and longevity risk institutionally, and its decline over 40 years (in favor of "choice") has left retirees worse off. Purpose's Longevity Pension Fund uses longevity-risk pooling — like a DB pension — inside a mutual fund structure (rather than an insurance/annuity structure that requires going "off book" and is individualized), so investors get pooled lifetime income; institutional investors in the fund include Canadian pension-like entities (referenced as "OMERS" and similar). This targets the industry's neglected "decumulation" phase, as most product innovation historically focused on the accumulation/savings phase.

- **Cash management and option-income ETF innovations.** Purpose (building on Claymore's earlier money-market ETF work) created one of the first ETFs linked directly to a bank deposit account rather than a security, launched around 2014, letting advisors sweep cash at scale with one trade instead of manual account-by-account movements — a product type Seif says the U.S. still lacks (money-market ETFs exist there, but not deposit-linked ones). On options-based income, Seif defends structured, institutional-grade options overlays (e.g., "Yield Shares," 20+ single-name products) as fundamentally sound for optimizing risk-adjusted return streams (e.g., 80% long / 20% covered call on a name like JPMorgan) when executed at scale with a "matrix" of strikes/dates and active tax-efficient rolling — contrasted with unsophisticated single-strike retail options overlays, which he calls low-quality execution. He also notes Canada's lack of a short-term/long-term capital gains distinction simplifies tax handling versus the U.S.

- **Rethinking the 60/40 portfolio and benchmark-driven investing.** Seif argues the industry's faith in 60/40 is a recency-bias artifact: research going back 100+ years found the 60/40 mix hit its long-term ~7% return target in only 5 of roughly 11 decades, and 3 of those 5 favorable decades occurred in the 1980-2020 period of generally falling rates — a period that dominates most practitioners' entire careers. He argues the real driver of good 60/40 outcomes was the 1980s pivot from spiking-and-staying-high rates (1970s-80s) to a 40-year secular decline, not an inherent property of the allocation. His broader critique: benchmark-beating is the wrong customer-facing goal; the only question that matters to a client is "am I going to be okay?" and portfolios/pensions should be designed around answering that, similar to how a DB pension frames a liability/goal against an asset pool. He also argues investors under roughly "T-minus-20-years" from retirement can reasonably skip bonds and take on more equity-like "hope-based" risk, while inside a 20-year runway to retirement, structure and discipline become essential (though the retirement date T can itself flex).

- **AI as an organizational re-platforming, not just a feature set.** Seif says he's never been more energized in his career, comparing the current AI moment to the early-2000s internet transition or mobile era — a chance to redesign how a company works, not just automate discrete tasks. Purpose is restructuring around smaller cross-functional "squads" (forward-deployed engineering, data science, and product embedded across the org) and prioritizing organizational vulnerability, communication, and free-flowing information over the historical model of top-down leadership strength. He criticizes the industry for treating AI merely as an efficiency feature (e.g., automating a task that took an hour down to five minutes) rather than as a platform shift requiring redesigned workflows.

- **Mentors, reading, and career advice.** Seif credits Rob Arnott as a formative mentor who also introduced him to advisors including Harry Markowitz, Peter Bernstein, and Richard Roll. Favorite books include "The Education of an American Dreamer" by Peter G. Peterson, "Creativity, Inc." (which inspired how he'd want his own career story told), "Unreasonable Hospitality," and "Never Split the Difference" by Chris Voss (Purpose recently brought in Voss's team to train staff on negotiation). For young people entering financial services, he emphasizes the industry will roughly double in size over the next decade, encourages framing a career as a "J-curve" with uncomfortable stretches rather than a steady staircase, and argues AI is enabling rather than threatening for new entrants. Looking back, he wishes he had internalized behavioral-finance and customer-outcome principles earlier in his career.

## Overview

This Masters in Business episode features Barry Ritholtz interviewing Som Seif, founder and CEO of Purpose Unlimited (parent of Purpose Investments, roughly $40B CAD across asset management and wealth), tracing his path from an aspiring architect and engineering graduate through RBC investment banking, to founding and selling Claymore Investments to BlackRock, to building Purpose and co-founding Wealthsimple, and then a rapid string of financial product innovations (crypto ETFs, longevity pension funds, deposit-linked cash ETFs, options-income ETFs).

The conversation's throughline is Seif's repeated insistence that he is not chasing entrepreneurship for its own sake but pursuing ideas that change how the industry serves customers. He frames several pivotal decisions — leaving RBC at a self-imposed deadline, selling Claymore despite emotional attachment, restarting immediately with Purpose and Wealthsimple, pushing crypto and longevity products through skeptical regulators — as products of deliberate self-reflection about motivation and alignment with a larger mission of client outcomes rather than benchmark-beating or personal enrichment. A recurring intellectual anchor is behavioral finance: the realization that Claymore's technically superior fundamental-index fund didn't actually improve investors' realized returns because of behavioral mistiming, which reoriented his thinking toward outcome/goal-based design (echoing the defined-benefit pension model) rather than pure performance.

The middle of the conversation surveys Purpose's greatest-hits list of firsts: the world's first spot Bitcoin ETF, the first Ethereum-holding public vehicle, deposit-linked cash ETFs, and the Longevity Pension Fund, which revives DB-pension-style longevity risk pooling inside a mutual fund wrapper. Throughout, Seif emphasizes proactive, collaborative regulatory engagement as a competitive advantage rather than an obstacle. The conversation closes with a debate about whether the 60/40 portfolio's historical reputation is largely a recency-bias artifact of the 1980-2020 rate environment, followed by Seif's enthusiasm about AI as an organizational re-platforming opportunity, and the standard Masters in Business closing segment on mentors, books, entertainment, and career advice.

## Implications

For practitioners, Seif's framing challenges the default industry narrative of benchmark-beating as the core value proposition, arguing instead for goal/outcome-oriented product design and client communication — a shift with implications for how advisors construct portfolios, talk to clients, and evaluate their own performance. His critique of the 60/40 portfolio's historical basis is a useful caution against recency bias for anyone building long-horizon asset allocation models, especially in a higher-and-more-volatile-rate environment. The account of the Longevity Pension Fund and deposit-linked cash ETFs offers a concrete template for U.S. practitioners: several Canadian product innovations (decumulation-focused pooled income funds, deposit-based cash ETFs) don't yet exist in the U.S. market and could represent white space. His account of the Bitcoin ETF regulatory approval process is also a practical case study in how sustained, collaborative regulatory engagement (nine months of work) can produce first-mover advantage. Finally, his description of restructuring Purpose around AI-native "squads" rather than treating AI as a bolt-on feature is a relevant organizational-design signal for any financial services leader currently thinking about AI adoption strategy.
