# Why Private Credit Got Entangled With Insurance

**Show:** Odd Lots
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points
- Hosts Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal frame the episode around a core finance principle: whoever makes a risky investment should bear the loss if it goes bad. The 2008 financial crisis was traumatic precisely because bank losses were socialized onto taxpayers. After 2008, policymakers deliberately pushed risk out of the regulated banking system into private credit vehicles so that losses would be borne by investors rather than socialized — but the guests argue that risk is now migrating again, this time into insurance, another sector where losses are not supposed to be purely private.
- Guests Andrew Granato (assistant professor of law, UT Austin) and Pranjal Drall (JD-PhD candidate in financial economics, Yale) describe the "flywheel" logic behind private-equity ownership of life insurers: a PE firm's buyout arm needs debt financing, its private-credit arm can issue that debt, and its life-insurance arm provides "permanent" patient capital (long-dated liabilities) that can hold illiquid private credit loans without LPs demanding liquidity. Warren Buffett pioneered the insight that insurance float is a great source of patient capital; what's new in the last ~15 years is the scale and aggressiveness with which PE has applied it, with an estimated $750 billion of life insurance assets now within private equity's purview.
- There is a spectrum of control arrangements between insurers and asset managers: from insurers wholly owned/controlled by a PE sponsor (management and affiliated fees flow to the parent) to arm's-length "third-party" asset management agreements where insurers competitively shop for the best private-credit manager. In owned/affiliated structures, insurers pay not just management fees but also fees for accounting, valuation, consulting and other affiliated services, with much of the economic activity happening outside the insurance corporate form itself.
- On competition and consumer outcomes: economists have long puzzled over the "annuity puzzle" — annuities are theoretically a great product but are chronically under-purchased, and evidence suggests buyers don't get great deals even though the market seems competitive. Some empirical evidence suggests PE-owned insurers actually offer better annuity terms to policyholders in the near term (because the parent can profit from private-credit fees and spreads elsewhere), but this may come at the cost of solvency risk decades later — a risk retail policyholders are poorly positioned to evaluate, unlike sophisticated institutional investors in a private credit fund.
- A central regulatory concern is valuation opacity. Private credit assets held on insurer balance sheets are rated not by the familiar public credit-rating agencies but through "private letter ratings" — ratings submitted confidentially to the NAIC (the association of state insurance regulators) that map to a 1-10 NAIC risk-notch scale but are never publicly visible or auditable. New empirical research keeps finding systematic overvaluation of these assets, echoing pre-2008 concerns about credit-rating-agency incentives.
- The paper's central argument concerns how insurer insolvency is handled versus bank insolvency. Banks have the FDIC: a pre-funded, risk-based deposit insurance fund funded by ex-ante quarterly assessments, backstopped ultimately by the U.S. government. Insurance, regulated purely at the state level (via the McCarran-Ferguson Act, which preserved state authority after a 1940s Supreme Court commerce-clause ruling), has no federal regulator and no equivalent of the FDIC. Instead, when a life insurer fails it doesn't go through bankruptcy; its domiciliary state runs a simultaneous state-by-state insolvency proceeding, and each state's guaranty fund pays policyholders up to a statutory cap (roughly $200-300K, varying by state) — but only after the insolvency happens, funded by post-hoc assessments on surviving insurers.
- Because guaranty-fund assessments are levied only after failure, and because roughly 34 states grant surviving insurers a full tax credit against those assessments (paid back over 5-10 years), the arrangement functions as a "stealth" taxpayer bailout that no legislature ever explicitly votes for — unlike a TARP-style vote. The design also fails to risk-weight assessments (they're based purely on premium volume, not investment risk), creating an implicit subsidy for insurers that take on more risk, and a perverse doom-loop risk where a bad insurer's failure levies fees on already-struggling peers.
- The FDIC's ~$250K cap covers most depositors, but the guaranty-fund cap covers only about the 40th percentile of life-insurance policyholders by policy size, since life-insurance buyers skew wealthier and buy larger policies — meaning the backstop is proportionally weaker than deposit insurance even before considering its structural flaws. The system has also never been tested at scale: there has been no failure of a large national life insurer with hundreds of billions in assets.
- Discussion of structural differences between bank runs and insurer failures: banks face correlated risk both in loan books (e.g., housing) and in depositor withdrawal behavior (e.g., SVB), while insurance liabilities are typically staggered/event-triggered so classic "runs" are less likely — except for certain policy types. Whole life policies with cash-value reserve accounts function like demand deposits with withdrawal rights, and there is historical precedent: Executive Life, a life insurer in the early 1990s, experienced an actual run, though at a scale (a few billion dollars) far too small to threaten the financial system. On the asset side, insurers face weaker "line-of-business" risk monitoring than banks (no federal equivalent of bank examiners flagging concentrated industry exposure), and with insurers holding an estimated 10-15% of assets in private credit, correlated sectoral exposure (e.g., to software/SaaS lending) is a live concern.
- "Shadow reinsurance" is described as a major visibility gap: insurers, especially PE-backed ones, often cede assets and liabilities to captive reinsurance subsidiaries domiciled in Bermuda or low-tax U.S. states like Iowa or Vermont. While the NAIC has granular, CUSIP-level visibility into a primary insurer's U.S.-held assets, that visibility disappears entirely once assets are reinsured into a captive.
- Granato and Drall propose several regulatory fixes: a Pigouvian-style capital surcharge on opaque/hard-to-value asset classes rather than trying to individually assess each private credit loan; ending tax credits for guaranty-fund assessments or moving to a pre-funded, risk-based FDIC-style system; and importing a "source of strength doctrine" from bank holding company regulation, under which affiliated entities in an insurance group could be required to help cover guaranty-fund shortfalls, aligning incentives across a PE-insurance group.
- The hosts close by connecting the discussion to a live news story: federal prosecutors are reportedly investigating Mark Walter (owner of the LA Dodgers, and of Guggenheim, which owns affiliated insurers Delaware Life and Clear Spring). Guggenheim's insurers reportedly revised their disclosed share of "affiliated assets" upward from roughly 3-5% to about 40% following scrutiny — illustrating in real time the opacity concerns discussed throughout the episode.

## Overview
This episode of Odd Lots tackles the growing entanglement between private equity/private credit and the life insurance industry, hosted by Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal with guests Andrew Granato (UT Austin law professor) and Pranjal Drall (Yale JD-PhD candidate), authors of the paper "Private Credit's State Backstop: How Private Equity Socializes Risk Through Insurers." The conversation opens with a framing device: financial systems work best when whoever takes investment risk also bears the losses, and 2008 was traumatic because bank losses were socialized onto taxpayers. Post-2008 reforms deliberately pushed risky lending out of banks into private credit funds so that investors, not the public, would absorb losses. The hosts and guests argue this same risk is now flowing into a second regulated, quasi-publicly-backstopped sector: insurance.

The middle of the conversation explains the mechanics and incentives of the PE-insurance-private credit "flywheel," where a private equity firm's buyout, private-credit, and life-insurance arms all reinforce one another, with insurers' patient, long-dated capital providing an ideal holding vehicle for illiquid private credit loans. The guests detail the spectrum from arm's-length third-party asset-management agreements to fully captured insurer balance sheets, and discuss how opaque, unauditable "private letter ratings" allow private credit assets on insurer books to be systematically overvalued relative to their true risk, drawing parallels to pre-2008 credit-rating-agency failures.

The heart of the episode, and the paper, is a detailed comparison of how bank failures versus insurer failures are handled. Banks have the FDIC — a pre-funded, risk-weighted federal backstop. Insurers instead rely on state-level guaranty funds that pay out only after failure, funded by assessments on survivors that are largely offset by state tax credits, amounting to an unlegislated "stealth" taxpayer bailout. The guests also unpack differences in run risk (insurers are less runnable than banks on the liability side, with some exceptions like cash-value whole life policies), asset-side correlation risk, and the visibility problems created by "shadow reinsurance" through captives in low-tax domiciles like Bermuda, Iowa, and Vermont. The episode closes with proposed reforms (valuation surcharges, ending guaranty-fund tax credits, a "source of strength doctrine" for insurance groups) and ties the discussion to breaking news about a federal investigation into Guggenheim-affiliated insurers Delaware Life and Clear Spring, where affiliated-asset disclosures were revised from roughly 3-5% up to about 40%.

## Implications
For practitioners and listeners tracking private credit and insurance, the episode is a warning that the "safety valve" narrative around private credit — that it moved risk to parties who could bear losses — may be partly illusory if that risk is re-concentrating on insurer balance sheets backed by a weaker, less-tested, and less transparent public backstop than banking's FDIC system. Policyholders, regulators, and even sophisticated allocators evaluating insurer-linked assets should scrutinize affiliated-party transactions, private letter ratings, and reinsurance/captive structures, since NAIC oversight has real visibility gaps once assets move offshore into captives. The Guggenheim/Delaware Life/Clear Spring disclosure revision (3% to 40% affiliated assets) suggests these concerns are moving from academic papers into active regulatory and law-enforcement attention, which could presage tighter valuation rules, guaranty-fund reform, or new capital surcharges on opaque asset classes.
