# Building an Autonomous Enterprise for Real-World Services with Netic Founder Melisa Tokmak

**Show:** No Priors: Artificial Intelligence | Technology | Startups
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** youtube_autocaptions

## Key Points
- Melisa Tokmak, founder and CEO of Netic, describes the company as building AI to run "millions of real world businesses that keep the world running." Netic works with large enterprises in essential services — billion-dollar-revenue home services companies in HVAC, plumbing, electric, consumer wellness, hospitality, automotive, and pet services — sitting between the company and its customers to understand customer needs and match them to the operational rules of the business, including deploying labor/services.
- Netic's agents (voice, text, web chat) handle the full front-office workflow: when a customer calls, e.g., an HVAC provider, Netic's AI determines the type of home, prior records, urgency, technician skill match, and customer lifetime value to decide who should be dispatched and when. Today over 70% of Netic customers' end-users have their first interaction with a "Netic-first" AI agent rather than a human.
- Tokmak explains why she built a scalable product company rather than pursuing an AI roll-up (buying and optimizing existing businesses with AI, as firms like Long Lake do), citing three reasons: (1) she wants tangible real-world impact rather than serving only tech/startup customers, drawing on her own background growing up in a small town in Turkey with a full scholarship to Stanford; (2) her skill set is building products/engineering, not M&A; and (3) roll-up products are typically built for the specific acquired companies and don't generalize, whereas she wants any real-world business to be able to run on Netic's platform.
- Before Netic, Tokmak spent about four years at Scale AI as a director of engineering, building business units across government, logistics, manufacturing, financial services, and healthcare. She says Scale gave her exposure to real-world business complexity but lacked a compounding "product" — the infrastructure work was operationally heavy but not itself a scalable product — which pushed her to start Netic to combine real-world impact with the technical challenge of fully autonomous execution of mission-critical workflows (versus AI as a consumer co-pilot).
- On robotics convergence: Tokmak argues robotics for these industries (HVAC, plumbing, roofing, etc.) is "quite far" in the future because of the enormous physical variation in buildings, the dexterity required for tasks like navigating walls or handling different screws/fixtures, and the human/emotional element of service calls, which often occur on people's worst days (flooded homes, no heat in extreme cold).
- On frontier AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta) as competitive threats: Tokmak does not see them as a competitive risk to Netic. She argues labs optimize for the most generalizable solution to a problem ("when we get AGI we'll ask how to solve it for essential services"), which she calls operationally and intellectually "a bit lazy" for messy vertical domains. She argues companies like Netic must excel at three layers — models, harnesses/orchestration, and the product/software built on top — to handle the "last mile" of serving millions of customers with different accents, contexts, and needs.
- Tokmak contrasts today's founder culture with what she considers a more committed approach: she worries many founders now build with an eye toward quick acquisition/exit rather than dedicating years to a mission, and that "shiny object" chasing and a "permanent underclass" anxiety (fear of missing an 18-month AI window) undermine the patience needed to build durable products. She references a Martin Luther quote about craftsmanship (a shoemaker honors God not with crosses on shoes but by making the best shoes) to describe her hiring and building philosophy.
- On hiring, Tokmak screens for "agency" by asking candidates about the hardest thing they've ever done and digging into whether they've shown sustained follow-through throughout their life (in school, projects, or personal circumstances), not just a single anecdote. She shared an example of a new hire whose answer centered on maintaining a strict personal health regimen consistently for 15+ years.
- Netic's five-year vision is to become a fully "autonomous enterprise" layer — automating everything in these companies except the actual service delivery and labor itself, so the human/blue-collar labor component can focus purely on service quality while Netic's compounding intelligence layer runs the rest. She also emphasizes wanting to keep working with "remarkable people" long-term, citing companies like Palantir, SpaceX, and Notion as examples that have prioritized craftsmanship over time.
- Tokmak pushes back on the perception that essential-services industries (HVAC, roofing, plumbing) are "old school" or slow to adopt technology, describing some of the most tech-forward business owners she's met as being in these industries. She cites a half-million-dollar contract closed end-to-end in 14 days as evidence of buyer sophistication, and describes how Netic connects to satellite data (e.g., to track hurricane damage to roofs by neighborhood) to help roofing companies proactively identify high-value leads and better prepare technicians, not just handle inbound calls.
- On private equity and AI: Tokmak says the classic PE playbook of finding an undervalued "gem," improving the team, and flipping it has largely disappeared because such opportunities no longer exist undiscovered. The new playbook is creating tangible value in owned businesses. She notes PE conversations with Netic still often start focused on cost-cutting, but she steers them toward net-new revenue generation, citing that Netic has generated over $600 million in customer revenue from AI-handled interactions as proof rather than relying on demos alone. She also notes that private equity firms increasingly hire AI-focused operating partners or engineers to evaluate AI vendors and educate portfolio companies, though they typically act as advisors to portfolio companies rather than mandating direct AI adoption from the top down.
- Asked what she's most excited about in AI beyond Netic, Tokmak highlighted education and access — pointing to her own experience finding informal mentors online from her hometown as a student without resources, and hoping AI will similarly expand access and agency for people who otherwise wouldn't have opportunities. She also expressed hope that AI narratives shift from being centered on job loss/negativity toward AI's positive impact, especially in domains like healthcare and education where people need help on difficult days.

## Overview
This episode of No Priors features Melisa Tokmak, founder and CEO of Netic, a company building AI systems that run front-office and operational workflows for large "essential services" businesses — home services companies in HVAC, plumbing, electric, consumer wellness, hospitality, automotive, and pet care. Tokmak describes Netic as sitting between these companies and their millions of end customers, using voice, text, and chat AI agents to understand customer needs, match them against a company's operational rules, and coordinate labor/service deployment — replacing the large human support teams these EBITDA-focused, often private-equity-owned businesses have historically relied on. She explains that over 70% of Netic's customer base now has end-users interacting with AI agents first.

The conversation covers Tokmak's path from Scale AI, where she spent four years as a director of engineering building business units across government and large enterprise verticals, to founding Netic roughly two years ago. She explains her decision to build a horizontally scalable product company rather than pursue an "AI roll-up" strategy (buying and optimizing existing businesses), citing her engineering/product skill set, her personal motivation to build for the real world rather than only for tech companies, and the belief that roll-up products don't generalize across acquired businesses. A significant portion of the discussion addresses how Netic differs from robotics-based automation (which she views as far off for these industries due to physical variation and human/emotional factors) and from potential competition by frontier AI labs, which she argues optimize for generalizable solutions rather than the deep vertical "last mile" work Netic focuses on across models, orchestration, and product layers.

The episode also explores Tokmak's views on founder culture and hiring — she expresses concern about founders optimizing for fast exits rather than long-term commitment, and describes her hiring philosophy of screening for sustained "agency" through candidates' life stories. Later, she discusses how private equity's playbook has shifted from finding undervalued assets to flip, toward creating tangible operational value in owned portfolio companies, and describes typical PE engagements with Netic evolving from cost-cutting-focused conversations to revenue-generation focused ones, citing the $600 million in AI-generated customer revenue Netic reports. The conversation closes with Tokmak's optimism about AI's potential to expand access and agency, particularly in education, drawing on her own personal story of growing up without resources in a small town in Turkey before attending Stanford on a full scholarship.

## Implications
For practitioners in AI, private equity, and vertical software, the episode suggests that "essential services" verticals (home services, wellness, roofing, HVAC) represent a large, underappreciated market where full front-office automation — not just chat/copilot assistance — is already generating substantial measurable revenue ($600M+ cited), and where buyer sophistication and adoption speed (a 14-day, half-million-dollar sales cycle) may be faster than commonly assumed. For founders building vertical AI companies, Tokmak's framing offers a differentiation thesis against frontier labs: labs optimize for generalizable solutions, while durable vertical value comes from combining model capability with deep operational "last mile" orchestration and product work specific to a domain's edge cases. For private equity and portfolio company operators, the shift she describes — from a cost-cutting-first mentality toward AI-driven net-new revenue generation — signals a maturing framework for evaluating AI vendors beyond demos, and a growing organizational pattern of dedicated AI operating partners inside PE firms. Her comments on robotics timelines (calling near-term robotic automation of these physical trades unlikely) are a useful data point for investors weighing robotics versus software-agent bets in physical-world service industries.
