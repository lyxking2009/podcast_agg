## Key Points
- Dan Nathan and Guy Adami open by dissecting a volatile Thursday: Microsoft up 15% and Meta down ~9% post-earnings, huge bounces in semis/memory (Western Digital, SanDisk, Applied Materials, CoreWeave, Nebius), and software getting hit. Guy argues the action "suggests more of a topping formation in the market than a bottoming formation," while Dan flags that many of these moves, even after 30-40% pullbacks from highs, still "scream bubble" — citing CoreWeave's round trip from $138 in May to a 22%-up day near $74.
- On software, they debate whether money flowing out of the AI/semis trade and into names like Salesforce, Adobe, Workday and ServiceNow is a durable rotation or another head-fake bounce. Dan argues sentiment swung from too euphoric last fall to too pessimistic now, and thinks IGV (currently low-90s) can retrace toward the ~115 level seen last fall if earnings season avoids big misses/guide-downs. Guy counters that looking "under the hood," IGV's construction (Palantir ~9.4%, Microsoft ~8.3%, Palo Alto ~8%) means a 15% Microsoft rally barely moves the ETF, which he reads as less bullish than it looks.
- The hosts cover a rotation out of tech into consumer staples/retail that Liz Thomas (SoFi) flagged earlier in the week as defensive positioning. Guy is skeptical of retail broadly given a soft GDP print, rising rates pressuring the consumer, and rising delinquency rates, and expects retailers to keep getting sold into rallies even as he still likes Walmart defensively (despite its own weak recent trading).
- Discussion of Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's post-meeting commentary and the bond-market selloff it triggered, referencing an Axios piece ("Walsh talks bonds puke... Worsh's credibility problem") noting Warsh reaffirmed the Fed's 2% inflation target even as yields rose. Guy thinks Warsh is trying to pull back decades of heavy Fed communication (which he views as not necessarily bad) but is "painting themselves into a corner" on a 2% target he sees as structurally unrealistic; he notes the Fed controls the front end of the curve while "the market controls everything else," and the market is telling a different story than the administration wants (lower yields).
- Bank of Japan currency intervention gets flagged as a second major macro thread: dollar-yen reportedly dropped from about 162 to 158 "in the blink of an eye," which Guy calls "eerily reminiscent" of July 2024, when a similar yen move preceded a volatility spike — he expects history could repeat. Separately, Dan raises whether a potential US dollar index breakout (from a range with resistance near 100-101, after falling from ~108-109 to ~98) could become a tailwind for US multinational earnings, citing FactSet's John Butters: companies generating >50% of revenue outside the US posted 73.6% Q2 earnings growth vs. 23% for domestically-focused companies, with Google, Exxon and Chevron top contributors.
- On banks, they discuss Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley pulling back ~15-16% from all-time highs alongside SpaceX's post-IPO weakness (SpaceX priced at 135, traded as low as ~110, back to ~114) since both banks led that underwriting syndicate. Guy thinks Goldman's pullback is overdone and a "run-of-the-mill selloff," and in a "would you rather," prefers investment banks (Goldman/Morgan Stanley) over money-center banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America) given consumer and rate pressure risk to the latter.
- On China, the hosts note Chinese tech/chip progress (DeepSeek, Huawei, and a new memory maker, CXMT, going public in Shanghai — reportedly with Apple lobbying the US administration to be able to buy its memory) as evidence China is "closing the gap" on AI models while operating with lower capital and energy costs than US hyperscalers. They flag KWEB as breaking out of a downtrend in place since October, above its 200-day moving average (~32), after four years of range-bound trading, and note FXI as the vehicle for a broader China view.
- Briefly on gold: with the metal holding the $4,000 level and its 200-day moving average (~4,500) flattening, Guy argues sentiment has swung from too bullish earlier in the year to too bearish now, expects Bank of Japan-driven volatility and the bond selloff to eventually become tailwinds for gold, and likes GDX as it nears breaking a 6-7 month downtrend.
- The core interview: Dan sits down with Tim McCourt (Senior Managing Director and Global Head of Equity, FX and Alternative Products, CME Group) at CME's Chicago headquarters to discuss CME's newly launched single stock futures. McCourt frames the launch timing (during Q2 earnings season) as deliberate — it showcases real-time price discovery and risk transfer around earnings reactions, in contrast to the opaque, thin, "wild west" after-hours/pre-market trading most investors currently rely on.
- McCourt explains the product covers 55 of the largest US-listed names with a 100-share multiplier (aligning with standard listed options), plus 22 names available as "micro" single stock futures with a 10-share multiplier for more precise, retail-friendly position sizing (and combinable with standard contracts for non-100-share delta hedging). Key structural benefits he cites: near-24-hour trading (Sunday night through Friday) alongside E-mini/micro E-mini futures, capital efficiency via margin (a regulatory/statutory minimum of 15%, with the exchange's indicative range roughly 15-28% depending on the name) instead of full payment or Reg T 50% margin, and simplified short-selling mechanics — no locate, no borrow, no uptick/Reg SHO-style rules, since selling to open and selling to close are the same transaction type.
- McCourt discusses distribution: CME has more than 130 retail partners globally (Dan references CME's presence at Robinhood's summit and Robinhood's tens of millions of funded accounts) and sees single stock futures potentially becoming a new entry point into futures trading — inverting the traditional path where traders start with stocks, move to ETFs, then options, and only "graduate" to futures later. He closes by calling the launch "a pivotal change in our strategy at CME Group" and says he'll return to share early usage/uptake data.

## Overview
This RiskReversal Pod episode opens with Dan Nathan and Guy Adami reacting live to a turbulent Thursday of earnings-driven moves — Microsoft surging 15%, Meta falling roughly 9%, and sharp swings across semiconductors, memory and software names. Guy frames the action as more consistent with a market topping than bottoming, while Dan highlights how extreme single-stock volatility (8-10% moves multiple times a week for months) has become the market's defining, and to him concerning, feature. The two debate whether beaten-down software names are setting up for a sustained rally into the back half of earnings season or are simply bouncing within an unhealthy structure, illustrated by their disagreement over what a 15% Microsoft rally really means for a concentration-heavy ETF like IGV.

From there the conversation widens into macro: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's post-meeting commentary and the bond market's skeptical reaction (yields moving higher despite Warsh's reaffirmed 2% inflation target), a Bank of Japan currency intervention that dropped dollar-yen sharply and that Guy compares to the volatility-triggering intervention of July 2024, and a possible US dollar breakout that could flip from a multinational-earnings headwind into a tailwind, supported by FactSet data showing internationally-exposed S&P 500 companies posting far stronger Q2 earnings growth than domestically-focused ones. They also cover Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley's pullback tied to SpaceX's rocky post-IPO trading, a rotation into Chinese tech (KWEB, FXI) on evidence China is closing the AI/chip gap with lower-cost approaches, and gold's prospects above the $4,000 level.

The back half of the episode is a sit-down interview Dan recorded in Chicago with Tim McCourt of CME Group, timed to the exchange's newly launched single stock futures on 55 major US names (plus 22 "micro" contracts with a smaller multiplier). McCourt walks through why CME launched during earnings season — to showcase real-time, transparent price discovery around earnings reactions that today mostly happens in opaque after-hours markets — and details the product's mechanics: near-24-hour trading alongside CME's existing index futures, capital-efficient margining (a 15% regulatory floor versus full payment or Reg T margin for stock), and simplified short mechanics without locates or uptick-rule complications. He positions single stock futures as complementary to, not a replacement for, stocks and options, useful for hedging, tax-efficient position trimming, and precise delta management alongside options positions.

McCourt also discusses distribution strategy, noting CME's 130-plus retail broker partnerships (including Robinhood, whose tens of millions of funded accounts Dan calls out) and describing an ambition to become traders' first exposure to futures rather than the last stop in a "stocks to ETFs to options to futures" progression. He closes by calling the launch a "pivotal change" in CME's strategy and commits to returning with early usage data.

## Implications
For active traders and portfolio managers, the episode underscores that single-stock volatility and earnings-season dispersion have become dominant market features that traditional after-hours/pre-market venues handle poorly — McCourt's pitch is that CME's single stock futures fill that transparency and access gap, giving traders a regulated, capital-efficient way to react to earnings in real time (nights, weekends, pre/post-market) without the borrow, locate, and uptick-rule friction of shorting stock. Options traders in particular get a new hedging/delta-management tool (100-share standard and 10-share micro contracts) that can be combined for precise non-round-lot hedges. On the macro side, the Warsh/bond-market tension and the Bank of Japan's yen intervention are both flagged as volatility catalysts practitioners should watch — Guy's July 2024 parallel suggests positioning for a possible volatility spike in the weeks ahead — while the dollar's potential breakout is framed as a variable that could start favoring internationally-exposed large caps (Google, Exxon, Chevron) over domestically-focused ones.
