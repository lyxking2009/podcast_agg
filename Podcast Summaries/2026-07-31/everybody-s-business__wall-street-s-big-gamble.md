# Wall Street's Big Gamble

**Show:** Everybody's Business
**Date:** 2026-07-31
**Transcript source:** rss_omny_srt

## Key Points
- The "Magnificent Seven" has stopped trading as a coherent group, according to Bloomberg Senior Editor Eric Wiener. AI has split the cohort: Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta are the big AI spenders; Nvidia is the chipmaker supplying them; Tesla is trying to get into AI without spending much; and Apple isn't spending on AI at all. As a result, Apple is outperforming this year precisely because it lacks AI exposure, Microsoft is rallying on strong cloud/AI revenue traction, and Meta is "getting killed" because investors don't believe in its AI/metaverse bets and it lacks a clear AI revenue stream. Alphabet, once seen as an AI afterthought, rebounded once Gemini gained traction and the market realized it owns the dominant search distribution channel for AI.
- Wiener argued the economy looks fine at the "top line" but is shakier underneath, because AI valuations are being priced as if adoption will be linear and near-term, which doesn't match historical technology-adoption patterns. The real risk he flagged is a liquidity crunch: if interest rates rise and stock prices fall enough that AI-spending companies can't raise capital via new stock issuance, the money that flows down into chipmakers, data-center construction, and broader economic growth could dry up, tightening markets and slowing commerce.
- The Federal Reserve, under new chair Kevin Warsh, held interest rates steady at its latest meeting despite pressure from Trump to cut. Markets read the outcome as a "hawkish hold" — three of twelve voting members dissented in favor of raising rates, an unusually high number that suggests some Fed officials lean toward hiking. Wiener contrasted this with a prior meeting where only one member dissented and it was called "Fed chaos," suggesting under Warsh three dissents are being treated as comparatively muted. He noted Warsh has also stopped giving the kind of forward guidance markets are used to (e.g., signaling likely future hike counts), leaving traders without a clear read on the path ahead, which itself creates volatility.
- Elon Musk claimed in an interview (reportedly with The Economist) that by 2036 the economy could be five times its current size and people may have no need to save for retirement due to AI-driven "abundance." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, responding in a Sean Hannity interview, said he'd push Musk's timeline out further but broadly endorsed the idea that AI is building an economy people "can't even imagine," citing that 25% of today's jobs didn't exist in 2000. Hosts Stacey Vanek Smith and Max Chafkin pushed back, noting Musk has a strong record as an innovator but a poor record on timelines, and asked Wiener directly about the inconsistency of AI executives promising abundance while simultaneously conducting layoffs.
- Wiener drew a distinction between the current AI boom and the dot-com bubble: in the dot-com era, people genuinely believed flawed narratives (e.g., that Pets.com was a "technology company"). He argued today's participants don't fully believe their own hype — if they did, their actions (layoffs, hedging) would look different. He agreed technology does dislocate workers and reshape the economy over time (citing automobiles replacing buggy whips) but called the idea that AI will "reinvent the entire economy" imminently "quite hyperbolic," pointing out that 30-year bond markets are still pricing in ~5% yields, implying investors expect money (and the traditional economy) to still exist decades from now.
- Ori Zohar, co-founder of the spice company Burlap and Barrel, described suing the Trump administration over tariffs — the company's second such lawsuit. He walked through three successive tariff regimes since "Liberation Day": IEEPA tariffs (ruled unlawful, refunded with interest after the company won at the Court of International Trade, now on appeal), and a newer round of "Section 301" tariffs introduced after the prior emergency tariffs expired, which the company is also suing over. Burlap and Barrel imports spices (e.g., Vietnamese cinnamon, Afghan cumin, herbs from Provence) from about 30 countries because those crops don't grow domestically, yet has paid hundreds of thousands of dollars in tariffs, including on goods with no US-grown alternative. Roughly 471 carve-outs have since been issued (e.g., cinnamon and black pepper are now exempt), which Zohar said itself demonstrates how "shoddy" the tariff framework is.
- Zohar explained the company partnered with the nonprofit legal group Liberty Justice Center, which supplies the legal expertise and funds the litigation (he noted a Supreme Court-level tariff case can cost millions in legal fees) — the group approached Burlap and Barrel after the company filed an amicus brief, then asked them to serve as plaintiff for subsequent tariff challenges. Zohar said the company made a public pledge during Liberation Day not to pass tariff costs onto either its overseas partner farmers or American customers, comparing the decision to a "Patagonia moment" of using legal action to change policy rather than seek damages. He said the tariffs have paradoxically forced the company to cut costs domestically (hiring, shipping, packaging) rather than abroad, since it can't easily substitute imported specialty ingredients. He also noted the issue crosses political lines — the Liberty Justice Center's head voted for Trump three times, while Zohar's company describes itself as "crunchy liberal" — and that customer reaction has been mostly positive, with some pushback along the lines of "keep politics out of my spices."
- The "This Week in Prediction Markets" segment covered a dispute between prediction-market company Kalshi and Wisconsin election officials. Bloomberg reporter Denitsa Tsekova explained Wisconsin issued an advisory, based on an 1849 state law, warning that voters who place a bet on the outcome of an election they also vote in could have their vote disqualified. Kalshi has pushed back publicly, calling this voter suppression and arguing that election prediction markets are federally regulated by the CFTC, so states shouldn't have authority to restrict them; Kalshi also argues betting increases young-voter engagement and turnout.
- Tsekova cited evidence that prediction markets can be manipulated or exploited for insider trading in political contexts: a case involving a Trump teleprompter operator investigated for alleged insider trading (jurisdiction still being determined), a U.S. soldier who bet on the fate of Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro, Kalshi banning at least three political candidates for betting on their own races, and a Bloomberg-reported case in Romania where a single trader placed roughly $1 million over six days on one presidential candidate, measurably moving the odds — illustrating that smaller, less liquid markets are especially susceptible to manipulation. She also noted parallels to the crypto industry's political mobilization behind Trump, including Trump family involvement in both Kalshi and Polymarket and reported interest from Trump Media in launching its own prediction market, and said the current CFTC and White House administration have been broadly supportive of prediction markets, which is part of why the underlying legal disputes are so contested.
- Tsekova framed the core legal/policy risks as twofold: manipulation (a foreign agent or motivated party using money and market access to influence perceived odds or actual outcomes) and insider trading (someone with privileged information about election results profiting before that information becomes public, and potentially passing it to others). She noted prediction markets were seen as more accurate than traditional polling in the 2024 election, which has boosted their credibility and mainstream media partnerships (e.g., appearing on cable news broadcasts), further increasing their reach and influence over public perception of races.

## Overview
This episode of Everybody's Business opens with Bloomberg Senior Editor Eric Wiener unpacking why the "Magnificent Seven" mega-cap trade has fractured: AI spending and AI revenue traction — not sector membership — now separate winners (Microsoft, Alphabet) from laggards (Meta) and outliers (Apple, which benefits from having no AI exposure at all). The conversation moves to the macro picture, with Wiener warning that beneath healthy headline growth numbers lies a fragile assumption that AI adoption will scale linearly, and that a genuine risk lies in a potential liquidity squeeze if rising rates and falling stock prices choke off the capital AI companies need to keep spending. This dovetails into a discussion of the Federal Reserve's latest meeting under new chair Kevin Warsh, which held rates steady but saw an unusually high three dissenting votes in favor of hikes — a "hawkish hold" that, combined with Warsh's more opaque forward guidance, has left traders on edge. The segment closes with a skeptical look at AI-abundance rhetoric from Elon Musk and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, with hosts and Wiener questioning whether AI executives' actions (layoffs) match their abundance narratives, and drawing a contrast with the dot-com bubble, where believers were more sincere.

The second segment shifts to trade policy, featuring Ori Zohar, co-founder of the spice company Burlap and Barrel, one of the small businesses suing the Trump administration over tariffs. Zohar walks through the chaotic sequence of tariff regimes since "Liberation Day" — emergency IEEPA tariffs that courts struck down and the government had to refund with interest, followed by Section 301 tariffs — and explains how his company, working with the nonprofit Liberty Justice Center, became a plaintiff nearly by accident after filing an amicus brief. He details the real-world cost to a small, ingredient-import-dependent business: hundreds of thousands of dollars paid in tariffs, hundreds of ad hoc carve-outs signaling policy incoherence, and downstream pressure to cut costs domestically rather than abroad. He frames the lawsuits as a values-driven, bipartisan stand (comparing it to a "Patagonia moment") rather than a pursuit of financial damages.

The final segment, "This Week in Prediction Markets," features Bloomberg reporter Denitsa Tsekova discussing a fight between Kalshi and Wisconsin election officials, who cited an 1849 state law to warn that betting on an election you vote in could disqualify your ballot. Kalshi argues this is voter suppression and that federal CFTC regulation preempts state authority. Tsekova lays out documented cases of manipulation and insider-trading risk tied to political prediction markets — from a Romanian election where a single large trade moved the odds, to banned candidates betting on their own races — and situates the dispute within a broader political alignment between crypto/prediction-market industries and the Trump administration, whose regulators have generally been supportive of these platforms.

Throughout, the episode returns to a common thread: powerful financial and technological narratives (AI abundance, tariff policy, prediction-market accuracy) are colliding with messier, more contested realities on the ground — for markets, for small businesses, and for the integrity of elections.

## Implications
For investors, the episode is a reminder that mega-cap tech is no longer a monolithic trade — differentiating between AI spenders with revenue traction versus those without (or those without AI exposure at all) matters more than treating the "Magnificent Seven" as a single basket. The liquidity-risk framing (capital access tightening if rates rise and stock issuance stalls) is a concrete signal to watch heading into Fed communications around Jackson Hole and upcoming inflation data. For small business owners, particularly importers, the tariff discussion illustrates that litigation — even without the resources of a large corporation — can be a viable lever against policy that raises costs, provided a legal partner willing to underwrite the fight can be found; it also shows how quickly tariff rules can change (three regimes in roughly six months) and the operational toll of that uncertainty. For anyone following prediction markets or election integrity, the Wisconsin-Kalshi dispute signals a coming wave of state-versus-federal jurisdictional fights as betting platforms expand into politics, with real documented cases of price manipulation in smaller/less liquid markets suggesting the risk isn't merely theoretical.
