## Key Points
- **Arbitrum's token is deliberately maximally open.** As framed in the discussion, roughly 97% of the world today can buy and hold the Arbitrum (ARB) token with no ownership limits and no disclosure requirements — it is treated as a broadly accessible, permissionless instrument rather than a security with attached rights.
- **The hypothetical "Superstate" alternate timeline.** The host poses a thought experiment: imagine Robert Leshner (Compound founder, now behind Superstate's compliant tokenization model) had instead pitched Offchain Labs on issuing a restricted, tokenized-equity version of the ARB token — one that functions like actual company equity with investor rights, but that only roughly 5% of the world (sophisticated/accredited investors) would be permitted to hold.
- **The core trade-off: broad access vs. investor rights.** The discussion frames this as an implicit trade-off crypto has made: a small segment of sophisticated investors would rather hold something with real investor rights (the ability to call the founder and object to decisions) than an open but rights-less token, even though the token has achieved far broader distribution than any traditional equity could.
- **Goldfeder rejects the trade.** Steven Goldfeder, co-founder and CEO of Offchain Labs, says he would not make that trade — he wants "to have his cake and eat it too": keep the 97% open accessibility while also getting regulatory clarity, rather than shrinking the eligible holder base to 5% in exchange for legal rights.
- **The ARB token wasn't originally designed as an equity/revenue vehicle.** Goldfeder recounts that during Arbitrum's 2017–2018 seed round, some investors said they'd only invest if Arbitrum issued a token, and the team turned them down, believing at the time that Arbitrum didn't need one. The token later emerged not from a plan to capture revenue (that came afterward) but from the practical need for on-chain governance once the project's vision shifted from short-lived, disposable L2 chain instances to a single long-lived chain with mutable contracts requiring oversight.
- **Wide distribution as a censorship-resistance feature.** A closing point argues the "97%" figure matters for decentralization: with a small, identifiable pool of holders (the hypothetical 5%), it becomes easier to know where those holders are domiciled and to compel them to act a certain way (e.g., via regulators), whereas a broad, dispersed 97% holder base is much harder to coerce.

## Overview
This is a short "Uneasy Money" segment from Unchained's crypto governance/tokenomics show, built around a single thought experiment about whether crypto tokens should carry formal investor rights the way equity does. The host sets up an alternate-history scenario: what if, instead of Arbitrum's fully open ARB token, Offchain Labs had issued a restricted, tokenized-equity instrument in the style of Robert Leshner's Superstate — accessible to only a small slice of sophisticated or institutional investors, but carrying genuine investor rights such as recourse against the founding team.

Steven Goldfeder, co-founder and CEO of Offchain Labs (the company behind Arbitrum), responds that he would not trade Arbitrum's current 97%-open, rights-free token model for a 5%-restricted, rights-bearing one. He frames the ideal outcome as regulatory clarity that preserves broad accessibility rather than a forced choice between openness and legal protection. He also gives historical context: Arbitrum's team declined early seed investors who conditioned funding on issuing a token, and the token itself only emerged later as a governance mechanism once the project's architecture evolved from short-lived L2 instances toward a single long-lived chain with mutable contracts — revenue distribution to token holders came still later.

The segment closes on a decentralization argument: broad, anonymous, globally dispersed ownership is harder for outside actors to pressure or compel than a small, identifiable set of institutional holders, framing wide token distribution itself as a resilience feature rather than merely a marketing or fundraising choice. ## Implications
For crypto projects weighing compliant or permissioned token structures (e.g., Uniswap's permissioned pools or Superstate-style tokenized equity), this exchange highlights that the choice isn't simply "get more legal protection" — it's a direct trade-off against decentralization and censorship resistance, since narrowing the eligible holder base makes that base easier to identify and pressure.

For founders and early-stage crypto teams, Goldfeder's account of turning down investors who demanded a token is a reminder that token issuance is a deliberate governance and distribution decision, not an inevitable fundraising step — and that revenue-sharing structures can be layered on well after a token's initial purpose (in Arbitrum's case, governance over mutable contracts) has been established.

For institutional and sophisticated investors, the segment underscores why some continue to prefer direct equity over governance tokens: the lack of formal investor rights (voting power over management decisions, disclosure obligations, legal recourse) in most crypto tokens remains a real barrier to deeper institutional participation, even as retail-level distribution of tokens like ARB has vastly outpaced what traditional equity offerings achieve.
