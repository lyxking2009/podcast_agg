# Amazon VP: Questions On Corporate Politics But They Get Increasingly Darker | Ethan Evans — The Peterman Pod

## TL;DR
Ryan Peterman brings back Ethan Evans, former Amazon VP, for a third round - this time a ladder of corporate-politics questions that get progressively darker. Evans's core answer is that visibility is a game you can win honestly: speak first, claim what is legitimately yours, and stop hoping good work will be noticed on its own.

## Key points
- On credit in collaborative work, Evans' first rule is that credit is easier to claim a little later than in the moment. People are maximally sensitive about attribution right when the work ships, and the claim survives much better once the immediate competitive moment has passed.
- The strongest practical lever is speaking first. Whoever announces the result gets the lion's share of the credit even when they explicitly name everyone else - so announcing with the "we" and your own contribution included is strictly better than waiting for someone else to announce it.
- If someone else announces first, the move is to add on with your specific piece - the part you actually contributed - rather than going quiet. Silence and hoping your contribution will be noticed is the failure mode he calls out most bluntly.
- The "color guy" analogy: in sports broadcasts the play-by-play announcer reports what happened and the color commentator explains the substance. If a product manager is the announcer, engineers can be their own color guy by publishing technical detail, architecture notes and scaling considerations as a follow-on - leadership welcomes the detail and nobody can object to it without looking bad.
- Credit sharing often just needs prompting. Evans recounts going to a VP and telling him "today is the day you write the congratulations message" - people intend to give credit but are not thinking about it, so a direct prompt usually works. If the manager or PM will not do it, share the news yourself.
- On whether everyone playing the visibility game creates a purely political org, he concedes the risk but argues the base rate is the opposite: most people never say anything, and even those who resolve to change do nothing differently tomorrow. He challenges the listener to be in the half that acts.
- His answer to those who refuse to play out of principle is unusually direct: refusing to speak is not integrity, it is choosing to be a martyr. The constructive version is to talk and make sure what you say is worth hearing - raise your own bar rather than opting out of the system.
- On how far to stretch claims, Evans is explicit about living in the gray. Soft skills are always context dependent, and there is no single best move. His boundary is never crossing into the black where you are lying, but also not restricting yourself to the pure white of facts nobody would dispute.
- His own example is calling himself "the first engineer on Prime Video" when the product was not yet named Prime at the time. He acknowledges a colleague objects to the framing, but argues the shortened version is defensible, far more memorable, and that simple messages work - the point is to claim as much as you reasonably can without getting caught out on something demonstrably false.
- The patents analogy makes the same point in reverse: many of his patents have co-inventors, and in some cases his name was added for social reasons while in others someone else was the real inventor. His resolution is to say "I have 70 patents, I co-" rather than narrating each contribution.
- The episode's framing question is whether AI doing the technical work makes non-technical skills and politics more important - Peterman's premise is that with models able to do much of the technical work, soft skills matter more and competition for fewer roles makes politics sharper. Evans takes the hard questions in that spirit.
