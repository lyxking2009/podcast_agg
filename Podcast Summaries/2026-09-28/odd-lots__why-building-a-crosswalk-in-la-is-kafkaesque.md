# Why Building a Crosswalk in LA Is Kafkaesque — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
A live Odd Lots episode from the Vermont Theater in Hollywood with Bill McBride of Calculated Risk and developer Zach Lazzari, on why Los Angeles builds so little: a crosswalk that was approved and will be built "in a few years or more" at a cost near a million dollars, a city that is roughly 95 percent residentially zoned, and a commercial real estate industry that can no longer make money from financial engineering.

## Key points
- The headline anecdote: Lazzari's firm got approval for a crosswalk at Clinton and Western, only to be told it would be built "in a few years or more" - and that it would cost on the order of a million dollars once engineering, electrical work and an underground vault the developer must build were included. His summary of the process is that it is like a Kafka novel.
- Zoning is the structural constraint. Lazzari says roughly 95 percent of Los Angeles is residentially zoned, so when a neighborhood's main street loses its independent businesses there is no adjacent mixed-use fabric to absorb the culture the way side streets do in Manhattan. McBride adds that changing zoning is genuinely hard.
- Both guests treat policy and zoning as the number one issue they hear from builders - McBride says infill and larger home builders alike name regulatory friction above everything else, in good times and bad.
- LA's unusual density of mom-and-pop businesses is explained as a byproduct of low density and comparatively affordable rents: talented people who cannot afford New York can still focus on their craft in LA, which produces an experimental population and a proliferation of independent shops, strip-mall style.
- Lazzari's development philosophy is explicitly imported from Jane Jacobs' The Death and Life of Great American Cities: buy multiple buildings on one street to build a whole community rather than scattering single projects, and recruit tenants the way A24 recruits filmmakers - looking for vision rather than another Shake Shack.
- On taste versus profit, Lazzari argues real estate is forgiving in a way film is not: if a tenant fails the building retains its value and a new tenant generally arrives, so culture-led development does not carry A24's write-off risk.
- The commercial real estate downturn is reframed as a discipline: with rates above cap rates, the old arbitrage of buying on a cap rate and financing cheaper no longer works, so developers are forced to actually make the product good. He says rents are rising where his firm has invested in talent.
- ADU reform is helping in California but only at the margin - it is a small addition to housing stock - while upzoning near transit is where real density changes are happening. They note California's housing crisis declaration also blocked density reductions that had been quietly shrinking beach communities.
- The parking trade-off is called out as the biggest blind spot for a developer raised in New York: LA buyers and tenants ask about parking before anything else, and bad parking can simply mean a business is skipped. Valet is offered as the workaround where it can work.
- McBride notes the build-to-rent model that large builders leaned into is now under pressure, and that most builders he speaks with remain committed to master-planned communities rather than rethinking the single-family home as a product.
- Hosts Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway both defend LA as a genuinely great walking city on weather grounds while conceding it does not meet the standard definition of walkable.
