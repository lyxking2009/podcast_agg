# Max Hodak: How Startups Build Speed — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
At Startup School 2026, Science CEO Max Hodak explains how startups build speed through boring infrastructure: purchasing systems, hiring filters, and attribution systems. Using Science's retinal implant (one patient read a 300-page novel with it) as the backdrop, Hodak argues that 'amateurs talk strategy, professionals talk logistics' — how your 17th employee buys things, how you know what an experiment costs, and why founders can't delegate their judgment determine how fast a company can move. There's no set of five bullet points that makes a startup work.

## Key points
- Science is building a retinal prosthesis: a chip implanted under the retina that restores vision to patients blinded by loss of rods and cones; the patient wears glasses with a camera and laser projector that excite the retina directly. One patient read a 300-page novel with the device.
- 'Amateurs talk strategy, professionals talk logistics' — and Picasso's cheap-turpentine quote: the universal startup lessons are in boring infrastructure, not strategy.
- The 17th-employee test: how does your 17th employee buy things? If everyone gets a credit card, founders get pulled into $3,000 power-supply approvals — the wrong place to exercise spending review. 'If they were at Anthropic, they're not going to be hassled over a $3,000 purchase.'
- Spending review must happen earlier — through budgeting and resource understanding, not purchase approval. Procurement systems (vendor accounts, POs, invoices) take two weeks and require active management and metrics.
- Speed is built with infrastructure, not smarts: 'It is mostly not that we are smarter. It is infrastructure like this; that is how speed is built.'
- Attribution is the next problem: when you buy gases, resins, and media in bulk, nobody knows what an experiment costs — so 'experiments are free.' Science built Helix, internal software where almost everything in the company is a button, to correlate costs through to manufacturing (e.g. $40,000 per wafer iteration).
- Hiring: the best startups build rigorous hiring processes and filters; judgment, horsepower, and agency matter most.
- Rethinking performance reviews; rate of iteration separates success from failure; you can't delegate your judgment; action produces information.
- The operating system of a company (how it buys, hires, and tracks) determines how far you can take it — there is no set of five bullet points that makes a startup work.
- Biotech is hard and capital-intensive, but the impact — restoring sight, healing the sick — is a form of power that can be shared, unlike economic or military power. 'This is not about money; this is about power.'
- On contrarianism: raising a Series A to 'vibe code' a purchasing system would draw board questions — Science could do it because Hodak controls the company.

## Overview
Max Hodak's Startup School 2026 talk uses Science — the company building a retinal implant that restored a blind patient's ability to read a 300-page novel — as the framing device for a talk about startup infrastructure. His core argument: the lessons that generalize across companies are logistical, not strategic. He walks through three systems in detail. First, purchasing: how your 17th employee buys things, why per-purchase founder approval is the wrong control point, and how procurement systems (and active management of them) build speed. Second, attribution: when you buy inputs in bulk, no one knows what an experiment costs, which makes experiments 'free' — so Science built Helix, internal software that turns every company action into a button and correlates costs through to manufacturing. Third, hiring and judgment: rigorous hiring processes, the primacy of judgment/horsepower/agency, why you can't delegate judgment, and why iteration rate separates winners from losers. He closes with Q&A on biotech's difficulty and its unmatched impact, and why the for-profit company is a powerful vehicle for changing the world.

## Implications
- For founders: examine how your 17th employee buys things, how you attribute experiment costs, and how you hire — these systems, not strategy decks, determine speed.
- Spending review belongs at the budgeting layer, not the purchase-approval layer; friction at the PO/invoice stage is a speed tax.
- Attribution software (making costs visible to the experiment level) is the prerequisite for knowing what you're actually spending and what to charge.
- Founders cannot delegate judgment; hiring for judgment, horsepower, and agency compounds across the company.
