# NYT CEO Meredith Kopit Levien on Running a Media Brand in the Age of AI — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway interview Meredith Kopit Levien, president and CEO of The New York Times, about how the company evolved from a newspaper into a diversified media brand spanning journalism, games, recipes, sports (The Athletic), and a major new push into video. Levien explains the brand promise that ties it together — high-quality independent journalism plus market-leading lifestyle products — how the Times allocates resources, the economics of the video bet, talent retention, and why the family-controlled structure lets the company play the long game.

## Key points
- Levien's definition of the Times: 'the essential service for curious people' — high quality independent news journalism plus market-leading lifestyle products (games, recipes, sports) worth being sought out by name.
- The hard part of her tenure: transitioning from playing defense (declining print business) to playing offense — thinking ambitiously about what the Times can be; her 13-year lesson is that media growth is always a question of growing the engaged audience.
- Brand promise across products: high-quality, independent, trustworthy information presented elegantly, that adds understanding — and in the case of lifestyle products, helps you be a better fan (Athletic), a smarter person (games).
- Resource allocation: the first dollar goes to high-quality independent news journalism — 'hard stop'; she pushes back on the idea that journalism is a loss leader, and describes her job as ensuring no editorial leader ever has to cut coverage for cost reasons (e.g. 70 people on the ground in Ukraine, Ebola reporting in the DRC).
- Talent: the Times has its largest newsroom in history (~1,000 more journalists than when she arrived); the pitch to stars (Jonathan Swan, Ezra Klein) is the best editors, lawyers, security, graphics people — and the biggest audience in journalism.
- Video: an ambitious, intentional push into video — explicitly NOT the cynical, ad-driven 'pivot to video' of 5-10 years ago; the thesis is that old formats don't go away (audio is still a monster success with The Daily), but there is a giant new audience of watchers that the Times serves only nascently today.
- The stock has sold off after the last two quarters' earnings, partly over video-production costs; Levien argues the family-controlled dual structure exists precisely to keep journalism independent and to let management make big bets and give them time to play out.
- On audience capture: the Times does not chase audiences; it pursues the truth wherever it leads — 'when you've angered both sides, that's how you know you've found your job.'
- On talent competition: the infrastructure for individual newsrooms is now a click away (Substack), and pay scales differ wildly across media — the Times' answer is the best-work-of-your-career model plus the platform and audience only it can offer.

## Overview
In this Odd Lots episode recorded August 6, 2026 (the day after the Times reported earnings), Meredith Kopit Levien lays out the New York Times business model in the AI era. She describes the company as two things at once: an essential service delivering high-quality independent journalism, and a portfolio of market-leading lifestyle products (Wordle/games, cooking/recipes, The Athletic). She explains the capital allocation philosophy — the first dollar always goes to journalism, and her job is to make the company successful enough that editorial decisions are never cost-driven, citing Ukraine coverage and DRC Ebola reporting as examples. The conversation covers the talent wars (notable departures like Ross Douthat to 60 Minutes and the Hard Fork hosts leaving), and why the Times can still win the best journalists. The centerpiece is the video strategy: Levien distinguishes it sharply from the earlier ad-driven 'pivot to video' fad, arguing this push is about building a watcher audience at the scale of millions, and that it complements rather than replaces reading and listening. She also addresses the earnings selloff, the value of the family-controlled share structure for long-term bets, and the company's stance on audience capture — pursuing truth even when it angers both sides.

## Implications
- For media executives: the Times' model argues the bundle (news + games + cooking + sports) is a durable moat, and that video is a new audience market rather than a replacement behavior.
- For investors: expect continued heavy investment in video with near-term margin pressure; the dual-class structure means management can absorb that patience.
- For journalists: the Times' pitch is the platform + support system (editors, lawyers, security, graphics) + audience — a differentiator against individual Substack operations.
- For the AI age: the through-line is uncompromised, trustworthy information as the brand promise — positioning the Times against AI-generated content commoditization.
