# 101. Becoming a Global Thinker: A Conversation with The Women in the Arena and Iron Butterfly — Wiser World

## TL;DR
In this collaborative episode, hosts Ally Roer of Wiser World, Sarah Skully of Women in the Arena, and Katie Hopkins and Megan Jaffer of Iron Butterfly discuss what it means to be a global thinker. They define it as seeking context, practicing curiosity and humility, and walking in others' shoes, with insights drawn from teaching, military service, and intelligence analysis. Practical strategies include micro-habits like 5-10 minutes of daily learning, intentionally diversifying news sources, and community engagement, while risks of not thinking globally include manipulation, fear, and missing the richness of the world.

## Key points
- Katie Hopkins cites former CIA analytic directorate leader Carmen Medina's definition: 'Intelligence is just walking a day in someone else's shoes,' framing global thinking as imagining life in different cultures, countries, and times to build empathy.
- Sarah Skully argues that global thinking is not reserved for scholars, but is a practice accessible to anyone through micro-behaviors like seeking diverse news sources, asking questions, and having conversations, with humility and curiosity.
- Ally Roer emphasizes context as the core of global thinking, comparing understanding a person to knowing what happened to them yesterday, and applies the same to countries: knowing history prevents viewing current events as isolated.
- Sarah highlights intellectual humility as a key principle—being willing to admit not knowing, open to revising views, and comfortable with being a beginner, which is essential for lifelong learning.
- Ally notes that people generally struggle with asking questions, recommending genuine inquiry rather than combative questioning, and suggests self-checking how many questions one asks in a conversation.
- Katie describes intelligence analyst toolkit: rigorous sourcing, identifying assumptions, and creating alternatives; she explains post-9/11 coordination processes that built intentional dissension across 18 agencies to prevent groupthink and ensure pluralism.
- Sarah explains pluralism as coexisting with contradictory truths; effective leaders can sit across from someone they disagree with and have coffee without needing to prove them wrong.
- Ally recommends using context as an antidote to overwhelm, citing her prior study of Iran and Israel/Palestine before the Israel-Iran conflict, which lowered her shock and allowed pause.
- Sarah and Katie suggest practical micro-habits: 5-10 minutes daily of enjoyable learning (podcast, article), finding sources that don't incite fear, asking neighbors about their perspectives, and community involvement like school events or city council meetings.
- Katie cautions against suppressing emotions when consuming news; feeling distressed is a normal signal of caring, and trying to be purely objective can skew perception.
- Ally uses a physical 'Brick' device to lock herself out of news and social media during family time, arguing no person is designed to consume current events constantly; intentional limits reduce overwhelm.
- For getting started, Sarah recommends tailoring learning to your preferred style (auditory, visual, social); Katie suggests travel and diverse friendships; Ally advises following international news accounts like BBC India/Africa and picking a country to explore via fiction or documentary.
- Risks of not being a global thinker: Ally warns of easy manipulation and living in fear; Sarah notes negative impacts on leadership, HR, and storytelling; Katie says in intelligence, lives are at stake, and personally, you miss the world's texture and beauty.
