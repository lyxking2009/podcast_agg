# Susan Cain: Why Introverts Make Great Leaders, Staying Human in the AI Age and the Bittersweet Life — In Good Company with Nicolai Tangen

## TL;DR
Susan Cain, author of Quiet and Bittersweet, tells Nicolai Tangen that 'highly sensitive people' (15-20% of the population, many but not all introverts) will have an authenticity advantage in the AI era because they resist letting machines take over self-expression. She argues introverted leaders often match or beat extroverted ones, citing Jim Collins's Good to Great and Adam Grant's Wharton research, discusses 'toxic positivity' via a Harvard Business School oil-rig case study, and describes precisely calibrating her own days between solitude and socializing to avoid burnout.

## Key points
- Cain says highly sensitive people (about 15-20% of the population, mostly but not exclusively introverts) have nervous systems that react more intensely to all stimulation and care deeply about authenticity; she believes this group will have a 'huge advantage' in the AI age.
- Introversion, extroversion, and high sensitivity are among the most heritable personality traits, observable from infancy via reactivity to stimuli like sugar water, persisting into adult decision-making style.
- Cain describes a historical shift from a 19th-century 'culture of character' to a 20th-century 'culture of personality' valuing magnetism and charisma — a shift she says social media has amplified.
- A Kellogg School of Business study found that in a typical large meeting, three people do 70% of the talking, meaning many good ideas from quieter participants go unheard.
- Citing Jim Collins's Good to Great, Cain notes all 11 top-performing US CEOs in that study were described by colleagues as quiet, unassuming, soft-spoken, and shy, yet fiercely dedicated with strong long-term vision.
- Douglas Conant, former CEO of Campbell Soup and a self-described shy, introverted person, personally wrote 30,000 handwritten thank-you letters to employees over his 10-year tenure.
- Adam Grant's research at Wharton found extroverted leaders produce better outcomes with passive employees, while introverted leaders produce better outcomes with proactive employees, because they're more likely to actually listen.
- On 'toxic positivity': Cain describes a Harvard Business School study of a Gulf of Mexico oil rig where CEO Rick Fox had workers spend 10 days openly discussing doubts and personal struggles, after which accident rates plummeted.
- Cain says she precisely calibrates her own daily schedule — including daily tennis and non-negotiable solo writing time — to balance social and solitary time, and cannot delegate that calendar management to anyone.
- Cain calls her biggest personal mistake spending about seven years as a corporate lawyer instead of pursuing writing, which she wanted to do since age four.
