# At The Money: Can Dividend Investing Give You Yield AND Growth? — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
In this episode of At The Money, Barry Ritholtz interviews David Bonson, founder and CIO of the Bonson Group and author of Profit from the Profit. Bonson explains why dividend growth investing remains relevant despite the AI-driven mega-cap rally, emphasizing that dividends are cash, provide downside protection, and can deliver both income and long-term growth. He outlines how to avoid yield traps, assess sustainability, use dividend yield as a valuation tool, and construct a 30-35 stock dividend growth portfolio.

## Key points
- David Bonson argues dividends are a component of total return, not an alternative; big tech and AI have led some to ignore dividends, but the dot-com bust shows the risk of that mindset.
- Dividends are cash and cannot be faked, unlike EBITDA or earnings; they reflect management's confidence in free cash flow and business growth, and slowing dividend growth can be an early warning.
- Companies with no high-return reinvestment opportunities often either hoard cash or pursue value-destructive M&A; mature dividend growers that return cash tend to deliver superior long-term returns on equity.
- Buybacks are frequently used to offset executive stock compensation rather than return capital to shareholders; record buybacks have not reduced net share count, while dividends provide tangible income and are cut only as a last resort.
- High dividend yield alone can be a trap: a stock price decline mechanically raises yield without improving cash returns; investors should distinguish yield driven by price drops from yield driven by dividend increases.
- Sustainability requires analyzing free cash flow, payout ratio, balance sheet, business model durability, and management's cultural commitment to dividend growth; alignment with founder or family ownership is a positive signal.
- Citigroup in 2007 illustrates a warning sign: borrowing $10.5 billion at 10% from Saudi and Kuwaiti investors to maintain its dividend preceded a 99% stock decline and eventual dividend cut.
- Valuation matters; Bonson uses a stock's current dividend yield relative to its own historical average as a valuation tool, selling Walmart after its yield fell from 3.5% to 1.5% and Broadcom after its yield dropped to 1%.
- Dividend growth portfolios have a market beta around 0.6 and were defensive in 2008, when the only two Dow stocks up were Walmart and McDonald's; they also beat inflation over time with dividend growth of 6-9% vs 2-5% inflation.
- From 2000 to 2009 the S&P 500 returned 0% while most dividend growth portfolios compounded at 7-8% annually, and in the 2009-2026 bull market dividend growth matched or beat the index in about half the years.
- The ex-dividend price drop does not negate long-term returns; dividend growth investors are buying the company's underlying cash flow growth, not arbitraging dividend payment dates.
- Long-term compounding of reinvested dividends can produce enormous yield-on-cost: examples include McDonald's 400,000% total return with reinvested dividends and Blackstone paying 30% annually on Bonson's original 2011 cost basis.
- Portfolio construction should include 30-35 diversified positions sized 1.5%-4% at purchase; sell when the thesis breaks or when valuation/yield becomes unattractive, as with BP after the Deepwater Horizon spill and Walmart/Broadcom after yield compression.
