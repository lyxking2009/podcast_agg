# AI for the Fortune 2000: How Tenex Builds an AI-Native SDLC — Alex Lieberman & CJ Hess — Latent Space: The AI Engineer Podcast

## TL;DR
Alex Lieberman and CJ Hess of 10X discuss how the company helps Fortune 2000 and mid-market enterprises become AI-native, scaling from an AI dev shop to 75 people in 18 months. They detail their story-point-based compensation model, internal 'meta harness' for agent workflows, and observed 2x increase in engineer output this year. The conversation covers enterprise AI adoption milestones (only 5% have non-engineering agents in production), model preferences favoring Opus 5.5 via Amp, skepticism towards vertical-specific AI startups, and New York's emerging AI ecosystem.

## Key points
- 10X is an applied AI partner targeting Fortune 2000 and mid-market companies (revenue $100M–$500M), grown to 75 employees in 18 months, with 90% being engineers or AI strategists.
- Compensation is output-based via story points: top engineers earn 4x more than the lowest, with some exceeding $1M/year; concerns about story point inflation or bad code have not materialized, but the model struggles to compensate non-coding work like meetings and architecture.
- The internal 'meta harness' standardizes engineering workflows with canonical plan documents, a CLI, and Git-checked logs of agent decisions, ensuring durable context and reducing reliance on human memory.
- Average story points per engineer per month have roughly doubled from start of 2026 to now, driven by improvements from Opus 4.5 to Opus 5.5 and reduced need for multiple specialized models.
- Model usage has consolidated: most engineers default to Opus 5.5 via Amp (with Claude Code subscription integration), occasionally using Codex (Sol 6.1) for computer-use tasks; token spend per employee is estimated between 1:1 and 2:1 relative to compensation.
- Only ~50% of enterprises have actually rolled out coding agents to engineering/product orgs, despite popular perception of 70–80%; ~90% have provided general-purpose LLM access, ~60% have training/enablement programs, and just ~5% have non-engineering agents in production.
- The biggest asks from enterprises are deploying agents for back-office repeatable work and solving RBAC/data permissioning for AI tools; 10X has productized a gateway solution after building bespoke systems.
- Enterprises often lack standardized processes, making agent automation impossible; 10X recommends building the process before automating, echoing 'you can't automate a process that does not exist.'
- Alex and CJ are generally bearish on vertical-specific AI solutions (e.g., Harvey, OpenEd) because of enterprise nuance, inertia, and expectation of one-of-one customization; they concede some may succeed with data moats or app-specific affordances, but frontier models could absorb many tasks.
- On replacing mid-tier SaaS, 10X argues the decision depends on the financial prize and cognitive load; host's Kill My SaaS competition replaced a $40k/year SaaS for $1k, but enterprises with high-stakes systems like Salesforce or SAP remain risk-averse.
- AI-driven job displacement is addressed through reallocation: subject matter experts are moved to higher-level strategic work; employees must take agency to reskill, as call centers and other routine roles decline.
- New York's AI scene is a smaller bubble than San Francisco but sits in a larger commercial pond; 10X aims to grow NYC as an AI hub, leveraging enterprise proximity and arbitrage between SF builders and NYC customers.
