# At The Money Fan Favorite: Looking Past Market Cap Weighted Indexes — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
Rob Arnott, founder of Research Affiliates, argues that cap-weighted indexes carry hidden costs from front-running index changes and concentration risk. He advocates fundamental indexing, weighting companies by economic footprint (sales, profits, net worth, distributions), which has outperformed cap-weighted value indexes by over 2% annually for 20 years with lower volatility.

## Key points
- Cap-weighted indexes have a hidden active side: S&P 500 index funds collectively hold roughly 25% of each constituent's market cap, and their requirement to buy additions at market-on-close on the effective date allows legal front-running, costing about 15 basis points per year in trading drag.
- S&P 500 additions are typically bought after 75% outperformance relative to the market over the prior year and at twice the market multiple; deletions are sold at half the market multiple with an average loss of about 7,000 basis points relative to the market.
- The flip-flop problem is costly: 28% of stocks added to the S&P 500 are dropped within a decade, and almost half of deletions rejoin within a decade. Deletion flip-flops underperform by 3,500 bps before removal, then outperform by roughly 180 percentage points before being re-added.
- Fundamental indexing weights companies by their economic footprint, averaging four measures: sales, profits, intangibles-adjusted net worth, and shareholder distributions (dividends plus buybacks), which naturally creates a value tilt and underweights frothy growth stocks.
- RAFI Fundamental Index strategies, live since 2005, have outperformed cap-weighted value indexes by about 2% to 2.5% per year compounded, with a tracking error of about 2.5%, and have beaten value in roughly three out of four years.
- The fundamental index's rebalancing alpha trims stocks whose prices have soared without fundamental validation and adds to fallen stocks with intact fundamentals, contributing to outperformance and lower volatility.
- Equal weighting is a legitimate alternative that introduces small-cap and value tilts and rebalancing alpha, but its Achilles heel is that equal weighting an index like the S&P 500 includes stocks that have soared into eligibility and excludes cheap underperformers, biasing the portfolio toward higher multiples.
- Growth indexes have outperformed largely due to multiple expansion; over the past quarter century, underlying fundamentals (sales, profits, book value, dividends) of value and growth portfolios grew roughly in parallel, while Russell value underperformed the Russell 1000 by 3,800 basis points peak-to-trough before bottom-bouncing since summer 2020.
- Nvidia illustrates the valuation gap: its market cap weight is 7-8% of the index, but its economic footprint based on fundamental measures is only about 1-1.5% of the economy.
- Fundamental indexing is not new or small: introduced in 2005, RAFI strategies now hold over $100 billion in assets across Schwab, Invesco, and PIMCO ETFs and mutual funds.
- Rob Arnott documented the S&P addition/deletion anomaly in a 1986 article, which was reportedly used to lobby S&P to pre-announce index changes to reduce front-running damage.
- Investors concerned about market concentration and valuation should consider a fundamental index, which trades differently from both growth and value and offers a better risk and valuation profile.
