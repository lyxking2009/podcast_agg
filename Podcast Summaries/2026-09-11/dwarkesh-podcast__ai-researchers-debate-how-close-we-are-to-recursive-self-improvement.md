# AI researchers debate how close we are to recursive self-improvement — Dwarkesh Podcast

## TL;DR

Dwarkesh Patel convenes three researchers — Beren Millidge (Zyphra), John Schulman (Thinking Machines, ex-OpenAI) and Charlie O'Neill (Baseten) — to argue about recursive self-improvement. The framing question: if 2036 arrives without superintelligence, why? The answers range from a persistent sim-to-real gap and research-bottleneck ceilings to the opposite case, where an agent 0.1% better than humans at AI research, run in parallel at chip speed, produces explosive takeoff.

## Key points

- Schulman's failure mode for superintelligence is a Moravec's paradox pattern: models get extremely good at whatever is put into a benchmark or environment, but a persistent sim-to-real gap blocks impact. He thinks this is unlikely, since RL is already generalizing, but it is his default scenario if meta-learning and continual learning are simply too hard.
- The 'feels dumb after a month' cycle: each new model triggers 'This is it. This is AGI,' then users adapt and find judgment bottlenecks — the model cannot check itself well enough, so explosive capability growth does not translate into explosive research productivity.
- Millidge frames the question as distance from the global optimum of a learner on a chip. If an agent is even 0.1% better than all humans at AI research, and you can run hundreds of thousands or millions in parallel at faster clock speeds, that outweighs every other bottleneck and yields very fast takeoff.
- The Moore's law analogy is used to argue against extrapolating a smooth curve: scaling laws have repeatedly required discrete discontinuities (pre-training, then RL) to keep looking like a straight line, and it is unclear that RL environments targeted at self-improvement can discover the next discontinuity.
- Automating AI research is described as roughly 'ASI-complete': an AI that can do AI research is close to an AI that can do most things, because research is the general-purpose capability.
- Timeline responses cluster around 5-10 years for the automation of AI research, with a long tail of domains where the compute has simply never been allocated and where learning a new field as fast as a human still matters.
- The guests disagree productively about whether context length and external memory can substitute for the kind of long-horizon learning that research requires — one argues no amount of a million-token context window replaces certain capabilities.
- The format itself is the point: three researchers from small, relatively open labs can speak on the record where frontier-lab employees cannot, making the debate more informative than the usual lab press cycle.
