# Crime Is Just One Prompt Away — Everybody's Business

## TL;DR

Everybody's Business pairs two stories that both come down to who bears the cost of AI. First, Evan Ratliff's Businessweek reporting on what happens when an AI is party to a crime — who is liable, can you jail the model, should developers be charged? Then David Papadopoulos walks through the bond market, where the Treasury Secretary is buying back long bonds funded by T-bills, and asks whether AI data-center borrowing is quietly pushing mortgage and credit-card rates higher.

## Key points

- The crime segment asks a genuinely novel legal question: when an AI is an accomplice to a crime, how should the criminal justice system treat it, and where does responsibility land — the model, the user, or the developer? Max Chafkin and Stacey Vanek-Smith frame it around Evan Ratliff's reporting and the reporting around a 'Salamanca' robot.
- A recurring theme is that AI changes the unit of accountability — an 'accomplice' that cannot be deterred or imprisoned forces the law to attach liability somewhere else in the chain.
- The bond market segment explains the mechanics of Scott Bessent's buybacks: rather than the textbook exchange of illiquid old bonds for new ones, the Treasury is using hundreds of billions in cash on hand and issuing short-term bills to buy back 10-, 20- and 30-year debt, suppressing the long end by removing supply.
- The strategy is explicitly risky: borrowing short to retire long assets, against a deficit running around 6% of GDP, healthy growth, AI companies borrowing heavily, sticky inflation, and oil prices lifted by war with Iran.
- Papadopoulos is blunt that the AI buildout's debt binge is competing with Treasuries for capital and is one of the forces keeping rates elevated — 'are the robots stealing our loans?' — with the hosts joking about a three-phase plan: first our loans, then our jobs, then us.
- Bessent's public posture is quoted at length: he describes the intervention as his dream setup because he has asymmetric information, is a veteran dollar-yen trader with deep Bank of Japan insight, and tells markets 'you can bet against me if you want.'
- The hosts read the asymmetric-information talk as a bluff: Treasury has no firepower it is willing to commit beyond trading skill and hints about future deficit cuts that have not materialized.
- Attention shifts to the Fed and chair Kevin Warsh's third meeting, with markets unsure he has the conviction to crush inflation — and the counterintuitive point that a short-term rate hike could actually improve long-end confidence.
