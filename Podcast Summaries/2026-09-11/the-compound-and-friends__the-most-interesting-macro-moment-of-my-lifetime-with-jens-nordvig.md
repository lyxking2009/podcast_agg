# The Most Interesting Macro Moment of My Lifetime with Jens Nordvig — The Compound and Friends

## TL;DR

Jens Nordvig, president of Vanda, calls this the most interesting macro environment of his lifetime, driven by an unprecedented AI capex boom where hyperscalers now issue as much long-term debt as the U.S. Treasury, pushing yields to multi-decade highs. He discusses Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's intervention in dollar-yen and Treasury buybacks, the conflicting goals of a weaker dollar amid inflation, and why Korea's AI-driven memory boom is a standout trade. Despite weak discretionary stocks, consumer spending remains robust, with Bank of America data showing 5% year-over-year growth.

## Key points

- Jens Nordvig describes the current macro environment as the most interesting of his lifetime, citing the AI productivity shock, hyperscaler debt issuance, and government debt dynamics as unprecedented converging forces.
- Hyperscalers (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta) are now issuing roughly as much long-term debt as the U.S. government, with the flow currently comparable to Treasury issuance; their bond issuance is expected to accelerate dramatically next year as capex plans continue to ramp through 2027.
- The U.S. budget deficit is running at about 6% of GDP, equal to the largest deficits under Reagan, and CBO projections show debt-to-GDP rising from above 100% to 200% within a couple of decades, making current yield levels harder to sustain than in 2007.
- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent intervened in the dollar-yen market and has been buying back long-dated Treasuries to cap yields; he stated 'I am the house' and claimed asymmetric information on Bank of Japan policy, correctly anticipating the September hike which moved from 3-4 basis points priced to over 90%.
- The administration wants a weaker dollar against Asian currencies to support manufacturing, but that conflicts with rising inflation; oil prices have stayed elevated for over six months, and 5-year breakeven inflation has risen from 2.2% to 2.4-2.5%, complicating Fed easing.
- Consumer spending remains solid despite sharp declines in many discretionary retail stocks: Bank of America aggregated credit and debit card spending per household rose 5% year-over-year in July, and CEO Brian Moynihan reported August spending up about 4% year-over-year.
- Discretionary stock weakness is not a reliable signal for the economy: the equal-weight consumer discretionary sector relative to the S&P 500 has been in a decade-long downtrend while the market kept rising; spending data shows both necessity and discretionary categories are growing.
- Jens Nordvig sees Korea as the most extraordinary macro country shock: the AI-driven memory chip boom has produced an exploding trade surplus, the won has had its best three-month run ever, and local retail investors have displaced foreigners as the main stock market driver; he recommends long Korea equities and currency.
- Gold has decoupled from real rates since 2022-2023 due to large-scale central bank buying, especially from China, and persistent ETF demand; while speculative option activity may not repeat January's bonanza, central bank and ETF flows should support gold on a multi-quarter horizon.
- Key risks flagged include further oil price spikes, a rapid 20-35 basis point move in long-end yields that could trigger equity selling, and concerns about the upcoming election; Jens says the bond market is already making equity investors nervous.
- Jens's firm, formed by merging Exante Data with Vanda, combines currency/fixed income and equity flow data and is launching an AI-powered analytics platform, with a hybrid model of high-quality data plus vetted human insight because most portfolio managers still trade discretionarily.
