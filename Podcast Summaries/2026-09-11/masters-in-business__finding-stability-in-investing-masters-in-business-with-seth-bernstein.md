# Finding Stability in Investing: Masters in Business with Seth Bernstein — Masters in Business

## TL;DR

Barry Ritholtz interviews Seth Bernstein, CEO of AllianceBernstein and head of asset management at Equitable Holdings, on a $900bn-plus asset manager and how it adapts. Bernstein's arc runs from an architect's plan abandoned at Haverford, through 32 years of JPMorgan predecessors, into running fixed income and currency globally — and his closing wisdom is compact: diversification is not how you get rich, it is how you stay rich.

## Key points

- Bernstein's original plan was architecture, abandoned both for lack of talent and lack of money; he joined one of JPMorgan Chase's 1980s predecessors (Morgan Guaranty Trust) in 1984, entering a year-long training program that taught liberal-arts hires accounting, corporate finance and higher math to build bankers and traders.
- Morgan Guaranty's culture of near-exclusive internal promotion created unusual opportunity: as the firm gained new powers — equity powers in 1991-92 as Glass-Steagall erosion began — it retrained existing employees rather than hiring in, and Bernstein moved into equity capital markets, then ran high yield, then debt capital markets and loan syndications.
- He describes the merger roll-up that turned Morgan Guaranty into JPMorgan Chase and his own path into media and telecom coverage, a rotation he characterizes wryly as what happens to people who get bored of bond underwriting.
- He ultimately became global head of fixed income and currency at JPMorgan before moving to AllianceBernstein, where he has been CEO since 2017 and where the firm manages more than $905 billion.
- The conversation's focus is how a large asset manager adapts to changing conditions — business mix, compensation, and the shift from an investment-management culture to a solutions-and-services culture driven by client needs.
- Bernstein's view on market timing is scathing and empirical: the number of people who can demonstrate genuine skill at timing markets can be counted on one hand.
- The episode closes on the distinction between getting rich and staying rich: diversification is a risk-management tool, not a return-maximizing one, and conflating the two is a common investor error.
- Ritholtz notes this is the 659th episode of Masters in Business over 12 years, a running chronicle of how allocators actually think rather than what they say publicly.
