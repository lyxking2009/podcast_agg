# Should Stock Tokens Be Limited to KYC'd Users? Or Be Tradeable by Anyone? — Unchained

## TL;DR

Brett Redford, president of Securitize, unpacks the stock token controversy ignited by AMC CEO Adam Aron's attack on Robinhood's offshore synthetic AMC tokens. He explains three types of stock tokens—issuer-sponsored, third-party custodial, and synthetic derivatives—and argues that issuer consent and KYC are essential for investor protection and national security, while still enabling innovations like instant settlement and global access. The SEC is expected to issue an innovation exemption soon, likely including an issuer opt-out mechanism.

## Key points

- Adam Aron, CEO of AMC, called Robinhood's AMC tokens "contemptable, outrageous, disgusting, detestable, inexcusable, vile," while Robinhood CLO Dan Gallagher, a former SEC commissioner, responded that Robinhood knows securities laws and will not desist.
- Robinhood's AMC token is an offshore debt security or synthetic wrapper rather than an actual share, and it joins a broader market of offshore stock tokens that existed before Robinhood's entry.
- Brett Redford distinguishes three types of stock tokens: issuer-sponsored tokens where the token is the actual share moved from DTCC to a transfer agent; third-party custodial tokens backed one-to-one by held shares, which may or may not pass full entitlements; and derivatives/synthetics like Robinhood's, which carry counterparty risk and typically lack voting rights.
- Redford argues issuer-sponsored tokens provide benefits like issuer knowledge of investors, improved communication, efficient voting, and disintermediation, but require issuer onboarding and infrastructure changes.
- He expects the SEC to release an innovation exemption as early as next week, which will likely require full pass-through of entitlements for third-party custodial tokens in the US.
- Redford expresses concern that synthetic stock tokens are being paired with memecoins and traded in unregulated offshore markets on weekends with no underlying liquidity, leading to extreme price dislocations like AMC tokens trading at 60 times the reference price.
- On issuer consent, Redford supports an issuer opt-out mechanism: tokenizers would notify issuers and give them a period (e.g., 30 days) to object; if they object, tokenization would be blocked.
- He acknowledges that swaps, CFDs, and ETFs reference stocks without issuer consent, but argues stock tokens are different because they are marketed as buying a share in the company.
- Redford stresses that KYC is necessary for US securities tokenization to prevent manipulation, comply with transfer laws, and address national security concerns like terrorist financing or foreign adversaries amassing positions in defense companies.
- Securitize has already tokenized its own publicly traded stock, making it the largest tokenized stock, and is working with Computershare and Continental to onboard issuers; Redford also notes tokenization still offers instant settlement, 24/7 trading, global access, and new stock loan markets despite KYC.
