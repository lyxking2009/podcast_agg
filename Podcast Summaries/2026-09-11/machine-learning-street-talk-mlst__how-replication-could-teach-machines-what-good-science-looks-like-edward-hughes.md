# How Replication Could Teach Machines What Good Science Looks Like — Edward Hughes — Machine Learning Street Talk (MLST)

## TL;DR

MLST talks with Edward Hughes, a former DeepMind researcher who built world models and agents before founding Inherent, about what it would take to build AI systems that do science rather than answer questions. The episode ranges from a contrarian read of AlphaGo's move 37 — innovative but not creative — to the 'burden of knowledge' that makes a horizontal layer of intelligence across all science the next big market.

## Key points

- Hughes's path ran through theoretical physics: a PhD on string theory and scattering amplitudes, where he used dualities to map hard calculations into easier geometric spaces. He concluded that work on scattering amplitudes was not the most leveraged way to add to human knowledge, a decision several of his contemporaries made at the same time.
- AlphaGo — and move 37 specifically — was the catalyst. Hughes argues the move was innovative but not creative: novelty inside a defined search space is not the same as generating a new explanatory framework.
- His research arc at DeepMind went from multi-agent reinforcement learning, motivated by the idea that human technology is a product of cultural evolution rather than individual genius, to meta-RL across huge task spaces (a 500M-parameter from-scratch RL agent that was large for its time, and now looks tiny), to world models including hand-writing the final Gelato/GDI model implementation before coding agents existed.
- Inherent has raised $50 million from Index Ventures and Radical Ventures at a $225 million post-money valuation with a team of 11 — a bet that the market for AI scientists is distinct from coding agents, chatbots, or enterprise copilots.
- The stated mission is a horizontal layer of intelligence across science that can power autonomous labs and next-generation R&D organizations — intervening not just on hypotheses but on the loop of experiment, replication and discovery.
- The 'burden of knowledge' is the structural argument: humanity has accumulated so much knowledge that no individual can reach the frontier of multiple domains, so the only way to attack cross-domain problems is with systems that carry knowledge horizontally.
- Hughes describes a phase transition about two or three months before recording, where agents began proactively reaching out to researchers with unsolicited help — useful, but initially annoying because they had no model of what would actually help.
- He frames the next era as collective rather than individual intelligence, and imagines the equivalent of OKRs for an age of recursive self-improvement — plus agents that can evaluate whether their own work constitutes a genuine scientific advance.
