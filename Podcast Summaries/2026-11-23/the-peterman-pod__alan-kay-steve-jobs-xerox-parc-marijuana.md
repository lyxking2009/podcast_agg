# Alan Kay: Steve Jobs, Xerox PARC, Marijuana — The Peterman Pod

## TL;DR
Alan Kay, Turing Award winner, discusses how ARPA's vision under J.C.R. Licklider and Bob Taylor's hiring and culture principles at Xerox PARC produced the Alto personal computer, GUI, and networking, which later influenced Steve Jobs and Apple. He recounts Jobs's 1979 PARC visit, his famous 'good enough to be criticized' remark about the Macintosh, and Jobs's iPhone question 20 years later, as well as Jobs's crucial funding of Pixar. Kay also critiques modern computing culture, social media, and offers advice for young people, touching on influences like Bertrand Russell and Jerome Bruner.

## Key points
- J.C.R. Licklider's vision, expressed in 1962, was that computers should become 'intellectual interactive amplifiers for all people on earth pervasively networked worldwide.' This vision drove the ARPA community, including Xerox PARC, to invent an entire industry rather than isolated technologies, with Alan Kay estimating that the combined gross world product from Xerox PARC is at least $35 trillion.
- Bob Taylor, a former ARPA deputy director, was primarily responsible for Xerox PARC's success by instituting a single blackball hiring system requiring unanimous enthusiasm for new hires, believing that 'you can't get a great person by assembling 10 good people together.' This kept headcount low—about 25 people produced all of PARC's grand inventions, according to Butler Lampson—and the organization was flat with only one title, 'member of research staff.'
- Xerox PARC's culture was engineered for synergy: beanbag chairs prevented aggressive leaps to one's feet during arguments, weekly Wednesday meetings with Chinese food (the 'food of computing' originating from MIT's all-night restaurants) served as the only mandatory gathering, and 'type two' arguments required disputants to restate the other's position until it was accepted, effectively cooling conflicts.
- Xerox entered computing research after its 914 plain-paper copier made it the fastest-growing company in the US, despite IBM rejecting the product based on a flawed Arthur D. Little market study. Jack Goldman, a physicist hired from Ford, convinced Xerox to create a long-range research lab far from Connecticut, allocating about 5% of revenues to R&D and using a 'mad money' poker-chip mentality for speculative research.
- The Alto, which began working in 1973, is credited as the first personal computer with a full GUI. Its design drew on prior art including Engelbart's GUI and mouse (both 1964), Sketchpad's light pen, and Licklider's vision. Alan Kay's 1968 Dynabook concept anticipated a tablet-like device; he later said Steve Jobs wanted to build one but 'forgot the software.'
- Steve Jobs visited Xerox PARC in 1979, three years after Apple was founded, but the Alto had already been publicly demoed and described in Scientific American (1977). Jobs was so impressed by the user interface that he missed object-oriented programming and Ethernet. Alan Kay described Jobs as a non-technical marketing guy with taste, saying 'Steve understands desire' and calling him indomitable.
- Alan Kay's criticism that 'the Macintosh is the first personal computer good enough to be criticized' angered Jobs, especially after Kay wrote a memo titled 'Have I got a deal for you, a Honda with a one quart gas tank.' Twenty years later, at the iPhone launch, Jobs handed Kay an iPhone and asked, 'Alan, is this good enough to be criticized?'—showing he never forgot.
- Kay introduced Steve Jobs to the Pixar team (then Spocket Systems) in the late 1980s, advising Jobs not to be obnoxious. Jobs invested over $100 million of his own money over roughly 10 years to fund Pixar's rendering technology development before Toy Story, which Kay considers 'Steve's finest period,' contrasting with Jobs's later return to Apple focused on making money.
- Kay criticizes modern computing culture, comparing social media to a 'legal drug' and warning that LLMs may encourage people not to use their brains. He explains that human genetic desires like status and story are overproduced in industrial society, and he recommends reading Ivan Sutherland's 'Courage in Research' and learning broadly without immediate purpose, as his theatrical background was crucial for UI design.
- Kay's intellectual influences include Bertrand Russell, who dictated 2,000-3,000 words a day and wrote the 850-page History of Western Philosophy from memory, and Jerome Bruner, with whom Kay had 14-hour conversations. He also recalls Edsger Dijkstra's comment that object-oriented programming was 'an exceptionally bad idea' could only have been invented in California, which Kay believes was just a joke.
- On altered states, Kay says he smoked marijuana as a teenage guitarist but it didn't help his playing; he prefers isolation tanks, invented by John Lilly, which allow awake dreaming without drugs. He notes that Xerox PARC was largely a drinking culture, not heavy drug use.
