# Darren Farber on Iran, China, and the Rise of Neoprimes

## Key Points
- The Iran conflict has consumed enormous munitions stockpiles rapidly — over 6,000 munitions in the first 16 days — raising urgent questions about U.S. magazine depth and readiness for a simultaneous conflict with China
- Defining strategic victory in Iranian contingencies is harder than military planners anticipated, with the Strait of Hormuz closure representing a critical chokepoint for global energy markets
- The U.S. faces a structural tension between two defense doctrines: the Eisenhower approach (technological superiority and deterrence) vs. the Taylor approach (large conventional standing forces) — the Iran war is forcing a reassessment
- "Magazine depth" — the depth of munitions stockpiles relative to war consumption rates — has emerged as the defining vulnerability of the American industrial base, with THAAD interceptors, Patriot PAC-3, and Tomahawk cruise missiles all critically depleted
- Replenishment timelines are measured in years, not months: Patriot PAC-3 requires over two years to replace the first barrage quantity; Tomahawks require a five-year replenishment horizon
- The Iran conflict has materially increased Taiwan risk by demonstrating that the U.S. cannot fight two major conflicts simultaneously with current stockpile levels
- "Neoprimes" — companies like Anduril, Palantir, Shield AI, and SpaceX — are displacing legacy defense contractors (Lockheed, Boeing, Raytheon) by deploying software-first, autonomous systems on faster timelines and with fixed-price contracts
- Congress now mandates that defense programs consider industrial sustainability requirements, creating structural tailwinds for companies that focus on the underlying subsystems and components supply chain
- Albion River launched Ignium in February 2026 with $300M in equity — a platform of six defense component companies — as a direct bet that Western militaries' biggest vulnerability is manufacturing capacity, not weapons design
- AI is transforming modern warfare tactics in ways that are disrupting traditional procurement models and creating new investment opportunities in the defense industrial base
- The most enduring investment value in defense sits with providers that design, qualify, and manufacture the subsystems embedded across multiple platforms — independent of any single prime contractor or platform outcome

## Overview

Darren Farber, Managing Partner of Albion River and Chairman/CEO of Ignium, returns to Invest Like the Best for a second conversation with Patrick O'Shaughnessy. This episode, titled "The New Rules of War," is recorded against the backdrop of the 2026 Iran war and Strait of Hormuz crisis — a conflict that has fundamentally changed the calculus for defense investors and strategists alike. Farber brings a rare dual perspective: he previously served as Special Advisor to the Deputy Under Secretary of Defense and as a member of the DoD Task Force for Business and Stability Operations before becoming a highly successful defense investor.

The conversation centers on what the Iran conflict has revealed about American military readiness. Farber dissects the concept of "magazine depth" — the ratio of available munitions to consumption rates in a real conflict — and argues that the rapid depletion of THAAD interceptors, Patriot missiles, and Tomahawk cruise missiles in the first weeks of the Iran war has exposed a structural gap in the U.S. defense industrial base. The concern is not running out of weapons in this conflict, but rather that depleted stockpiles make a potential China confrontation over Taiwan far more dangerous. This dynamic is reshaping defense procurement priorities at both the Congressional and executive level.

Farber also examines the rise of "neoprimes" — Silicon Valley-born defense companies like Anduril, Palantir, SpaceX, and Shield AI that are challenging the legacy defense industrial complex. Unlike traditional primes (Lockheed Martin, Boeing, Raytheon, Northrop Grumman), neoprimes operate with software-first architectures, deploy operational systems in months rather than years, use fixed-price contracts, and achieve dramatically higher revenue per employee. The Iran conflict has served as a real-world test of these new systems, with autonomous drones and AI-enabled targeting proving far more effective than anticipated. The strategic question for investors is where value accrues in this transition — Farber's thesis is that it accrues to the subsystem and component manufacturers embedded across all platforms, rather than to the platform primes themselves.

## Implications

The Iran war has accelerated what Farber calls the "new rules of war" — a fundamental shift in how conflicts are fought, what weapons matter, and who builds them. For investors, the implications are significant: the defense industrial base is being restructured around software, autonomy, and manufacturing capacity rather than legacy hardware platforms. Congressional mandates for industrial sustainability mean that component and subsystem manufacturers — the companies that provide the critical subsystems that every weapons system depends on — have structural demand regardless of which platform wins procurement competitions. Albion River's Ignium platform, which launched with $300M in equity in February 2026 and unites six component companies across software, power, and energetics verticals, is a direct expression of this thesis.

The Taiwan dimension adds urgency. Magazine depth depletion from the Iran war means that U.S. deterrence posture toward China has weakened, at least temporarily. This creates pressure to dramatically accelerate production capacity — which is itself an investment opportunity but also a test of whether the U.S. industrial base can respond at the speed required. Farber's view, shaped by his DoD experience, is that the bottleneck is not capital or intent but production infrastructure, skilled labor, and critical mineral supply chains. The companies that solve these bottlenecks — whether neoprimes with software solutions or Ignium-style component platforms — will capture disproportionate value in the defense spending cycle that is now clearly underway.
