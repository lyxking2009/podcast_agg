# If Global Trade Breaks, It’s Worse Than 2008 | Ed Conway — The Master Investor Podcast with Wilfred Frost

## TL;DR
Ed Conway, author of Trade World, argues that modern supply chains are more integrated than ever and a breakdown in global trade would dwarf the 2008 financial crisis. He links surging bond yields across the UK, US, and Japan to fiscal sustainability worries, structural market shifts, and geopolitical risks, while explaining how offshoring began in clothing and reshaped economies. Conway warns that current wars and rising trade barriers could be the foothills of a larger conflict, but notes the system's resilience and adaptability.

## Key points
- Global bond yields are surging: Japanese government bonds hit their highest level since the 1990s and UK 30-year yields reached multi-decade highs. Ed Conway attributes this to a confluence of fiscal sustainability concerns, AI-related capital investment expectations, geopolitical reassertion, and the possible reversal of the disinflationary era. Structural changes in the gilt market—hedge funds replacing traditional long-term holders—add to volatility. Despite market pricing for four Bank of England hikes, Conway expects only one or two, and notes the Bank's decision to end quantitative tightening may have temporarily reduced long-end yields but the UK remains an outlier with higher yields than other G7 nations.
- Conway argues that the global economy is far more integrated than in 1914, with supply chains genuinely spanning the globe. He warns that a breakdown in the global trading system would 'make the financial crisis look like a walk in the park.' Approximately 90% of traded goods pass through maritime choke points like Malacca, Bab el-Mandeb, and Panama, making freedom of navigation critical. The example of UK fish fingers—caught in the North Sea, sent to China for processing, then returned—illustrates how invisible and geographically complex supply chains have become.
- Offshoring began in the clothing sector after World War II when US department stores outsourced textile production to Japan and Hong Kong, teaching local manufacturers to meet American consumer preferences. This model was later adopted by technology firms and automakers, contributing to the hollowing out of manufacturing regions in the US, UK, and Europe. Conway links this economic dislocation to the rise of anti-establishment politics, including Donald Trump and Brexit, though he notes the history of offshoring is under-documented.
- Britain's dominance in cotton textiles drove the Industrial Revolution, with cotton's share of UK GDP rising from 2.6% in 1770 to 23% by 1830. The invention of the cotton gin by Eli Whitney enabled massive expansion of American cotton production from 1.5 million pounds in 1790 to 167 million pounds by 1820. Conway argues this innovation inadvertently prolonged and expanded slavery by creating enormous demand for cotton, despite the cotton gin being a labor-saving device. Today, almost all commercial cotton is a single species—American upland—creating monoculture vulnerability to disease.
- Conway highlights how the drive for efficiency has created dangerous monocultures: nearly all cotton worldwide is American upland cotton, and wheat production relies on a narrow genetic base. He notes Russia is now the world's largest wheat exporter, surpassing the US, and is not subject to sanctions on grain because of global food security concerns. The concentration of wheat exports in Russia and Ukraine gives them unprecedented market power, reminiscent of England's historical use of wool as economic leverage.
- Protectionist sentiments are not new: even Adam Smith, the father of free-market economics, supported tariffs on food for national security, stating 'defense is of much more important than opulence.' Conway points out that the current debate over reshoring and tariffs echoes the 19th-century Corn Laws controversy. He argues that Donald Trump's trade policies, while aggressive, reflect a mainstream concern about over-dependence on Chinese manufacturing, and Europe will have to engage with this debate more seriously.
- Despite closure of the Bab el-Mandeb strait by Houthi rebels, global trade proved more resilient than feared: shipping companies rerouted around Africa, adding only marginal cost because transport costs are a tiny fraction of goods' value. Oil prices rose but not as dramatically as expected, partly due to covert shipto-ship transfers near Iran. Conway notes that trade often adapts through gray markets and middlemen, but these adaptations are gradually inflationary, reversing the disinflationary tailwind of globalization.
- Conway is cautiously worried: underlying growth outside AI/data centers is weak, UK manufacturing is shrinking, and energy costs remain high. He draws parallels to the interwar period, warning that current wars in Ukraine and the Gulf could be seen as the foothills of a larger conflict, especially given the unprecedented integration of global supply chains. However, he also emphasizes the wonder and resilience of the global trading system and advises listeners to appreciate the hidden stories behind everyday products.
