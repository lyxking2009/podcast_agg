# Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics – Part III — The Memo by Howard Marks

## TL;DR
In his September 22, 2026 memo, Howard Marks argues that Treasury attempts to suppress long-term interest rates through buybacks treat symptoms rather than causes—such as 3.7% inflation, a 6%-of-GDP deficit, and AI-driven capital demand—and are unlikely to succeed. He contends the U.S. dollar's reserve status lets deficits continue for now but warns that debasement and higher rates are eventual risks, echoing Druckenmiller and Buffett. For investors, he recommends not selling U.S. stocks wholesale but considering limited diversification away from the dollar while urging fiscal responsibility as the only real solution.

## Key points
- Howard Marks argues that government attempts to override the laws of economics are likely to be ineffective and harmful, using the analogy of nature's 'Circle of Life'; interventions such as suppressing predators can cause second-order effects that throw the entire system out of balance.
- The Treasury's August 19 buyback announcement sought to lower long-term rates after the 30-year Treasury yield closed above 5.3% on August 17; it doubled the maximum buyback size from $2 billion to $4 billion and Secretary Bessent indicated a 'whatever-it-takes' willingness, but the yield decline lasted only one day before bouncing back.
- Stanley Druckenmiller, who ran George Soros's Quantum Fund day-to-day during its famous 1992 bet against the Bank of England that earned about $1 billion, wrote in The Wall Street Journal that artificial yield suppression is a subsidy to procrastination and that governments defending prices against fundamentals always lose.
- The rise in long-term rates reflects fundamentals: PCE inflation was 3.7% in July versus the Fed's 2% target, and elevated oil prices associated with the Iran war threaten to keep inflation high, causing investors to demand higher inflation-protection components in long-term bonds.
- U.S. fiscal discipline is absent: the deficit is around 6% of GDP with unemployment at only 4%, net interest outlays exceed $1 trillion (more than the defense budget), and running large deficits during prosperity can be inflationary because government spending adds more liquidity than taxes withdraw.
- Treasury buybacks are financed from general cash resources that are ultimately replenished through additional issuance, so they do not reduce total indebtedness; they may shorten the maturity profile and necessitate more frequent refunding, which is unlikely to bolster confidence in the fiscal picture.
- Strong demand for capital from routine economic growth, massive federal deficits, and the multi-trillion-dollar AI buildout is putting upward pressure on interest rates; McKinsey estimates over $5 trillion will be spent worldwide through 2030 on AI data centers, and net Treasury issuance of roughly $2 trillion annually adds to supply.
- Marks disputes Treasury Secretary Bessent's claim that yields do not reflect underlying fundamentals, arguing they do reflect inflation and fiscal risks; he notes Evercore ISI reported markets were underwhelmed when the first round of buybacks tripled to $6 billion but yields still moved higher.
- The goal should not be to force rates down; it should be to respond to the factors pushing rates up. Marks compares buying bonds to lower yields to a doctor applying an ice pack to a feverish patient—it may lower temperature temporarily but does not cure the underlying illness.
- On whether U.S. debt is a problem, Marks cites Herbert Stein's maxim that if something cannot go on forever, it will stop, but argues the dollar's reserve status—involved in 89% of FX transactions and 57% of allocated reserves in Q1 2026—makes it likely the U.S. can continue financing deficits in its own currency for now, even though gold recently surpassed the dollar as the leading central bank reserve asset per MUFG.
- The real risk is not default but debasement: creating large amounts of dollars can reduce their purchasing power, as illustrated by Weimar Germany's overprinted 1,000 mark note; Warren Buffett warned at Berkshire's May 2025 meeting that the U.S. fiscal deficit is unsustainable over a very long period, though no one knows whether that means two years or twenty.
- The only genuine solution is to change behavior: forget phrases like 'pay down the debt,' adopt fiscal responsibility, flatten the curve, increase revenues as a percentage of GDP through higher income taxes especially at the upper end and eliminating tax preferences, hold spending growth below GDP growth, and boost productivity through AI and pro-business policies while keeping added revenue from being spent.
- For investors, Marks advises against selling U.S. stocks to escape fiscal risk because dollar-denominated assets like bank accounts, money market funds, and bonds do not eliminate the risk; diversification away from the dollar may require non-dollar assets, gold, non-U.S. real estate, or non-U.S. equities, but such moves introduce other risks and should not be done on a large scale.
