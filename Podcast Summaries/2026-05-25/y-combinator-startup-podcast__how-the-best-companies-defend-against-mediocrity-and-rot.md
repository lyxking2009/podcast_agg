## Key Points

- **Shareholder primacy was invented in the 1980s, not by Adam Smith**: The idea that Delaware C Corps must "maximize shareholder value" dates to a normative consensus created by academics and legal scholars in the 1960s-70s, with no legislative mandate or democratic vote. It's ideology, not law.
- **"Incorruptible" is the missing playbook**: Lean Startup taught zero-to-one. Eric Ries' new book protects what you built — because success makes you a more valuable takeover target, not a safer one.
- **The Twilio case study**: Jeff Lawson built Twilio to $4B revenue (390% stock gain from IPO). His founder control shares expired after 7 years. Within 199 days of expiration, investors removed him. He earned "so little grace" despite creating billions in value.
- **Public Benefit Corporations (PBCs) are a must-do**: A 2-page Delaware legal filing that restores "purposeful incorporation." Cheap, fast, doesn't require investor consent if you only have SAFEs. Restores what was the legal default before the 1980s normative shift.
- **FedMart → Price Club → Costco is the foundational story**: Sol Price created FedMart with "fiduciary to the customer" philosophy. Fired in 1975, he built Price Club; a FedMart employee who quit in protest built what merged with Price Club to become Costco — which still runs Sol's philosophy behind a "governance fortress."
- **Novo Nordisk's nonprofit structure created $600B in value**: A 1920s two-entity structure (for-profit subsidiary + nonprofit foundation trustees) protected GLP-1 R&D through 10+ years of uncertain results. Trustees blocked a merger that would have killed the research program. Companies with this structure are 6x more likely to reach year 50.
- **Independent directors are structurally investor-aligned**: Nominally independent directors have no financial stake in the mission — but they get future board jobs through investor recommendation. Classic "2 VCs, 2 founders, 1 independent" = investor control, not balance.
- **Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust is the model**: Perpetual purpose trust where outside trustees appoint directors. Structural integrity allowed Anthropic to turn down a $200M contract, had sidewalks chalked with "thank you," and became the employer the best AI safety talent wants to work for.
- **Ethos + integrity = incorruptible**: Clear higher principle (what you stand for) + structural protections (legal frameworks, governance design) that remain defensible when money and power try to override them.
- **Dual class shares alone are insufficient**: They expire, get defeated during stock declines, or die with the founder. The book advocates structural solutions that survive any individual — foundation models, perpetual purpose trusts, or at minimum, documented fallback structures.

## Overview

Eric Ries — author of *The Lean Startup* — joins the YC podcast to discuss *Incorruptible: Why Good Companies Go Bad and How Great Companies Stay Great*, a book about protecting the companies that Lean Startup taught people to build.

**The core diagnosis**: We have built an economy of temporary organizations led by temporary managers on behalf of temporary investors. Average stock holding periods, executive tenures, and company lifespans are all dramatically down. Trust in institutions has collapsed — causally connected to governance structures, not a cultural accident.

**Historical arc of shareholder primacy**: For most of corporate history, companies were chartered with specific public purposes. Shareholder primacy — the idea that a company exists only to maximize investor returns — was imposed through 1960s-70s academic consensus (Milton Friedman's famous 1970 NYT op-ed), normalized as if it were settled law, and was never subject to any vote. Delaware's 1899 general incorporation law still assumed companies would have specific purposes.

**The Sol Price / Costco lineage**: Sol Price was a lawyer who applied a "fiduciary to customer" principle to FedMart (1950s San Diego), the template for Walmart (Sam Walton named it in tribute). Fired in 1975, Price built Price Club. A FedMart employee who quit in protest built the company that merged with Price Club to become Costco. Costco still runs on Sol's philosophy, consistently earns the worst possible "governance ratings" from rating agencies, and has massively outperformed governance-best-practice Kroger.

**Novo Nordisk's 100-year model**: In the 1920s, Danish scientists built an insulin company as a for-profit subsidiary of a nonprofit foundation, explicitly worried about life-saving medicine under profit maximization (they foresaw Martin Shkreli a century early). Foundation trustees blocked a merger in the early 2000s that would have ended GLP-1 research after 10+ years with no proof of efficacy. That research became Ozempic and Wegovy. Novo Nordisk briefly exceeded Denmark's GDP in market cap. The preserved value: $600B+.

**Anthropic as proof of concept**: Ries helped design Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust. Structural integrity allowed them to turn down a $200M contract, Claude went to #1 when they did it, and the company has a massive talent moat because the best AI safety researchers believe in the mission's protection. Even with a carefully curated cap table, a large chunk of Anthropic ended up in bankruptcy proceedings via SBF's FTX — proof that careful curation isn't enough without structure.

## Implications

- **For YC founders**: Converting to a PBC costs almost nothing and should be default. If you only have SAFEs, you need zero investor consent. The governance documents you sign at formation lock in your trajectory.
- **For corporate governance**: The "best practice" of independent directors, shareholder primacy, and frequent turnover has empirically destroyed value (Twilio, FedMart, Polaroid). The Costco/Novo Nordisk model outperforms on every metric.
- **For the AI ecosystem**: Anthropic's governance model may become a template for AI labs serious about safety. Structural integrity creates a compounding talent and trust moat.
- **For capitalism broadly**: The 40-50 year run of shareholder primacy is ending. Younger generations who have seen its failure are ready for alternatives. The legal tools already exist.
