## Key Points

- **Two pubs close every day in the UK**: Long-running structural decline from changing leisure habits, smoking bans, regulation, rising costs — but the best pubs are entering a golden age.
- **Food transformed pub economics**: The 2007 smoking ban was an "economic earthquake" that drove old drinkers out but attracted new audiences. Food — from gastronomy to Scotch eggs — completely changes the margin and footfall profile.
- **The Devonshire model**: Entry-level pricing (pint from £4.95, set menu £29.95) alongside high-end options (£150 dry-aged beef). Volume and accessibility are the philosophy; margins run 15–25% when full.
- **The science of perfect Guinness**: Proprietary 82/18 nitrogen/CO2 gas mix, specific keg temperature staging, shortest possible pipe runs, 13–14 second pour, 90-second rest. Studied in Dublin before opening.
- **Labor market is healthy, counter to the narrative**: 18–23 year-olds are the most reliable, hard-working employees. Brexit reduced some EU casual labour but new channels emerged; culture and pay competitiveness matter more than supply.
- **Inflation hits food hardest**: Food input costs rose dramatically since 2021; The Devonshire raised some prices only once in 2.5 years. Response is smarter purchasing, waste reduction, and production efficiency — not repricing.
- **Partnership structure is critical**: Three partners with non-overlapping skills — Charlie (commercial/financial), Oishin (front-of-house/operations), Ashley (food/product). Complementary skills over shared ones.
- **Sound design is intentional**: No TVs, no background music, sound-deadening materials, nooks and booths. Ambient conversation and clinking glasses is the deliberate atmosphere.
- **Supplier relationships are long-term**: Three-year price lock-ins on drinks; direct relationships with farmers and fishermen. Pay a fair price for the best quality — driving prices down destroys relationships.
- **Gen Z drinking less is overblown**: Rogers pushes back firmly — young people still pile in and have a great time; slightly healthier overall, but GLP-1 drugs may be a marginal headwind.

## Overview

Bloomberg's Joe Wisenthal and Tracy Alloway record a special two-part episode from London. Part 1 interviews Oishin Rogers (co-founder of The Devonshire pub, near Piccadilly); Part 2 is a live-show interview with chef Ashley Palmer-Watts (formerly head chef at Heston Blumenthal's The Fat Duck).

**Part 1 — Oishin Rogers on the business of pubs:**
Rogers traces the history of British pubs from 1,000-year-old institutions to the modern landscape of managed house chains (Stonegate, Wetherspoons, Mitchells & Butlers) versus freehouses. The Devonshire occupies a former Jamie Oliver restaurant that was itself previously a pub; reclaiming the pub license in Westminster took years. 

The Devonshire's economics: 165 staff, entry-level pricing alongside premium options, obsessive attention to atmosphere, cleanliness, and staff wellbeing. The 2007 smoking ban was the inflection point that forced pub modernisation — losing old drinkers but enabling a new audience expecting better food and no smoke. The gastro-pub spectrum ranges from wet pubs (drinks only) to near-restaurants; The Devonshire deliberately lives in the middle with both a formal upstairs restaurant and a chaotic ground-floor bar.

Rogers diagnoses pub closures as historical: leisure time is now divided across sport, streaming, concerts, and screens. But he argues we're entering a golden age for pubs as people crave real, chaotic shared human experiences — precisely because screens and AI can't replicate them.

**Part 2 — Ashley Palmer-Watts on the kitchen:**
Palmer-Watts (previously responsible for 5 Michelin stars at The Fat Duck and Dinner by Heston) details the Guinness process with scientific precision, the menu design methodology (three days of food conversation in Soho), and how to scale Michelin-quality cooking to 650 covers per day. They make their own bread, butter, and bacon. Menu storytelling is deliberate — each dish has a provenance. The restaurant has raised prices only once since opening, preferring operational efficiency over passing on costs.

## Implications

- **For macroeconomists**: A pub is a real-time meter of UK consumer health — labour costs, food inflation, leisure spending, and demographic trends visible in miniature. Ashley's "nothing ever comes down" on food inputs is a useful data point.
- **For investors in hospitality**: The sector is bifurcating — chain operators struggling with commodity menus and central purchasing versus destination pubs with authentic storytelling thriving despite macro headwinds.
- **For the UK economy**: The pub industry embodies the labour market, Brexit effects, and SME regulation all at once. Licensing burdens, noise ordinances, and business rates remain barriers to entry.
- **For technology pessimists/optimists**: Rogers' observation that customers crave real, chaotic human shared experiences — specifically in contrast to the 2D AI future — is a meaningful counter-signal on social behaviour.
