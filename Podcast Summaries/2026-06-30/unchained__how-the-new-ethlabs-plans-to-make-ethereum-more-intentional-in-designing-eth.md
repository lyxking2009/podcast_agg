## Key Points

- Ethlabs is a new independent nonprofit R&D lab launched on June 22, 2026, founded by five former Ethereum Foundation researchers: Ansgar Dietrichs (executive director), Barnabé Monnot, Caspar Schwarz-Schilling, Josh Rudolf, and Julian Ma — each previously working on protocol finality, scaling, data availability, VM development, and economics.
- The lab was created to fill a gap left by the Ethereum Foundation's deliberate narrowing of scope: the EF will "protect the things in Ethereum that should not change," while Ethlabs pushes "the parts of Ethereum that must evolve."
- Ethlabs is funded by Bitmine Immersion Technologies (NYSE: BMNR, which holds 5.42 million ETH), SharpLink (NASDAQ: SBET), and Joe Lubin (Ethereum co-founder), plus Anchorage, Octant, SNZ, and 50+ community partners. Funding flows through an independent grants administrator to preserve research independence, with quarterly reporting and annual audits.
- The lab has a 2-3 year runway and plans a team of approximately 20 people.
- Dietrichs argues that only eight crypto-native assets (excluding stablecoins) have market caps above $10 billion, and only Bitcoin and Ethereum exceed $100 billion — a position ETH has held for five years without clear articulation of its value story.
- A central concern is that ETH currently has the worst interoperability properties of any major asset in the Ethereum ecosystem: unlike many other assets that can "teleport" between chains natively, ETH cannot move between L2s without bridges.
- Ethlabs' six initial work streams are: L1 scaling, blob scaling, interoperability solutions, institutional adoption support, agentic finance, and DeFi infrastructure.
- The 15-minute finality problem — the roughly 15 minutes it takes a transaction to become irreversible under Ethereum's Gasper consensus mechanism — is a key technical target for Ethlabs research.
- Dietrichs stresses that ETH's fee burn mechanism was never an intentional design decision and should be reconsidered as a deliberate choice reflecting the asset's economic role.

## Overview

Laura Shin interviews Ansgar Dietrichs, executive director of the newly launched Ethlabs, exploring why five former Ethereum Foundation researchers decided to form an independent nonprofit R&D lab just weeks after the EF cut its staff by 20% and budget by 40%. Dietrichs explains the division of labor: the Foundation will retreat to its core mandate of protecting Ethereum's immutable properties, while Ethlabs takes on the work of evolving the parts of Ethereum that must change to compete and grow. The key distinction is that Ethlabs is explicitly market-facing, opinionated about Ethereum's role in global finance, and willing to engage with competitive dynamics that the Foundation's culture historically avoided.

A large portion of the conversation centers on the problem of intentionality. Dietrichs argues that many of Ethereum's current design decisions — including the fee burn introduced in EIP-1559, the relationship between L1 and L2s, and ETH's interoperability properties — were not deliberate choices but rather accidents of the technical development process. He makes the case that Ethereum must consciously define what ETH is economically: not just as a collateral asset or a gas token, but as the programmable store of value and settlement medium for the global onchain economy. Without this clarity, Ethereum risks ceding ground to other blockchains or fragmented multi-chain architectures, even as institutional adoption accelerates.

Dietrichs also describes Ethlabs' organizational model and how it differs from the EF. The lab sits "between two worlds" — working directly with builders like Uniswap's Hayden Adams to understand real pain points (contract size limits, cross-chain liquidity fragmentation), then translating those into protocol work, shared standards, and infrastructure. With funding tied to neither the EF nor profit-seeking entities, Dietrichs believes Ethlabs can maintain genuine neutrality focused exclusively on what is good for Ethereum. He also addresses concerns about Ethlabs potentially competing with the EF, acknowledging the tension openly: some backers have admitted the two organizations will be in competition for research directions, not just complementary.

## Implications

Ethlabs represents a structural shift in how Ethereum's core development ecosystem is organized. Rather than a single foundation controlling research direction, the ecosystem is moving toward a network of independent, specialized organizations — Ethlabs, Etherealize, Consensys — each with distinct mandates. This creates both opportunities (more market-responsive research, clearer accountability) and risks (coordination failure, duplication of effort, competition for talent and influence). The fact that major ETH treasury companies like Bitmine and SharpLink are funding Ethlabs raises legitimate questions about whether large ETH holders can steer protocol decisions, even through an independent grants administrator structure.

The episode highlights that Ethereum's most urgent challenges are not purely technical but narrative and economic: ETH's value proposition remains vague compared to Bitcoin's digital gold story, and the relationship between L1 security and L2 value capture is unresolved. If Ethlabs succeeds in making these design decisions explicit and intentional — rather than emergent — it could strengthen ETH's long-term monetary premium. But if the effort fragments Ethereum governance further or is perceived as capture by financial interests, it could accelerate the credibility erosion that the EF's restructuring was meant to address.
