## Key Points

- Neil deGrasse Tyson and comedian co-host Negin Farsad sit down with astrophysicist Mordecai-Mark Mac Low (Curator, Department of Astrophysics, American Museum of Natural History) to discuss "peculiar galaxies" — the catalogue of oddly shaped galaxies that first prompted astronomers to ask why some galaxies look so different from others.
- Galaxy collisions at the stellar level are extremely rare because the distances between individual stars are vast; what actually collides and interacts are the giant gas clouds within galaxies, which can trigger bursts of star formation.
- Active galactic nuclei (AGN) and quasars represent supermassive black holes actively feeding on surrounding material, making them among the brightest objects in the universe — sometimes outshining entire galaxies containing hundreds of billions of stars.
- Planets can form around black holes via accretion disks: Jupiter-mass rocky worlds are theorized to accumulate from the material in these disks, though conditions for habitability near a black hole differ drastically from those around ordinary stars.
- Dark matter plays a critical role in galaxy formation and structure; computer simulations that incorporate dark matter distribution can reproduce the large-scale structure of the universe, including the filaments and voids observed in galaxy surveys.
- Mac Low's research uses magnetohydrodynamical (MHD) supercomputer simulations to study why stars form from interstellar gas in some regions of galaxies but not others, a question that bridges scales from sub-light-year cloud cores to thousands of light-years of galactic structure.
- The 1994 Shoemaker-Levy 9 comet impact on Jupiter serves as a real-world example of the kind of violent cosmic collisions that shaped planetary and solar system history, providing insight into Earth's early bombardment era.
- Large moons like Earth's Moon are now understood to be relatively common around exoplanets; moon formation via giant impact is a process that likely recurs across planetary systems throughout the galaxy.
- If Jupiter were to accumulate significantly more mass — roughly 13 times its current mass — it would cross the threshold for deuterium burning and qualify as a brown dwarf, though igniting full hydrogen fusion as a true star would require far more mass still (~80 Jupiter masses).

## Overview

In this episode of StarTalk Radio (Season 17, Episode 39), Neil deGrasse Tyson and comedian Negin Farsad welcome Mordecai-Mark Mac Low, Curator of Astrophysics at the American Museum of Natural History, for a wide-ranging conversation about peculiar galaxies, galaxy collisions, dark matter, and the surprising planetary systems that can orbit supermassive black holes. The episode takes its name from the informal nickname astronomers sometimes give to oddly shaped galaxies that, in optical images, resemble the splattered remains of an insect on a windshield — a visual shorthand for the chaotic aftermath of galactic interactions. Mac Low explains that when two galaxies pass through each other or merge, individual stars almost never collide; instead, it is the interstellar gas clouds that smash together, compressing and heating gas in ways that can trigger massive waves of new star formation.

The conversation moves through the exotic landscape of active galactic nuclei, exploring how supermassive black holes accreting material from surrounding disks can radiate energy equivalent to trillions of suns. Tyson and Mac Low examine what kinds of planetary systems might exist around these black holes — accretion disk material can, over time, clump into Jupiter-mass rocky bodies — and whether such worlds could ever support life, given the extreme radiation environment. The episode also revisits a vivid example from our own solar system: the 1994 impact of Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 into Jupiter, which provided direct observational evidence of high-velocity collisions in action and illustrated the violence that characterized the early solar system.

Dark matter anchors the final major section of the episode. Mac Low describes how cosmological simulations that include dark matter halos successfully reproduce the web-like large-scale structure of the universe, while simulations without dark matter fail to match what telescopes observe. He also touches on the timescales involved in star and planet formation — molecular cloud cores collapse over millions of years, planetary systems take tens of millions more to stabilize — and on the prevalence of large moons, suggesting Earth's Moon-forming giant impact was not a one-off cosmic accident but part of a broader pattern in planetary system evolution.

## Implications

The episode highlights how astrophysics has moved from purely descriptive classification of strange-looking galaxies to a mechanistic understanding rooted in supercomputer simulation. The fact that galaxy mergers drive star formation rather than destroy it reframes collisions as creative rather than purely destructive forces in the cosmos, with direct consequences for how astronomers interpret the spike in star formation rates observed in the early universe when galaxies were more densely packed and mergers more frequent.

The discussion of planetary systems orbiting black holes broadens the concept of where life might theoretically arise and challenges anthropocentric assumptions about what constitutes a habitable environment. Meanwhile, Mac Low's emphasis on dark matter as the gravitational scaffolding of all large-scale structure underscores how much of the universe's architecture is governed by matter we cannot directly see — a reminder that visible stars and galaxies represent only a small fraction of what actually exists, and that computational modeling has become as essential to cosmology as the telescope itself.

## Notable Quotes

- "When people imagine galaxies colliding, they picture stars smashing into each other — but stars are so tiny relative to the distances between them that two galaxies can pass right through each other and barely a single star gets hit." — Mordecai-Mark Mac Low
- "It's the gas clouds that collide. And when they do, the compression triggers enormous bursts of new star formation — the collision actually makes the galaxy more fertile." — Mordecai-Mark Mac Low
- "A quasar is essentially a black hole that is eating, and eating very fast. The energy released from that feeding can outshine every star in the galaxy combined." — Neil deGrasse Tyson
- "We can build simulations with dark matter and without dark matter. The ones without dark matter produce a universe that looks nothing like the one we actually live in." — Mordecai-Mark Mac Low
- "Shoemaker-Levy 9 was nature doing an experiment for us — it showed us, in real time, what it looks like when something from the outer solar system comes screaming in and hits a planet." — Neil deGrasse Tyson
- "Accretion disks are universal. Wherever you have mass falling onto a central object — a star, a black hole, even a baby planet — you get a disk. It's just geometry and angular momentum." — Mordecai-Mark Mac Low
- "If Jupiter had about thirteen times more mass, it would start burning deuterium and we'd call it a brown dwarf. Our solar system almost had two suns — almost." — Neil deGrasse Tyson
