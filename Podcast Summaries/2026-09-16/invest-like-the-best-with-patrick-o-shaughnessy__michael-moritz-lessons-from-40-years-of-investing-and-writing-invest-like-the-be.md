# Michael Moritz - Lessons From 40 Years of Investing and Writing - [Invest Like the Best, EP.491] — Invest Like the Best with Patrick O'Shaughnessy

## TL;DR

Michael Moritz reflects on his 40-year career in investing and writing, discussing how his family's refugee history shaped his outsider mindset and drive. He details his approach to evaluating founders via early-life experiences, his time at Sequoia, and lessons from leaders like Steve Jobs, Don Valentine, Alex Ferguson, and Elon Musk. Moritz also shares his views on the current venture capital landscape, AI's impact, and the importance of enduring institutions and relationships.

## Key points

- Moritz attributes his drive and vigilance to growing up as the child of Jewish refugees from Nazi Germany in Wales; his parents never used the word 'survivor' but instilled a constant sense that 'doom was not very far away' and the need to be wary.
- He contrasts his two grandfathers: the paternal one, a civil servant with a state pension, stayed in Germany and perished; the maternal one, a cattle trader, left 72 hours before war broke out and survived, illustrating the danger of relying on institutions versus entrepreneurial street wisdom.
- Moritz's method for evaluating people focuses on their first 12-14 years, inspired by Carl Jung and his own experience writing about Lee Iacocca, whose Italian immigrant outsider feelings shaped his career.
- A key interview question he uses: 'If you could go back and change one thing, what would you do?' One Hispanic founder broke down recalling stealing candy and his father's disciplinary reaction.
- He believes great leaders drive history, citing Elon Musk as possibly the greatest industrialist in American history, whose achievements across multiple industries leave him 'gobsmacked'.
- Moritz was hired by Don Valentine at Sequoia in 1985 despite no technical background, because Valentine saw success in non-traditional investors like Arthur Rock; Moritz credits this to luck and imperfect information that no longer exists.
- He argues venture capital today is far more difficult due to universal information access and too many intermediaries, and he doubts he would flourish in the current environment.
- Upon taking over Sequoia in 1996, his priorities were not to screw it up, ensure no resting on laurels, and do right by investors, including expanding internationally to Israel and China and creating Sequoia Heritage.
- Moritz reflects on his partnership with Doug Leone, emphasizing the need for tolerance and understanding others' motivations, a lesson reinforced by Alex Ferguson's ability to support players like David Beckham in dark times.
- He describes Alex Ferguson's leadership: Ferguson didn't want to be loved or feared but respected, and he supported players privately, such as meeting Beckham at the airport after World Cup criticism.
- On AI, Moritz is cautiously optimistic, citing historical fears about nuclear weapons and genetic engineering that did not lead to catastrophe; he expects net job creation but believes extraordinary writing will become more valuable.
- He sees the internet as amplifying extremes and making civility harder, paralleling radio's role in Nazi Germany, and notes that economic distress can be exploited by authoritarian leaders.
- Moritz's Time magazine profile of Steve Jobs in the 1980s severed their relationship; he respected Jobs but did not hold him in awe because he knew his flaws, and he believes monomania drives great achievements at the cost of relationships.
- His mother's constant predictions of doom and fault-finding left him unable to celebrate success and feeling that everything is unfinished; he attributes this to heritage and notes it still affects him.
- For parenting, Moritz advises arming children with confidence and freedom, avoiding being censorious, and regrets his own interactions with his parents due to arrogance and lack of understanding.
- He co-founded the San Francisco Standard with Griff Gaffne to prove local journalism can be profitable, maintain editorial independence, and provide a center-right opinion voice in the city.
- Moritz believes the toughest challenge in life and writing is being in touch with your inner voice and overcoming outside expectations; he feels he hasn't fully achieved this, echoing Joseph Campbell's 'privilege of a lifetime is becoming who you are.'
- Reflecting on his family history, he identifies three categories in crises: resistors, silent, and collaborators, and believes true resistors are rare, as seen in the example of Anna, his father's nanny, who helped his family.
- He credits Bill Deeds, editor of the Daily Telegraph, for encouraging him to move to America, which led to his job at Time and subsequent career in venture capital.
- On stepping back from Sequoia, Moritz believes leaders should get out of the way, and he went 'cold turkey' to avoid lingering; he now focuses on painting, philanthropy, and media, but misses the monomaniacal focus.
