# #502 – Psychiatry, Insane Asylums, Mental Illness, ECT, Lobotomies, Freud &#038; Jung — Lex Fridman Podcast

## TL;DR

Andrew Scull, historian of psychiatry, argues that modern psychiatry is in crisis: despite over $20 billion in NIMH research, diagnostic categories like schizophrenia and bipolar disorder remain symptom-based and unreliable, and existing drugs are only partially effective with serious side effects. He traces the field's dark history from asylum overcrowding and eugenics to lobotomy, insulin coma, and early ECT, showing how misplaced biological enthusiasm led to catastrophic abuses. Scull sees limited hope in psychosocial supports, cautious use of drugs and talk therapy, and more humility about the complexity of the mind.

## Key points

- Modern psychiatry faces a crisis: NIMH directors Thomas Insel and Steven Hyman invested over $20 billion in genetics and neuroscience expecting to root diagnoses in brain pathology, but Insel admitted that 'the lot of the mentally ill has improved not one bit' after 13 years of funding.
- The DSM evolved from a 32-page pamphlet in 1952 with 106 vague psychoanalytic categories to DSM-III in 1980, led by Robert Spitzer, which introduced explicit symptom-based checklists to improve reliability; however, this reliability does not equal validity, and genetic studies show overlap between schizophrenia, bipolar, and autism, undermining these categories.
- Serious mental illness shortens life by 15–25 years, and the largest centers of inpatient psychiatric care in the US are Los Angeles County Jail, Cook County Jail in Chicago, and Rikers Island in New York—evidence of public policy failure.
- The asylum era began with optimism and claims of 60–80% cure rates, but chronic patient accumulation led to overcrowding, degeneration theory, and eugenics; over 60,000 sterilizations were performed in the US, with California disproportionately high, continuing into the 1960s.
- Nazi Germany adopted American eugenic ideas and under Hitler's T4 program killed up to 250,000 mentally ill people, developing the gas chamber technology later used in the Holocaust; German psychiatry largely collaborated.
- The Rockefeller Foundation funded German psychiatric geneticist Ernst Rüdin, a key architect of Nazi sterilization and extermination laws, demonstrating financial ties between American philanthropy and Nazi eugenics.
- General paralysis of the insane (GPI) was identified as tertiary syphilis, spurring hope that mental illness had infectious origins; Julius Wagner-Jauregg won the 1937 Nobel Prize for malaria fever therapy to treat GPI, though claims were exaggerated and the treatment was replaced by penicillin.
- Henry Cotton at Trenton State Hospital promoted focal sepsis theory, surgically removing teeth, tonsils, stomachs, spleens, and colons; he claimed 80% cure rates but 45% of abdominal surgery patients died within a year, and the practice of tooth extraction continued until 1960.
- Insulin coma therapy, introduced by Manfred Sakel in 1933, induced hypoglycemic comas for schizophrenia, requiring up to 60 comas with 1–5% mortality and significant brain damage; it failed a 1950s randomized controlled trial and was abandoned.
- Metrazol-induced convulsions, based on the false belief that epilepsy and schizophrenia were antagonistic, caused violent seizures, fractures, and a feeling of impending death, and was used until ECT offered a cheaper alternative.
- Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) originated when Ugo Cerletti and Lucio Bini observed electric stunning of pigs in a Rome slaughterhouse and applied electrodes to a human; early unmodified ECT caused fractures and memory loss and was used as a disciplinary tool in the 1950s-60s.
- Lobotomy: Egas Moniz won the Nobel Prize in 1949, and Walter Freeman popularized the ice-pick lobotomy, traveling in his 'Lobotomobile' and performing 20–30 operations per day; many patients were left incontinent and zombie-like, and the practice died out due generational change and antipsychotics.
- The film One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest, based on Ken Kesey's experience, powerfully shaped public perception of ECT and psychiatry, though modern ECT with muscle relaxants and anesthesia has been reported as lifesaving for some severe depression despite ongoing memory and brain damage concerns.
- Psychoanalysis began with Freud and Josef Breuer's case of Anna O., developing free association and the id-ego-superego model; Freud and Jung brought psychoanalysis to America at Clark University in 1909, but mainstream asylum psychiatry ignored it.
- Psychoanalysis expanded after World War II due to shell shock and VA training programs, but analysts resisted evidence-based evaluation; cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) emerged from Aaron Beck, Albert Ellis, and Albert Bandura, focusing on symptom relief, shorter treatment, and measurable outcomes.
- The first antipsychotic chlorpromazine (Thorazine) was discovered accidentally when French Navy surgeon Henri Laborit noted it produced a 'chemical lobotomy'; it was brought to North America by Heinz Lehmann and marketed by SmithKline & French, with two million patients taking it within two years.
- Antipsychotics reduce positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) but not negative symptoms (apathy, social withdrawal); they cause tardive dyskinesia, Parkinsonism, and restlessness, and these side effects were ignored for about 20 years until George Crane's 1970s paper.
- The 2005 CATIE study found no efficacy difference between first- and second-generation antipsychotics, but 67–82% of patients dropped out due to side effects; second-generation drugs cause severe weight gain, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes.
- Antidepressants like SSRIs (e.g., Prozac) were marketed on the serotonin imbalance theory, but they beat placebo only marginally for many patients; over 40% are non-responders, and side effects include emotional numbing, sexual dysfunction, and difficult withdrawal.
- Diagnostic creep has expanded categories like autism and depression, driven by loosened criteria and pharmaceutical marketing; NICE now recommends cognitive behavioral therapy over drugs for mild depression.
- Scull advocates broadening psychiatric research beyond biology and drugs to include psychosocial supports, reducing loneliness, and restoring agency; he urges humility about hype for psychedelics and ketamine, and sees potential in AI-assisted therapy but cautions against overclaiming.
