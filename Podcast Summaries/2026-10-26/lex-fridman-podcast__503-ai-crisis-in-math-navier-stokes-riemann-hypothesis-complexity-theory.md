# #503 – AI Crisis in Math: Navier-Stokes, Riemann Hypothesis & Complexity Theory — Lex Fridman Podcast

## TL;DR
Lex Fridman discusses AI's recent breakthroughs in mathematics with Steven Brunton, Ankur Moitra, and Alex Kontorovich. OpenAI released solutions to hundreds of problems, including quasi-Riemann hypothesis, Navier-Stokes blowup, and the Unique Games Conjecture, while Anthropic formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean. The guests explore what this means for mathematicians, arguing that human curiosity, question-asking, and understanding will remain valuable even if AI can prove theorems, and they offer advice for navigating the changing landscape.

## Key points
- OpenAI's drop of 700+ results includes proofs of the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, Navier-Stokes finite-time blowup with smooth forcing, the Unique Games Conjecture, and many others, shocking mathematicians like Terence Tao.
- Anthropic formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean with 13 million lines of code, involving only 2-3 humans, overturning Alex Kontorovich's prediction that such a formalization was years away.
- Alex Kontorovich describes the moment as a 'chaos of opportunity,' not just a crisis, and says we may be entering a golden age of mathematical knowledge.
- Ankur Moitra feels intellectually lonely because great results often came from humble questions, and the old playbook for success for grad students is thrown out.
- Steven Brunton, as an engineer, is hopeful for applied technology but notes the proof of Navier-Stokes blowup doesn't immediately help solve the trillion-dollar engineering problem of efficient turbulence prediction and inverse design.
- The 'solved axiom' is proposed: if AI can solve any problem a human+AI could solve, it would not necessarily destroy mathematics because math's value lies in human understanding and stories, not just theorem statements.
- The doorman fallacy is used as an analogy: mathematicians do more than prove theorems; they build theories, make connections, and provide understanding, which may become more valuable.
- Unique Games Conjecture was proved true; Subhash Khot, who introduced it, said he would not read the proof, reflecting mixed emotions about AI solving foundational problems.
- Anthropic's 3SUM result (faster algorithm) surprised the fine-grained complexity community and changed the organizational landscape of the field, showing even single algorithmic improvements can reshape research directions.
- Quasi-Riemann hypothesis proof gives a zero-free strip from real part 7/8 to 1; the methods cannot reach 1/2, so a new idea is needed for full RH, but the AI chained many existing techniques in a non-obvious way.
- Navier-Stokes counterexample constructs a specialized vortex that stretches and thins in 3D, causing infinite vorticity in finite time while keeping energy finite, but it operates in a non-physical regime.
- Lean and Mathlib have become central: autoformalization of existing written math is essentially solved, enabling sorry-free formal proofs and shifting communication of mathematics toward stories plus formal verification.
- Ankur Moitra emphasizes that natural data may make hard computational problems easy (e.g., protein folding, SAT solvers), and understanding the structure that makes problems easy is an important research direction.
- Subliminal learning experiment: fine-tuning an LLM on random numbers from an owl-obsessed model transfers the owl preference, illustrating hidden statistical relationships and alignment challenges.
- For young people, advice includes staying curious, learning fundamentals, focusing on intrinsic motivation, and pretending you already have tenure to choose meaningful problems; AI tools can help learn but human skills like asking good questions remain crucial.
- The mathematics community faces institutional changes: journals overloaded, need new models for reviewing AI-generated proofs, possibly more collaborative/crowdsourced verification, and rethinking what achievements to celebrate (e.g., Fields Medal future).
