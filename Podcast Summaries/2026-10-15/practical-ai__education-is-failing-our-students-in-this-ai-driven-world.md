# Education is Failing our Students in this AI-Driven World

**Show:** Practical AI  
**Published:** 2026-10-15  
**Transcript source:** `rss_transistor_vtt`

## Overview

A two-host episode with Daniel Whitenack and his co-host Chris Benson (no guest), prompted by Chris expecting his first child and both hosts thinking about what education should look like in an AI-driven world. The conversation is about what happens to foundational skills when AI handles execution: Chris notes that his own Rust proficiency matters less than it did a year ago because he can turn out working code quickly and spend his time on creative direction, and that AI-assisted research has changed his authoring workflow. Their argument is that the struggle does not disappear when you offload work — it relocates — and that if education lags, students will pay for it in a workplace that no longer rewards the old way of demonstrating competence.

## Key Points

- Both hosts are thinking about education because of personal circumstances — Chris expecting his first child, and having raised two stepchildren through public school.
- Chris reports a concrete personal shift: his Rust skill matters less than a year ago because AI lets him produce code quickly, redirecting his effort to creative and directional work.
- The core claim: struggle does not disappear when work is offloaded — it gets placed somewhere else, and the new location is what education needs to target.
- Chris mentors new graduates arriving in the workplace and sees early- and mid-20s hires struggling to adjust to how work is now structured.
- The concern is compounded by foundational skills: if students offload too early, they may lack the judgement to evaluate what AI produces.
- AI has genuinely supercharged productivity — Chris says his output is far above what was possible a year ago — so the problem is not that AI fails at the work.
- Both hosts work with universities in advisory roles and see the adjustment happening too slowly.
- Conclusion: the self-reflection process in education and in workplaces needs to speed up.

## Implications

The useful reframing for anyone training juniors is that removing struggle entirely is a mistake — if AI does the hard part, the skill you were building never forms, and the person cannot tell good output from bad. The concrete implication is that entry-level roles need to be redesigned around evaluation and direction rather than production, which is precisely the work that is hardest to give to someone without judgement.
