## Overview

Jensen Huang joins Brad Gerstner and Clark Tang for his third BG2 conversation, recorded days after the Xi State Dinner, the White House Accord on superintelligence, and his receipt of the National Medal of Science. The through-line is whether AI revenues can justify the spending. Huang breaks down the biggest bottlenecks heading into 2027, the path to the singularity, open versus closed models, and says he is 99.99% optimistic about the decade ahead. They explore why building technology responsibly also means moving fast, how power constraints could shape the next phase, whether SpaceX could help put data centers in orbit, and why NVIDIA sees demand extending well beyond two AI labs. The chapter list is unusually specific on the numbers: 50 GW of chips against 35 GW of power, Vera Rubin Ultra orders, and a review of Wall Street consensus.

## Key Points

- Huang's answer to the central question — does AI revenue justify the capex — is framed through demand that extends well beyond the two leading labs, not just the current hyper-scaler order book.
- Explicit power arithmetic: 50 GW of chips against 35 GW of power, which makes power the binding constraint rather than silicon.
- The 2027 bottleneck list is about the compute build-out, not model capability.
- A "three-legged stool" framework for America's superintelligence posture, discussed under what "America winning" means.
- A safety analogy to airbags, and an argument that building responsibly also means moving fast.
- The falling cost of intelligence, discussed via Navier-Stokes.
- Physical AI and robotics as the next demand vector after language models.
- Elon, Colossus and SpaceX: whether data centers in space are a real option, and SpaceX's possible role in putting them there.
- Open vs. closed models, and the risk of putting "too much pressure on two labs."
- Vera Rubin Ultra orders, global reach across Europe, Japan and beyond, and his stated 99.99% confidence in the decade ahead.

## Implications

The power-versus-chips arithmetic (50 GW of chips, 35 GW of power) is the most consequential number in the episode: it moves the constraint from semiconductor supply to electricity generation and grid interconnection, which changes which parts of the supply chain benefit. And the "too much pressure on two labs" point is a demand-diversification argument — NVIDIA wants the customer base broadened precisely because concentration in two buyers is the main risk to its own revenue line.
