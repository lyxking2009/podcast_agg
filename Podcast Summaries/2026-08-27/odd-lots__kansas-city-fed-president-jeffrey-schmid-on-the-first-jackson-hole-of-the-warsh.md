# Kansas City Fed President Jeffrey Schmid on the First Jackson Hole of the Warsh Era — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Kansas City Fed President Jeffrey Schmid discusses the Jackson Hole symposium's focus on financial innovation in payments, including atomic settlement and the need for liquidity and duration. He notes the economy is growing, but inflation remains above target and must be addressed, with several dissents at recent FOMC meetings. Schmid praises Chair Kevin Warsh's debate-friendly style while acknowledging uncertainty about the Fed's reaction function, and highlights structural labor-force changes from retiring baby boomers and AI-driven data center demand.

## Key points
- Jackson Hole symposium theme is financial innovation in payments, with focus on atomic settlement where payments and reconciliation become instant, requiring proven liquidity behind each transaction.
- Federal Reserve moves between $5 trillion and $10 trillion daily through multiple payment systems, and the goal is to make payments 'boring' by ensuring resilience and protection.
- Labor force is undergoing structural change as roughly 4 million baby boomers retire per year, creating opportunities but also risking loss of institutional knowledge; Schmid advocates using AI to transfer retiree expertise to younger workers.
- The U.S. economy is growing nicely, and Schmid says he is optimistic about expansion durability, but inflation remains above the 2% target and has surged to mid-3s, which must be addressed.
- Long-end bond yields, such as the 30-year above 5%, reflect supply and demand for credit amid a growing economy and AI/data center buildout, competition between commercial and public credit, and are seen as a signal but not directly controlled by the Fed.
- Schmid sees the yield curve as fairly normalized for an economy growing 2% to 3.5%, with the price of money influenced by dynamic growth in GDP, especially the data center and AI build; he wants to peel back how much of growth is driven by this boom.
- Businesses in the Kansas City district report competing with data center construction for labor, equipment, freight, and commodities like steel and copper, which Schmid believes is important for getting inflation back to 2%.
- Higher longer-term yields can have a behavioral tightening effect on credit decisions, though raising short-term policy rates mainly affects current decisions like inventory purchases rather than long-term factory investments.
- Schmid worries that if the Fed is not perceived as taking inflation seriously, there could be long-term costs; he notes several thoughtful dissents at recent FOMC meetings and supports Chair Warsh's call for debate.
- Dissents occur when a policymaker believes risks between the inflation and employment mandates are weighted differently; Schmid says he transmits the views of the seven states in the Tenth District.
- Chairman Kevin Warsh's leadership is characterized by welcoming debate, a 'good family fight,' and a preference for the Fed being on page B12 rather than A1; Schmid contrasts this with past chairs like Jay Powell and appreciates the openness.
- The Fed's reaction function under Warsh is not yet fully clear, and the task forces reviewing communication and data have not yet engaged in direct dialogue with regional Fed presidents, according to Schmid.
- On r-star, Schmid believes it is normalizing but may be higher than pre-2008 levels, and he currently views interest rates as fairly accommodative.
- Schmid sidesteps the deficit question, saying fiscal policy is the domain of Congress and the executive, and the Fed's role is to react to data to achieve its dual mandate.
