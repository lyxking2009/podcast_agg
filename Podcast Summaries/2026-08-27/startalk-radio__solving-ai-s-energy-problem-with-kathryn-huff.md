# Solving AI’s Energy Problem with Kathryn Huff — StarTalk Radio

## TL;DR
Nuclear engineer Kathryn Huff explains why fission and advanced small modular reactors may be essential to meet surging, 24/7 power demand from AI data centers and industrial electrification. She argues nuclear is one of the safest energy sources by deaths per terawatt-hour and that the U.S. will need at least 200 gigawatts of new nuclear capacity to reach net zero by 2050. The episode covers SMR costs, recycling spent fuel, and private investment from Amazon and Microsoft.

## Key points
- Kathryn Huff is an associate professor of nuclear, plasma, and radiological engineering at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign and served as Assistant Secretary for Nuclear Energy at the U.S. Department of Energy from 2022 to 2024.
- Fission splits heavy atoms like uranium-235 or plutonium-239, while fusion combines light isotopes of hydrogen or helium; the binding energy curve peaks at iron, which is why stars stop producing energy when they reach iron.
- Comparing deaths per terawatt-hour, nuclear is among the safest energy sources—slightly below solar and wind—even including Chernobyl and Fukushima, because its enormous energy output offsets rare accidents; geothermal may be the only source lower.
- The U.S. has about 60 years of spent nuclear fuel from producing close to 20% of its electricity; ignoring packaging, the uranium alone would fill a football field only a few meters high.
- AI data centers, Bitcoin mining, electric vehicles, and industrial processes like carbon-free steelmaking are creating large, always-on power demands; a multi-billion-dollar data center cannot run at 2% capacity when the wind stops blowing.
- DOE's net-zero-by-2050 pathway requires building 550–770 gigawatts of new firm clean power, of which at least about 200 gigawatts must be nuclear.
- Small modular reactors (SMRs) can be co-located with data centers to reduce transmission costs and speed deployment; first-of-a-kind nuclear may cost around $120 per megawatt-hour, falling to about $60 for nth-of-a-kind, comparable to renewables plus storage.
- Recent U.S. large reactor builds—Vogtle Units 3 and 4, Westinghouse AP1000s—were over schedule and over budget, driving industry interest toward smaller designs from Holtec, GE Hitachi, Westinghouse AP300, and NuScale.
- Advanced reactors using sodium, molten salt, or high-temperature gas coolants can reach about 800°C versus 300°C for conventional light-water reactors, enabling direct industrial heat; X-Energy’s high-temperature gas reactor will first deploy at a Dow chemical plant.
- Sodium-cooled fast reactors are passively safe via negative feedback from fuel and coolant expansion, but sodium is corrosive, opaque, and pyrophoric—it can burst into flames when wet.
- Fusion remains far from commercial deployment; NIF uses inertial confinement while ITER in France uses magnetic confinement, but challenges like first-wall protection remain, though fusion inherently lacks long-lived high-level waste.
- Spent nuclear fuel can be recycled by separating fission products and reusing uranium and bred plutonium; France already recycles, but the U.S. lacks infrastructure, though sodium-cooled fast reactors and molten salt reactors are compatible with recycling.
- Recycling could extract valuable isotopes like technetium-99m for medical imaging while reducing waste volume and radioactivity lifetime.
- Uranium mining and enrichment have geopolitical bottlenecks; Russia historically dominated conversion and enrichment, and the U.S. has banned Russian uranium imports while building domestic and allied capacity from Canada, Australia, and Kazakhstan.
- Tech companies are already investing in nuclear: Amazon invested in X-Energy, and Microsoft is funding the restart of Three Mile Island Unit 1, with power purchase agreements driving new reactor orders.
- Sandia National Laboratory experiments show a jet engine rammed into a reactor containment structure leaves it intact, supporting safety claims against terrorist attacks and accidents.
- Final disposal is advancing: Finland is about to start operating a final repository, Canada selected a site, and Sweden received permission to construct one.
- Kathryn Huff’s current research develops multi-physics software that couples neutronics and thermal-hydraulics across scales to model advanced reactors and fuel cycles.
