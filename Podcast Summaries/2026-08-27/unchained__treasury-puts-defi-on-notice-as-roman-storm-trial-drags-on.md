# Treasury Puts DeFi On Notice as Roman Storm Trial Drags On — Unchained

## TL;DR
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's 'economic D-Day' on Iran leaves open whether sanctions enforcement will target decentralized protocols like Uniswap or Ethereum or focus on centralized intermediaries. Guest Peter Van Valkenburgh discusses the GENIUS Act's stablecoin freeze-and-seize rules and the delayed Roman Storm retrial, arguing prosecuting Tornado Cash developers for money transmission and sanctions evasion criminalizes zero-knowledge innovation. The episode also covers the SEC's proposed Reg Crypto exemptions for ICO-like fundraising and Trump's mention of Hyperliquid, raising questions about CFTC jurisdiction over decentralized derivatives venues.

## Key points
- Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced an 'economic D-Day' on Iran, threatening secondary sanctions on any source of regime revenue, including digital asset intermediaries, but the release does not explicitly name DeFi protocols like Uniswap or Ethereum. Peter Van Valkenburgh expects pragmatic targeting of centralized entities like Iranian exchanges and brokers, but notes the broad language doesn't rule out decentralized infrastructure.
- Bessent claimed the U.S. seized or rugged $1 billion in Iranian crypto, but Taylor Monahan says that figure doesn't match known freezes; she points to a 344 million USDT on Tron freeze in April and says no billion-dollar Iranian crypto freeze has been observed, questioning the receipts.
- Under the GENIUS Act rulemakings, stablecoin issuers must be able to freeze and seize tokens on secondary markets, but they are not required to treat secondary holders as customers for full KYC/AML due diligence. Van Valkenburgh supports this balance, arguing full customer treatment would undermine stablecoin utility and privacy.
- Roman Storm's retrial on sanctions and money laundering charges has been delayed from October 2025 to April 27, 2027, while the judge considers his Rule 29 motion for acquittal. The first trial resulted in a guilty verdict on the money transmission count but mistrial on sanctions and money laundering conspiracy counts.
- Van Valkenburgh calls the money transmission theory in Storm's case problematic, citing prosecutors' 'frying pan' analogy: a frying pan doesn't hold heat but transmits it. He contrasts this with clear FinCEN guidance that non-custodial anonymizing software providers are not money transmitters, and notes the Berman Amendment protects transactions in information from sanctions overreach.
- Van Valkenburgh argues Tornado Cash was the first large-scale test of zero-knowledge cryptography on Ethereum and that its immutable design means prosecuting Roman Storm won't stop the protocol; he calls the developers 'heroes' for battle-testing privacy technology and warns of a chilling effect on U.S. crypto innovation.
- The SEC's 'Reg Crypto' proposal includes two exemptions: a one-time offering up to $5 million over four years and a second path allowing up to $75 million in any 12-month period. Peter Van Valkenburgh says these originate from Hester Peirce's safe harbor and are a good hedge, but less permanent than the stalled Clarity Act.
- Trump name-dropped Hyperliquid, a no-KYC perpetuals decentralized exchange, raising questions about how the CFTC would regulate a derivatives venue without customer identification. Van Valkenburgh says the CFTC has broad statutory authority over derivatives but only over commodities derivatives, not spot markets, and lacks a tradition of retail investor oversight.
- On stablecoin secondary market rules, Taylor Monahan says Tether is more aggressive in freezes than Circle, and that chain analysis is not magic; effective sanctions enforcement requires cooperation and human intelligence across issuers, custodians, bridges, and governments, not just freezing decentralized protocols.
