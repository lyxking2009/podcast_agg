# Ethereum Privacy for Institutions | Mo Jalil and Oskar Thoren — Bankless

## TL;DR
Mo Jalil and Oskar Thoren, co-founders of ETH Systems (a for-profit spin-out from the Ethereum Foundation), discuss why institutions need privacy to adopt Ethereum and how they are building confidential infrastructure. They argue that privacy is the missing piece for institutional adoption, and share case studies like inter-dealer compressions and nation-scale private payments that require custom cryptographic solutions. The company positions itself as 'cypherpunks in the boardroom' translating institutional requirements into reusable open-source building blocks and scalable products for compliant DeFi.

## Key points
- ETH Systems is a for-profit spin-out from the Ethereum Foundation, co-founded by Mo Jalil and Oskar Thoren, focused on bringing privacy to Ethereum for institutional users; the team previously ran the EF's institutional privacy taskforce for a year, publishing market maps, write-ups, and proof-of-concepts.
- Privacy is the missing piece for institutional adoption of Ethereum because institutions require both business confidentiality and regulatory compliance; public Ethereum's transparency conflicts with this, but cryptography can enable both.
- The founders have complementary backgrounds: Mo built algorithmic trading systems at Goldman Sachs and later hedge funds before leading APAC business development at the EF; Oskar has a decade in crypto privacy, including work at Status and developing zero-knowledge proof tooling.
- State of privacy solutions: many building blocks exist (zk-proofs, private L2s) but institutional use cases often require customization due to specific business or legal constraints; the team sees the challenge as primarily an engineering problem, not a research one.
- Case study: inter-dealer compression at a Tier 1 investment bank currently relies on a trusted third party costing tens to hundreds of millions per year; using Ethereum's shared state could replace this third party, but requires privacy so trades are not visible to the world.
- Another case: a country needed nation-scale private payments with four actors (sender, receiver, auditor, government) instead of the usual two; existing protocols failed for years on performance or compliance, but EF's open-source POC provided a viable solution and led to the country reaching out to ETH Systems.
- ETH Systems operates with two workstreams: business-driven consulting (architecture reviews, POCs, production builds for institutions) and open-source development of reusable building blocks, technical specifications, and public goods; open source is necessary for security and institutional trust.
- The company chose a for-profit structure because public goods funding is unreliable for multi-year institutional work, and procurement processes often require a for-profit counterparty; they aim to generalize bespoke learnings into scalable, venture-scale products rather than remain a pure consultancy.
- Institutional demand for Ethereum privacy is massive and largely inbound; DeFi protocols like Aave, Uniswap, and Lido show institutions what's possible, but they need compliant versions; regulatory clarity (e.g., Genius Act) is opening floodgates.
- Vision for 2033: privacy becomes invisible infrastructure, with trillions of dollars of assets on-chain, global access to any asset, and the benefits of DeFi (transferability, ownership) while maintaining selective transparency.
- Transparency and privacy are not contradictory: using zero-knowledge proofs and cryptography, systems can disclose aggregate metrics (like volumes, TVL) while hiding individual identities and positions; this addresses concerns that privacy would undermine auditability.
- The team calls themselves 'cypherpunks in the boardroom,' bridging the gap between crypto's cypherpunk ideals and institutional needs; they encourage DeFi protocols to collaborate with them to adapt products for compliant institutional use.
