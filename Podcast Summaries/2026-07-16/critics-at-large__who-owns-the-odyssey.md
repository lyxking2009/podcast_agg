# Who Owns the Odyssey?

**Show:** Critics at Large | The New Yorker  
**Date:** 2026-07-16  
**Duration:** unknown

## Key Points

- The episode centers on Christopher Nolan's 2026 film adaptation of Homer's *Odyssey*, a $250 million IMAX production drawing on Emily Wilson's landmark 2017 English translation
- The hosts trace the epic's nearly 3,000-year evolution: from oral tradition through countless textual revisions to modernized film, arguing the work has always been in flux
- Nolan's adaptation modernizes the dialogue using Wilson's translation and focuses thematically on the erosion of societal values, speaking to contemporary cultural anxieties
- The critics discuss translation choices by Wilson, Robert Fagles, and Daniel Mendelsohn and what each reveals about the era that produced them
- The episode draws comparisons to other classical Hollywood adaptations including *Troy* (2004) and Nolan's own *Oppenheimer* (2023)
- Referenced: David Denby's *New Yorker* essay situating Nolan's film within the history of epic cinema

## Overview

Staff writers Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry, and Alexandra Schwartz convene for the weekly Thursday episode to examine Christopher Nolan's sprawling cinematic take on Homer's *Odyssey*. The conversation moves fluidly between the film itself — its maximalist production scale, its cast, and its formal ambitions — and the broader question of who has the authority to claim ownership of a text so old it predates authorship as we understand it. The hosts consider how the poem's meaning has shifted across translations and adaptations, and what Nolan's particular choices (especially his reliance on Emily Wilson's translation, the first by a woman) say about our cultural moment. The episode positions Nolan's film not as a definitive version but as the latest iteration in an ongoing communal project.

## Implications

The episode raises a genuinely interesting critical question: whether a text that has always been remade can be "owned" by any single adapter or translator. Nolan's choice of Wilson's translation is itself a cultural statement — her scholarly and political approach to the poem foregrounds questions of gender, power, and labor that earlier translations tended to suppress. The film's enormous budget and A-list pedigree ensure wide cultural reach, potentially bringing the *Odyssey* to audiences who would not encounter it through literature. Critics at Large uses the occasion to reflect on how pop-culture adaptations shape collective memory of canonical texts, often more durably than scholarly editions.

## Notable Quotes

> "The Odyssey persists because the world does keep on evanescing behind us. The question of how to move on is always fresh." — Vinson Cunningham
