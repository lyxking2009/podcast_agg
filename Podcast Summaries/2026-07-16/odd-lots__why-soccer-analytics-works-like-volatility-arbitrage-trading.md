# Why Soccer Analytics Works Like Volatility Arbitrage Trading

**Show:** Odd Lots  
**Date:** 2026-07-16  
**Duration:** 51 min

## Key Points

- Soccer analytics has evolved dramatically from simple on-ball event counting (spreadsheet-friendly) to tracking data at 25 frames per second to full skeletal/body-pose data, each layer unlocking richer insights.
- The core intellectual link between volatility arbitrage trading and soccer analytics is the "expected outcome vs. realized outcome" framework — just as options traders price implied volatility against realized moves, soccer analysts price expected goals and expected actions against actual match results.
- Randomness is a first-class citizen in soccer models: referee decisions, irregular game states (e.g., red cards), and low-scoring dynamics mean a team can consistently outperform its expected metrics and still lose matches, requiring analysts to think in probabilities over large samples.
- VAR (Video Assistant Referee) created both noise and opportunity for analytics teams — it altered match states in ways that must be cleaned or modeled separately, as seen in examples like a nine-man Tottenham vs. Chelsea.
- One of the hardest problems is translating neural network outputs into language a coach can act on at halftime — the analyst role is as much communication and translation as it is modeling.
- MLS presents a unique analytics problem because of its salary cap structure, pushing teams toward portfolio-style roster construction rather than buying a single superstar.
- Analytics can democratize soccer by helping smaller clubs identify undervalued players, but wealthy clubs' data infrastructure still confers significant advantages.
- Jude Bellingham is cited as an example of multi-positional excellence that analytics can quantify across both in-possession and out-of-possession metrics, capturing value that traditional scouting undersells.
- Data sourcing is a scrappy, frontier problem — one anecdote involves finding a critical dataset through a developer in Ukraine.
- Teams optimize for different and sometimes conflicting objectives — relegation avoidance, profit, entertainment value, winning trophies — meaning there is no single universal analytics framework.

## Overview

This episode of Odd Lots explores the surprising conceptual overlap between professional soccer analytics and volatility arbitrage trading, featuring guests Yours Becker (soccer analytics consultant) and Mike Tracy (head of risk at Apex Fintech, with a parallel career in soccer analytics). The conversation traces how soccer analytics has matured from skepticism in the early 2000s through the Moneyball era into a sophisticated discipline handling tracking data, skeletal pose models, and real-time probability updates. A central theme is that soccer — despite appearing fluid and continuous — can be decomposed into a series of discrete micro-events, making it tractable for the same probabilistic machinery that quantitative traders apply to financial markets.

The hosts and guests dig into the practical realities: noisy data, VAR disruptions, the challenge of communicating model outputs to coaches, and the structural differences between leagues like MLS (salary cap, portfolio thinking) and European leagues (open market, relegation pressure). The 2026 FIFA World Cup is noted as generating over 90 petabytes of data, signaling how mainstream data collection in soccer has become.

## Implications

For investors and quantitatively minded listeners, the episode reinforces that the "expected value vs. realized value" mental model transfers across domains — the same edge-finding logic that underlies volatility arbitrage applies to identifying undervalued soccer players or misprice match probabilities. The MLS salary cap discussion is particularly instructive as an analogy for portfolio construction under constraints, relevant to any capital-limited allocator. The broader takeaway is that as tracking and skeletal data become commoditized, the sustainable edge will shift from data access toward model interpretation, communication, and the ability to act on insights in real time — a dynamic that mirrors the compression of alpha in quantitative finance.

## Notable Quotes

- "The point of soccer analytics, at the most basic level, is to avoid catastrophic mistakes — bad player investments and relegation." — Yours Becker
- "Soccer is continuous on the surface, but underneath it's a series of micro-decisions and micro-events. Once you see it that way, the math becomes tractable." — Yours Becker
- "In volatility trading you're constantly asking: what's the market implying versus what do I think will actually happen? Soccer analytics is exactly the same — expected goals versus realized goals." — Mike Tracy
- "The hardest part isn't building the model. It's walking into a locker room at halftime and translating a neural network into three sentences a manager can act on." — Yours Becker
- "MLS is interesting because the salary cap forces you to think like a portfolio manager. You can't just buy the best player; you have to build the best team within a budget." — Mike Tracy
- "If you actually had a good predictive model for soccer outcomes, you'd consistently beat the bookmakers. The fact that almost no one does tells you something about the difficulty." — Mike Tracy
- "Jude Bellingham is the rare player where the analytics and the eye test completely agree — he's creating value in ways that only show up when you measure both sides of the ball." — Yours Becker
