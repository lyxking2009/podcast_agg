# Nobel Prize Winner: AI’s Impact On Drug Discovery And Biology | Venki Ramakrishnan — The Peterman Pod

## TL;DR
Ryan Peterman flies to Cambridge to interview Venki Ramakrishnan - Nobel laureate, ribosome structural biologist and scientific advisory board member at Demis Hassabis's Isomorphic Labs - about what AI actually changes in drug discovery. Ramakrishnan's answer is disciplined: AI is real progress at the front of the funnel, and the experimental and clinical work downstream is still the bottleneck.

## Key points
- Ramakrishnan starts from what worked: AI predicting protein folding has been genuinely successful, though not perfect - and he notes experimental work is itself error-prone, so the bar for 'perfect' is lower than people assume.
- The drug-discovery workflow he describes: identify a target protein, find a critical pocket, then search for molecules that block it. AI is good at proposing candidate molecules and at the initial filter; it cannot yet reliably predict toxicity across the board.
- Molecules also have to be synthesizable and testable. 'The experimental part is always going to be the bottleneck' - getting assays done, accumulating enough data for IND-enabling work, then running trials.
- Many drugs fail in clinical trials despite promising animal data; monoclonal antibodies - highly specific molecules - have still put volunteers into critical care in Phase 1. So AI's front-end gains do not de-risk the back end.
- His verdict on timing: the field is optimistic, but you have to wait a few years for clinical proof that AI actually accelerated programs rather than just generating more candidates.
- On biology vs physics: he calls himself a 'failed physicist' who moved fields because physics felt incremental, pivoted into molecular biology without a biology PhD, and worked on the ribosome when the field had stalled for 15 years.
- He discusses the politics of the Nobel - how credit is allocated when a field has many contributors, and what it is like to win for work that others had chased for decades.
- On AI and science more broadly: the more complex the biology, the less impact AI has had so far - which is the opposite of the narrative that AI has already transformed biology.
