## Key Points
- The distinction between public and private markets hasn't legally disappeared — the regulatory rules haven't changed — but product issuers are aggressively packaging private assets into retail-accessible wrappers like ETFs, interval funds, tender offer funds, and non-traded closed-end funds
- Since 2010, private credit has raised approximately $1.8 trillion; every major alternative firm (Blackstone, Apollo, KKR, Ares, Blue Owl) is building retail distribution channels, with 314 interval/tender offer funds holding $277 billion in assets as of January 2026
- Retail investors historically serve as the exit liquidity for institutional "smart money" at the end of every market cycle — the current push to "democratize" private investing fits that pattern
- Closed-end funds (CEFs) frequently trade at a significant discount to NAV (e.g., Pershing Square's PSUs at a ~20% discount) because there is no arbitrage mechanism to close the gap, unlike ETFs which have in-kind creation/redemption
- Products like USVC (AngelList) and USPE (TAP) target retail with low minimum investments ($500) and promise exposure to pre-IPO companies like SpaceX, but charge fees that can stack to 3-4% annually due to fees-on-fees structures
- Dave Nadig advocates for two regulatory reforms: strictly enforcing the 15% illiquid asset limit in funds marketed as liquid, and requiring independent valuation agents for private assets held in registered vehicles
- Cliff Asness's phrase "volatility laundering" captures how private assets marked quarterly appear artificially stable compared to their true underlying volatility
- Nadig predicts high-profile blow-ups in private equity or private credit funds within the next few years, which he believes will be healthy in waking up investors to the risks — though the most dangerous scenario (private assets flooding 401(k)s) has not yet materialized

## Overview
In this "At the Money" segment, Barry Ritholtz speaks with Dave Nadig, president and director of research at ETF.com, about the blurring boundary between public and private investments. Nadig explains the structural mechanics of closed-end funds, interval funds, and tender offer funds — the vehicles being used to bring private equity, private credit, and pre-IPO stakes to retail investors. He is cautiously skeptical: while not dismissing private investing outright, he argues that median private fund returns don't justify the illiquidity and fees, that retail is typically the exit liquidity in late-cycle markets, and that current valuation and transparency practices are inadequate for products touching the public hand.

## Implications
The rapid packaging of private assets into retail wrappers represents a structural risk to retail investors who may not understand that "liquid" alternatives can gate redemptions, mark assets at management discretion, and charge layered fees that compound the performance hurdle. Regulators who have not yet updated enforcement to match product innovation leave investors relying on good faith from managers with obvious conflicts of interest. The potential inclusion of these products in 401(k) plans — not yet happening at scale — would represent a significant escalation of systemic risk, and even without that, the current environment echoes prior late-cycle moments where retail absorbed institutional exit positions.

## Notable Quotes
> "At the end of every cycle in my career, it is retail that is looked at to be the exit. Whether that's buying beanie babies, used cars, or stocks, it doesn't really matter."

> "It's not surprising to me that we're seeing a lot of discussion around quote unquote democratizing private investing. Whether it's venture capital or private credit, doesn't really matter, it's all the same thing."

> "These are funds that are roach motels. Money goes in, money never comes out."

> "Cliff Asness calls that volatility laundering, and it's exactly the perfect phrase. You're taking what would obviously be wildly volatile assets and you're marking them once a quarter."

> "The median fund does okay, doesn't do great. You're better off in the S&P. It's expensive, and it's illiquid versus the S&P. But a top decile fund does really well, diversified, non-correlated and very often outperforms the index."

> "Either it's private and illiquid or public and liquid, but private and liquid doesn't really make sense — at that point it might as well be public."

> "I feel fairly confident suggesting we're going to have some event in the next couple of years which is going to pull the scales off our eyes."
