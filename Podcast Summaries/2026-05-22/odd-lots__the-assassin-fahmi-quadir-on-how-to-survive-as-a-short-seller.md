## Key Points
- Fahmi Quadir, founder and CIO of Safkhet Capital, runs an exclusively short-biased fund and earned the nickname "The Assassin" as an anonymous identity used to sign research before she became publicly known
- Short selling is in an existential crisis: years of underperformance due to persistent bull markets, the 2021 meme-stock short squeeze risks, and institutional allocators shifting to market-neutral strategies have made it nearly impossible to raise capital for short-only funds
- Quadir is pivoting to launch a long-focused shareholder activism fund in Korea, her first-ever long strategy, driven by Korea's 2024 corporate governance reforms that for the first time enshrined fiduciary duty to shareholders in the corporate code
- The current environment is described as a "Golden Age of Fraud" — targets are abundant, but traditional levers (whistleblowing to regulators, building conviction positions over time) are broken: SEC enforcement dropped ~26% from 2023 to 2024, then another ~22% the following year
- Consumer stress is a key short theme: household debt servicing is at 11% of income (higher for student-loan-burdened households), with a bifurcation between an older economically stable generation and a younger, more indebted one
- Levered private equity roll-ups in consumer discretionary and healthcare services are identified as favored short targets; healthcare is a long-term theme tied to reimbursement and cost-of-care populism
- On Wirecard: Quadir's research focused on money laundering rather than accounting fraud, ultimately tracking the arrest of Jan Marsalek's closest US associate in the Southern District of New York as the key signal of the company's imminent collapse
- Korea's "chaebol" conglomerates are undergoing a generational transition; new third-generation owners are more capital-aware and globally oriented, and financial regulators now shame companies trading below book value — creating a rich target set for governance-focused activism

## Overview
Recorded live at an Odd Lots event in London, this episode features Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal in conversation with Fahmi Quadir about the state of short selling, the challenges of surviving in a relentlessly bullish market, and her surprising pivot to long-focused activism in Korea. Quadir describes the structural erosion of short-seller infrastructure — from weakened regulatory enforcement to the chilling effect of meme-stock volatility — while arguing that short sellers remain one of the last remaining arbiters of market-based corporate governance. She walks through the Wirecard investigation methodology and explains why Korea's combination of governance reform, generational ownership transition, and undervalued holding companies makes it an exceptionally compelling new frontier.

## Implications
The near-extinction of conviction short-sellers has real market integrity consequences: without the informational alpha that dedicated shorts provide, price discovery suffers and frauds persist longer. Quadir's pivot to Korea is a meaningful signal that governance-focused activism can generate alpha in emerging markets undergoing regulatory modernization, and Korea's model — naming and shaming below-book companies, enshrining shareholder fiduciary duty — may be a template others can follow. The parallel she draws between today's AI-era circular capital flows and the opaque utility holding companies of the 1920s (which ultimately collapsed spectacularly) is a sobering frame for evaluating current private-market valuations.
