## Key Points

- **SpaceX trades at ~110x sales and ~589x EBITDA** at the ~$2.5T valuation (~$137/share at IPO), implying ~37% annual revenue growth through 2031 — a threshold the hosts view as largely priced for perfection.
- **Kyle's DCF model pegs fair value at ~$110/share**, roughly 10% below IPO price; applying a 40% margin of safety implies an annualized return of approximately –18%. Neither host adds SPCX to their Intrinsic Value Portfolio.
- **SpaceX operates three distinct segments**: Space (launch services), Connectivity (Starlink), and AI (xAI/Grok/X, merged into SpaceX in February 2026). Each segment has a radically different financial profile.
- **The Space segment's core moat is cost**: reusable Falcon 9 launches cost ~$74M vs. NASA's ~$2.5B for comparable missions — over 30x cheaper. A 99% success rate across 650+ launches validates the technology. Starship targets 100-ton payload capacity at ~$100/kg, vs. Falcon 9's ~$2,000/kg.
- **Starlink is the cash engine**: 10.3M subscribers across 164 countries, ~40% operating margins, ~9,600 operational satellites (~75% of all active satellites globally). ARPU is intentionally declining ($88 → $66) to grow the subscriber base. V3 satellites promise ~20x throughput improvement.
- **The AI segment is a significant drag**: 2025 operating losses of ~$6.3B on ~$3.2B revenue, with ~$12.7B capex (~4x annual revenue). xAI/Grok is underperforming OpenAI and Anthropic with no clear path to profitability.
- **Hosts peg the realistic TAM at ~$600B**, far below SpaceX's claimed $28.5T addressable market. The spread reflects disagreement on how much of satellite internet, launch services, and AI markets SpaceX can realistically capture.
- **Key-person risk is binary**: Musk's simultaneous commitments to Tesla, X, xAI, Neuralink, and DOGE create concentration risk that the hosts identify as a primary concern.
- **IPO raised $85.7B** with pro forma cash of $101.5B and total debt of $30B. 2025 adjusted EBITDA was $6.5B with $1.9B in interest expense.
- The hosts acknowledge SpaceX's extraordinary track record but argue that **great companies can still be bad investments at the wrong price** — drawing the parallel to situations where execution was flawless but valuation left no margin of safety.

## Overview

Kyle Grieve and Shawn O'Malley conduct a segment-by-segment fundamental analysis of SpaceX's IPO, examining whether the ~$2 trillion valuation is justified. The episode traces SpaceX's origin story — Elon Musk attending a Mars Society convention in 2002, early near-bankruptcy after three failed Falcon 1 launches, and eventual breakthrough as the first private company to send a liquid-fueled rocket to orbit in 2008 — before diving into the current business structure.

The three-segment framework is central to the analysis. The **Space segment** (launch services) is the historical core: 650+ launches, ~99% success rate, and a reusable rocket model that has compressed launch economics by 30x. The planned Starship vehicle, targeting 100-ton payload at ~$100/kg, could further transform the economics of orbit if it reaches commercial readiness. The **Connectivity segment** (Starlink) is the financial engine today: 10.3M subscribers, ~40% operating margins, and growing faster than any prior satellite internet deployment. The **AI segment** (xAI/Grok, merged in Feb 2026) is the wildcard: burning ~$6.3B in operating losses annually against a backdrop where OpenAI and Anthropic remain well ahead on capability benchmarks.

The core valuation tension: at ~$2.5T market cap (~$137/share), SpaceX trades at multiples (110x sales, 589x EBITDA) that require sustained ~37% annual revenue growth through 2031. Kyle's base-case model, using 37% revenue CAGR with EBITDA margins rising to ~26% and a 30x exit multiple, yields a fair value of ~$110/share — roughly 10% below IPO price. Applying a standard 40% margin of safety makes the implied return significantly negative on an annualized basis.

The hosts conclude by declining to add SPCX to their Intrinsic Value Portfolio, citing: valuation disconnected from fundamentals, the AI segment burning cash without clear competitive advantage, key-person concentration risk, regulatory exposure, and the fundamental question of whether Starship milestones can be hit on schedule.

## Implications

- **The xAI merger is a significant risk transfer**: Starlink's ~40% operating margins are currently subsidizing ~$6.3B in annual AI-segment losses. If Grok does not improve its competitive position, Starlink profitability may be structurally constrained for years.
- **Starship is the key optionality bet**: At $100/kg to orbit (vs. $2,000/kg today), the addressable launch market expands dramatically — but this optionality is already priced into current valuation multiples.
- **Satellite internet competition is emerging**: While Starlink holds a commanding position (~75% of active satellites), Amazon Kuiper and others are scaling. ARPU compression from $88 to $66 suggests pricing power may be limited.
- **Musk's regulatory leverage is double-edged**: SpaceX benefits from favorable US government relationships, but any political shift (or Musk-related controversy) could expose the company to adverse FAA licensing, contract cancellations, or international market restrictions.
- **The bull case is real but priced in**: SpaceX's technology lead, launch cadence, and Starlink subscriber base are genuine competitive advantages — the debate is purely about whether $2.5T reflects these advantages or extrapolates them too aggressively.
- For long-term investors, the hosts suggest waiting for a material price correction before sizing any position.
