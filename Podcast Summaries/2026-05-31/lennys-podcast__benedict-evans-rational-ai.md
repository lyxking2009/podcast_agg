## Key Points
- We are in "1997" for AI: early, exciting, and deeply uncertain — most applications that will matter have not been built yet, and most things still do not work reliably
- The right question about jobs is not "what percentage of tasks can AI do?" but "is this a task or a job?" — sometimes the task is the entire job (elevator attendant), but often automating the task just unlocks more demand for the surrounding work
- Price elasticity has historically meant that making something cheaper leads to more of it, not less employment — the number of accountants kept rising through every wave of automation (adding machines, spreadsheets, ERP, cloud)
- Foundation model companies likely lack pricing power long-term because the models appear to be commodities without strong network effects, making them resemble cloud infrastructure (AWS) rather than platform monopolies (Windows)
- Distribution is becoming the dominant competitive moat as software gets easier to build — Google, Meta, and Apple are all leveraging existing distribution to push AI, much as Microsoft used browser distribution in the late 1990s
- The unexpected boom in professional services and forward-deployed engineers at AI labs mirrors classic consulting dynamics: companies need outside help to figure out which workflows to reimagine before they can automate anything
- Adoption speed is higher than previous platform shifts (internet, mobile) because the infrastructure already exists — no need to wait for hardware rollout or network deployment — but organizational change still takes 3–10 years
- The anti-AI backlash is a broad, fuzzy mix: some concerns are real (deepfakes, job dislocation), some are exaggerated (data center water use at 0.017% of US consumption), and many mirror the earlier social media backlash pattern
- The best career advice in an uncertain AI transition: don't avoid the technology out of moral opposition — immerse yourself, understand what you can do with it today, and demonstrate that fluency when hiring
- AGI and superintelligence definitions keep shifting ("AI is whatever machines can't do yet"), making those debates less useful than focusing on what this technology does today and how to deploy it

## Overview
Benedict Evans, independent tech analyst and former Andreessen Horowitz partner, joins Lenny Rachitsky to give a grounded, historically informed view of where AI is actually heading. Evans frames the current moment as "1997 for AI" — a period of genuine excitement and enormous potential where most of the important applications have not been built yet and most things still do not work reliably. Drawing on his latest 80-slide presentation "AI is Eating the World," he walks through three interlocking questions: where all the capital is going and whether model labs will hold pricing power, what AI means for the software industry, and how it will reshape work and employment more broadly.

Evans consistently applies lessons from previous platform shifts — spreadsheets for accountants, the internet for newspapers, mobile for social networks — to argue that outcomes are almost always more complicated than either utopian or dystopian predictions suggest. He challenges the framing that AI will simply eliminate jobs, pointing to price elasticity, the "task versus job" distinction, and decades of evidence that automation tends to increase employment in affected fields rather than reduce it. At the same time, he takes seriously the uncertainty for people entering the job market now, particularly in professional services, and recommends deep engagement with the technology rather than avoidance.

## Implications
The most practically significant takeaway is Evans's thesis that value in the AI stack will accumulate further up than the model layer — in distribution, applications, and the organizations that successfully redesign their workflows — rather than at the foundation model companies themselves, which face commodity pricing dynamics similar to mobile telecoms or cloud infrastructure. For product builders and investors, this means the "1997 internet" window for defining durable AI businesses is open now but requires moving past "doing the old thing with AI" toward genuinely new categories that were not possible before. For workers and managers, the implication is that the hard part of most professional jobs — judgment, relationships, knowing what question to ask — is not what AI automates; the risk is failing to adapt fast enough to wield AI fluency as a differentiator in a tightening job market.
