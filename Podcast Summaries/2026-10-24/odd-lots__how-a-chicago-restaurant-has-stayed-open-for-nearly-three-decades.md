# How a Chicago Restaurant Has Stayed Open For Nearly Three Decades — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Richard Mott, owner of Chicago's North Pond restaurant and the Spark Shared Kitchen, explains why independent fine dining is one of the worst businesses economically—hyper-competitive, no barriers to entry, law-abiding operators face a 5-10% cost disadvantage, and many competitors are in it for non-economic reasons. He details how his 30-year-old restaurant has navigated shifting tastes, with wine sales falling from 33% to 24% of revenue in the last 18 months, forcing price increases and a focus on special-event dining. Mott also discusses his shared kitchen venture, which lowers startup costs for food entrepreneurs by pooling expensive kitchen infrastructure and licensing, and notes that business schools now emphasize entrepreneurship over traditional corporate pipelines.

## Key points
- Mott started as a Booth finance major but left the corporate path after an internship at Progressive Insurance, deciding to work for himself. He bid on a school coffee shop project, turned it into a sandwich/lunch operation, expanded to 20 locations across five universities, but eventually the comprehensive contract model favored large firms like Aramark and Compass, squeezing out niche operators.
- Business schools have transformed: when Mott attended Booth, there was no entrepreneurial studies; now entrepreneurship is the largest concentration. He notes that he was derided by classmates for starting a business, but top schools now have entrepreneurship centers and support search funds and entrepreneurship through acquisition.
- North Pond's farm-to-table focus began in the late 1990s before the term existed, inspired by Alice Waters' restaurant. The early menu listed farmers like Mickey Klug, and the restaurant built a network of about 100 small local producers, helping create the supply chain for sustainable dining in Chicago.
- Restaurant economics are brutal: independent fine dining has a negative industry-wide return; hyper-competition, no barriers to entry, no proprietary moat, and law-abiding operators operate at 5-10% cost disadvantage due to widespread wage theft and labor abuse among competitors. Many entrants are non-economic buyers (e.g., wealthy individuals) making competition unfair.
- Alcohol sales, especially wine, are critical to profitability because food has high labor costs while wine has minimal service cost. At North Pond, wine/alcohol was 33-34% of sales for 28 years, but dropped to 24% in the last 18 months, a significant hit because that was the most profitable revenue, driven by generational shifts toward less drinking, health trends, and alternatives like cannabis/CBD.
- Inflation and labor scarcity have hit restaurants hard: starting cooks' wages are up 30% over 3-4 years, food costs are among the highest inflation sectors, and because restaurants buy ingredients weekly, there is no lag in cost pass-through. Mott raised prices four times in two years; a dinner for two with wine pairing now costs about $400, shifting customer mix toward special occasions.
- The shared kitchen model at Spark Shared Kitchen differs from ghost kitchens: it's in an industrial area (Brighton Park) not suitable for delivery density, and it offers hourly rentals and dedicated 400-1600 sq ft kitchens. This lowers startup costs and time—entrepreneurs can get a shared kitchen user license on Monday and start business Tuesday, avoiding six months of lease/licensing/buildout.
- Shared kitchens pool expensive capital equipment, e.g., a $26,000 industrial ice cream machine for multiple small producers, allowing each to produce far more than with a small machine, boosting efficiency and enabling small food businesses to scale without huge upfront investment.
- Chicago's city licensing has improved from 'department of no' under earlier administrations to a more supportive B-minus under Mayor Rahm Emanuel and current officials, though still not efficient, but the tone is now to help businesses open rather than block them.
- The restaurant industry has seen a reckoning on harassment and labor law abuse, partly spurred by Me Too; women in the industry have widespread horror stories, and high-profile chefs are being held accountable, though abuse is not eliminated.
