# HIGHLIGHTS: Fabricio Bloisi - CEO of Prosus

## Key Points
- Prosus operates a portfolio of consumer tech brands reaching approximately 1.5 billion customers, including iFood (Brazil), Swiggy (India), Just Eat (Europe), and PayU (fintech across emerging markets).
- Bloisi acquired iFood for roughly $5 million in 2013 when it processed 20,000 orders per month; the platform now handles 200 million orders per month, illustrating the compounding power of the ecosystem model.
- The 'jet ski' innovation model empowers small teams of five to ten people to run 10–20 rapid experiments as if they were business owners, contrasting with slow, budget-heavy corporate innovation cycles.
- Bloisi argues AI is 'underhyped,' citing advances in the months between December 2025 and May 2026 that exceeded what most had expected for the entire year, particularly in agentic and robotics capabilities.
- Prosus has deployed close to 20,000 AI agents internally, with the acquisition of Advolve enabling AI-driven marketing that reduced cost per user acquisition by approximately 25%.
- The company is building a proprietary 'Large Commerce Model' trained on billions of real transactions from two billion customers, designed to predict behavior and cross-sell services across food, travel, events, and payments in a unified system.
- Bloisi's 'ambidextrous organization' philosophy holds that operational discipline and disruptive innovation must coexist — companies that are only disciplined will eventually be disrupted by startups.
- In Latin America, 5% of Despegar (Decolar) net revenues now come via iFood cross-selling after less than six months of integration, demonstrating early ecosystem cross-sell success.
- Prosus projects revenues of at least $7.3 billion and adjusted EBITDA of at least $1.1 billion for fiscal year 2026, with a target to double revenue by FY2028 and reach billions in annual profitability.
- Bloisi offered to invest $10 billion to build European tech but was instead required by regulators to divest his Delivery Hero stake, which he argues undermines Europe's ambition to create global tech leaders.
- Bloisi trains all Prosus executives in AI programming within four-hour workshops and uses biweekly senior management meetings to ensure breakthrough innovations spread across all 40,000 employees within two weeks.
- His long-term vision is for Prosus to become a half-trillion-dollar company within ten years, positioned as a global leader in AI-powered lifestyle ecosystems originating outside the US and China.

## Overview
Nicolai Tangen sits down with Fabricio Bloisi, CEO of Prosus, to trace a journey from a small startup with two co-founders to leading one of the world's most ambitious consumer tech ecosystems, reaching roughly 1.5 billion customers across Latin America, India, and Europe. The company's brands include iFood (food delivery in Brazil), Swiggy (India), Just Eat (Europe), and PayU (emerging-markets fintech). Bloisi describes years of early failure before finding the cultural and operational principles that enabled explosive growth — most strikingly illustrated by iFood, which Prosus acquired for roughly $5 million in 2013 when it was processing 20,000 orders per month and now handles 200 million orders per month.

The conversation centers on Bloisi's distinctive innovation philosophy, which he calls the "jet ski" model: empowering small teams of five to ten people to experiment as if they were business owners, running 10 to 20 iterations rapidly before scaling what works. He contrasts this with the traditional corporate model that allocates large budgets over long timelines and often delivers little. Bloisi also introduces the concept of the "ambidextrous organization," arguing that disciplined execution and disruptive innovation must coexist simultaneously — companies that rely only on discipline will eventually have their lunch taken by startups.

AI runs throughout as a major strategic theme. Bloisi argues that AI is "underhyped," pointing to recent advances in agentic capabilities that exceeded even optimistic projections. Prosus has deployed close to 20,000 AI agents internally, acquired AI marketing firm Advolve (achieving 25% lower cost per user acquisition), and is training all executives in AI programming. The company is also building what Bloisi calls a "Large Commerce Model," a proprietary model trained on billions of customer transactions designed to predict behavior and personalize services across food, travel, events, and payments.

Bloisi closes with a pointed critique of European regulators, recounting that he offered to invest $10 billion to build European tech but was instead forced to divest his Delivery Hero stake — a decision he views as counterproductive to Europe's stated goal of producing globally competitive technology companies. He frames Prosus's long-term mission as proving that transformative tech companies can be built outside Silicon Valley, projecting the company will reach half-a-trillion dollars in value within a decade.

## Implications
Bloisi's account of Prosus's trajectory carries significant implications for the debate over whether world-class technology companies can be built outside Silicon Valley. His insistence that AI is "underhyped" — and the fact that Prosus has already deployed ~20,000 internal agents — suggests that consumer-facing technology platforms in emerging and developed markets may reach productivity inflection points faster than conventional analyst models assume. The "Large Commerce Model" concept, trained on billions of transactions across food, payments, travel, and grocery, positions Prosus as a potential challenger to the data moats that Big Tech incumbents have accumulated, particularly in markets where Google and Amazon have weaker footholds.

The European regulatory episode is also telling for policymakers and investors. Bloisi's account of being forced to divest Delivery Hero while simultaneously being asked to help make Europe more technologically competitive captures a genuine tension in European competition policy: rules designed to prevent monopolistic concentration may be inadvertently blocking the formation of scaled ecosystems capable of competing with US and Chinese platforms. For investors in European tech and for LPs in global tech funds, Prosus's aggressive acquisition of Just Eat Takeaway and La Centrale — and the iFood playbook being applied to those assets — is a significant real-world test of whether emerging-market execution culture can regenerate growth in mature, slow-growth European markets.
