# A Different Way to Lower Your Grocery Bill

## Key Points
- A CNN poll found that 61% of Americans have changed what they buy at the grocery store due to rising food prices, reflecting widespread affordability strain.
- Azalea Fresh Market in Atlanta is a public-private partnership grocery store that opened in a former Walgreens location in downtown Atlanta, serving Georgia State University students and a food desert community.
- The store charges significantly below-market prices: a dozen eggs for $1.69, chicken breast for $4.99/lb, and half-gallon milk for $2.85, and guarantees prices with a 'price promise' policy.
- About 40% of purchases at Azalea Market are made using SNAP/EBT (food stamps), an increase since opening that tracks with rising financial hardship among shoppers.
- The store's financing model involves a small city grant plus loans the store is required to repay, with the city acting as a low-interest bank — CEO Paul Naire explicitly calls this 'capitalism at best, not socialism.'
- Mayor Adams of New York City has proposed opening one or more city-assisted grocery stores in New York, and contacted Azalea Market to learn from their model; Azalea's team advised against having city employees run the store directly.
- AI filmmaker Matt Zion argues that AI filmmaking is a 'totally new category' — a distinct medium rather than a replacement for traditional film production — and has found that audiences don't mind AI if the story is compelling.
- Zion uses a technique of 'reverse-prompting' Claude AI, pretending to be a hater writing complaints, so that Claude builds a genuine critical case against AI art rather than sycophantically agreeing with the creator.
- Zion says he knows of no production that previously used human workers and switched to AI, pushing back on fears of immediate job displacement in Hollywood.
- The Enhanced Games, a steroid-permitted sporting event backed by Silicon Valley investors, was largely a bust — non-enhanced athletes won key events, one world record in swimming is disputed due to a speed suit, and the company's stock has fallen 70% from its high.
- A comedian's AI-generated travel song about Puerto Rico (made with Suno) went viral and may become an official promotional song for the island, sparking a debate between hosts about whether AI-assisted music can be genuinely good art.
- Street interviews in Chicago revealed that ordinary Americans are meal-stretching, shopping by sale, switching to store brands, and some are going into debt just to cover grocery costs.

## Overview
This episode of Everybody's Business tackles the ongoing affordability crisis through three distinct segments. The main segment, which gives the episode its title, examines the emergence of government-assisted or city-supported grocery stores as a solution to food deserts and rising food costs. Hosts Stacy Vanick Smith and Max Schaffkin speak with the general manager and CEO of Azalea Fresh Market in Atlanta, Georgia — a public-private partnership that opened in a former Walgreens location in downtown Atlanta and aims to provide affordable fresh groceries to students at Georgia State University and residents in a food desert area.

The Atlanta store's model is presented as a preview of what New York City's Mayor Adams is proposing: a city-assisted grocery store to open in New York. The show pushes back on critics calling this "socialism," with the Azalea Market CEO Paul Naire making clear the store took a small grant plus loans that must be paid back, with the city acting more like a low-interest bank than a government handout operation. The store prices are strikingly low — a dozen eggs for $1.69, chicken breast for $4.99/lb, half-gallon milk for $2.85 — and about 40% of purchases are made with SNAP/EBT benefits. Street interviews from Chicago illustrate that families are deeply stressed, budgeting tightly, switching to store brands, and in some cases going into debt just to afford groceries.

The episode's second segment features a conversation between host Max Schaffkin and AI filmmaker Matt Zion, recorded at the Artist and Machine Conference in Brooklyn. Zion, a documentary producer for over a decade, has pivoted to using AI tools like Runway and MidJourney to create short films, including a World War II newsreel parody and a twelve-minute sci-fi short called "DJ." The conversation explores whether AI filmmaking constitutes art or "slop," how Zion uses Claude to reverse-engineer hater feedback to better understand his critics, and whether AI filmmaking actually displaces human workers. Zion argues AI filmmaking is an entirely new medium rather than a replacement for traditional production.

The episode closes with brief segments on a viral AI-generated travel song about Puerto Rico (made with the Suno music tool and debated between the hosts), and an update on the Enhanced Games — a steroid-permitted sporting event — which turned out to be a largely underwhelming debut, with non-enhanced athletes winning key events and the company's stock down 70% from its peak.

## Implications
The episode illustrates that the grocery affordability crisis is moving from a political talking point to a catalyst for structural innovation. City-assisted grocery stores, once dismissed as socialist overreach, are gaining traction as a pragmatic, market-compatible response to food deserts — with Atlanta's Azalea Fresh Market serving as a proof of concept that can be replicated in cities like New York. The show highlights that the key design choice — using private operators with city-backed financing rather than municipal employees — may be the difference between a functional community resource and a bureaucratic failure. If this model proves scalable and fiscally sustainable, it could represent a new template for urban food policy.

The AI filmmaking segment speaks to a broader tension in creative industries: the tools are advancing faster than the cultural consensus about what they mean for art, labor, and authenticity. Matt Zion's framing of AI as a "new medium" rather than a replacement challenges the dominant narrative of displacement, but his own work — where he is the sole human creative — does embody a real shift in how content can be made with minimal crew. The episode captures a moment where AI's effects on both consumer markets (grocery prices, viral content) and creative industries are real but still being negotiated in real time.
