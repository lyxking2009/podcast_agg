# Frank Wright - Reality Is Radicalising People

## Key Points
- Frank Wright's video recorded in Makerfield while campaigning for Restore Britain went viral after Elon Musk shared it on May 24, 2026, accumulating over 40 million views — Wright's core claim was that reality, not extremism, is the force radicalising ordinary British people.
- Wright argues liberal democracy has transformed from a system of genuine self-governance into a machinery of manufactured consent, drawing on Walter Lippmann's concept of a 'pseudo-environment' and Edward Bernays's propaganda techniques, rendering elections incapable of producing real change.
- Restore Britain, led by former Reform UK MP Rupert Lowe, launched as a pressure group on June 30, 2025, registered as a political party in February 2026, and claimed approximately 97,000–123,000 members — Wright describes it as the most serious vehicle for a 'politics of the common good' currently available in Britain.
- Wright traces Britain's mass migration crisis to Western regime-change wars in the Middle East, arguing these wars directly drove displacement patterns that destabilised European societies, and that the same political class responsible for the wars then profited from managing the migration crisis.
- On liberal democracy's structural failure, Wright contends that 'the economic system is prior to the political system; the political system creates the crises; the economic system monetises them' — meaning systemic crises are deliberately generated and then financialised.
- Wright situates his political philosophy in Catholic Social Teaching and Pope Leo XIII's social doctrine, arguing for a politics grounded in human dignity, stable community, and national identity as an alternative to what he calls the 'liberal death machine' of consumerist nihilism.
- The episode covers surrogacy as a case study in the commodification of human life — Wright treats it as emblematic of a system that prices and sells biological reality itself, consistent with his broader critique of liberal markets reducing persons to economic units.
- Wright is openly sceptical that mainstream conservatism can deliver real change, describing it as 'profitable grievance marketing' that functions as controlled opposition within the same liberal system rather than a genuine alternative to it.
- On the question of who runs Britain, Wright argues that formal democratic structures have been hollowed out and that actual governance reflects the interests of a transnational financial and administrative class rather than elected representatives or the voting public.
- McCormack presses Wright on whether Restore Britain can build a tent wide enough to govern — Wright acknowledges that broadening the coalition without diluting the message is the central strategic challenge, and discusses the Makerfield campaigning as evidence of working-class receptivity.
- Wright discusses the possibility of standing for Parliament himself, suggesting his role may evolve from intellectual commentator to direct political actor as the Restore movement grows.
- On debt and inflation, Wright argues that the current economic structure deliberately impoverishes the middle class through monetary expansion, connecting this to a broader system of dependency creation designed to maintain political control.

## Overview
Frank Wright, a writer and LifeSiteNews journalist who recently went viral after Elon Musk shared his video on X — garnering over 40 million views — joins Peter McCormack for an extended 2+ hour conversation about Britain's systemic decline. The viral clip, recorded while Wright was campaigning with Restore Britain in Makerfield near Manchester, featured Wright stating plainly that Britain had become morally and financially bankrupt, that normal life had been replaced by a permanent state of emergency, and that reality itself — not extremist rhetoric — was the force driving ordinary people toward radical political conclusions. McCormack probes Wright on the intellectual and personal roots of these views, the viability of the Restore Britain movement, and what a genuine national restoration would look like in practice.

Wright's central argument is that liberal democracy has ceased to function as advertised. Drawing on political theorists from Carl Schmitt to Walter Lippmann and Edward Bernays, he contends that Western governments have operated through manufactured consent and media-managed pseudo-environments since at least World War I, and that this system of control is now collapsing under the weight of its own contradictions. He traces today's crises — mass migration, institutional corruption, economic dysfunction, cultural decay — to a unified "liberal death machine" that treats citizens as replaceable consumer units rather than as members of a meaningful national community. The conversation covers why mainstream conservatism has failed as a force for real change, how Restore Britain differs from Reform UK, and what it would take to build a political coalition large enough to actually govern.

The episode's second half ranges across topics including surrogacy and the commodification of human life, the question of who actually runs Britain, working-class dignity, the economics of debt and inflation, and whether Frank Wright himself might seek a parliamentary seat. Throughout, McCormack plays a genuinely probing interlocutor, pushing back on Wright's framing, asking how his vision avoids ending in conflict, and pressing him to define what restoration actually means in concrete policy terms. Wright points to Rupert Lowe's Restore Britain movement — which had reportedly signed up 97,000 members within weeks of launching and registered as a formal party in February 2026 — as the vehicle through which a "politics of the common good" could be achieved.

The episode aired the same day it was recorded, May 29, 2026, just days after Wright's viral moment and at a moment of peak public interest in Restore Britain's political trajectory. The discussion is notable for its combination of intellectual depth — Wright ranges from Catholic Social Teaching to AJP Taylor's pre-WWI England to James Burnham on elite power — with frank engagement on contentious subjects including immigration, demographic change, institutional failure, and the limits of liberal democratic legitimacy.

## Implications
This episode captures a significant moment in British right-wing populism: a Catholic intellectual who went viral for articulating widespread working-class disillusionment is now explicitly aligning himself with a fast-growing political party that sits to the right of Reform UK. Wright's framework — linking immigration, economic dysfunction, institutional corruption, media manipulation, and cultural decline into a single unified critique of liberal modernity — represents a more philosophically coherent version of the grievances that have driven Brexit, the rise of Reform, and now Restore. The fact that a two-and-a-half-hour intellectual podcast on these themes can attract the audience McCormack commands suggests that the demand for serious engagement with these ideas, rather than mere culture-war soundbites, is substantial and growing.

The episode also raises questions that extend beyond British politics. Wright's argument that reality itself has become so bad that it is doing the work of political radicalisation — without any need for extremist recruiting — is a diagnosis that, if accurate, has implications for how policymakers, media institutions, and centrist political parties think about de-radicalisation and political legitimacy. If the problem is not bad-faith propaganda but accurate perception of genuine institutional failure, then the standard response of labelling critics as extremists or conspiracy theorists only accelerates the delegitimisation of the very institutions those responses are meant to protect.
