# Dan Niles: We Are 100% in an AI Bubble... Don't Go Broke Trying to Call the Pop

## Key Points
- Niles flatly states the market is 100% in a bubble, but argues this is not a reason to exit — bubbles can persist and expand significantly before bursting, and going broke trying to call the top is a real and common investor mistake.
- He maps the current AI cycle to 1997-1998 rather than 1999-2000, placing the market in the middle innings of a multi-year infrastructure buildout rather than at peak speculation.
- NVIDIA's quarterly revenue has increased approximately 9.5 times over three years, a strong number but still short of Cisco's approximately 15-fold surge at the dot-com peak, suggesting the cycle has more room.
- The shift from conversational AI to agentic AI — autonomous systems that browse, research, and coordinate complex workflows — requires 10 to 100 times more compute tokens, creating a structural step-change in demand for AI infrastructure.
- Hyperscale cloud provider capex growth expectations have risen from approximately 30% to 70% within 2026 alone, reflecting genuine AI infrastructure demand rather than speculative over-ordering.
- Niles identifies early 2027 as the likely timeframe for a major correction, forecasting semiconductor stocks could fall 30-50% from their eventual highs as growth rates face tough year-over-year comparisons.
- Pending mega-IPOs from OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX could create significant liquidity pressure on markets by absorbing capital that would otherwise support existing AI stocks.
- He advocates holding meaningfully elevated cash alongside AI-exposed positions as a tactical hedge, noting that being highly vigilant about how the cycle eventually resolves is as important as participating in the upside.
- The PHLX Semiconductor Index posted its best start to a year on record in 2026, up approximately 82%, which Niles views as consistent with late-cycle exuberance but not yet at a terminal extreme.
- Niles' investing philosophy for bubble environments: 'Be Nimble. Hold strong convictions, but hold them loosely' — size positions to survive a correction while still capturing the remaining upside.
- Macro risk signals Niles monitors include simultaneous record stock highs, elevated oil prices, and high Treasury yields — he warns one of these metrics will eventually reprice sharply, potentially triggering market turmoil.
- He cites ChatGPT's November 2022 launch as the starting gun for the current AI cycle, meaning the buildout is entering only its fourth year — comparable to the early Netscape browser era of the internet, when every macro shock proved to be a buying opportunity.

## Overview
Dan Nathan hosts Dan Niles, founder and portfolio manager of Niles Investment Management, for a wide-ranging conversation about the state of the AI-driven market in mid-2026. Niles opens with a blunt assessment: the market is unambiguously in a bubble, driven by AI enthusiasm and easy money — but his central message is that acknowledging a bubble does not mean you should bet against it or stand aside. The historically informed playbook, he argues, is to ride the bubble with discipline and vigilance rather than go broke trying to call the top.

Niles draws systematic parallels to the late 1990s dot-com era, placing 2026 in the role of roughly 1997-1998 — years three and four of a genuine infrastructure buildout, not the frothy speculative peak of 1999-2000. He points to NVIDIA's approximately 9.5-fold quarterly revenue increase over three years as impressive but still lagging Cisco's 15-fold surge during the dot-com peak, which suggests the cycle has meaningful runway left. The AI buildout only launched in late 2022 with ChatGPT, making it relatively young compared to the multi-year internet ramp. Niles sees the macro backdrop — easy money, relentless AI capital expenditure from hyperscalers, and real productivity improvements — as analogous to the forces that kept the 1990s bull market alive through multiple macro shocks.

A key structural driver Niles emphasizes is the emergence of agentic AI. Unlike conversational chatbots, autonomous agents that can browse the web, aggregate data, produce reports, and coordinate multi-step workflows require 10 to 100 times more compute tokens per session. This step-change in compute intensity has caused hyperscale cloud providers to raise capex guidance dramatically — from roughly 30% growth expectations at the start of the year to 70% — and Niles views this as a genuine demand signal, not just hype. He remains constructively positioned in semiconductors, networking infrastructure, and AI-adjacent companies while carrying elevated cash to manage downside risk.

Looking ahead, Niles is candid about the eventual reckoning. He forecasts a significant correction in early 2027, potentially 30-50% in semiconductor stocks, driven by slowing growth as AI comparisons are taken against an inflated 2026 base, potential liquidity stress from massive IPOs including OpenAI and Anthropic, and the natural exhaustion of speculative momentum. His advice to investors: stay in the trade, be nimble, hold convictions loosely, hold meaningful cash, and watch the signals closely — but don't let fear of the eventual bust cause you to miss the remaining upside.

## Implications
Dan Niles' framework offers a pragmatic template for navigating speculative markets that have underlying technological substance. His acknowledgment that a bubble is 100% underway, combined with a constructive near-term posture, challenges the binary thinking that dominates retail investor discourse — where one must either be fully bearish on a bubble or fully dismissive of the risk. The episode is significant because Niles, a veteran chip analyst with two decades of experience across multiple technology cycles, is explicitly telling investors that the historical record supports riding bubble momentum while managing risk, rather than trying to heroically time the exit. His timeline — roughly one more year of upside before a 2027 reckoning — gives investors a concrete planning horizon and specific signals to monitor, most importantly the transition of agentic AI growth rates from acceleration to deceleration.

The broader market implication is that the debate is no longer about whether AI valuations are stretched but about where in the bubble lifecycle markets currently sit. Niles' case for 1997-1998 rather than 1999 is consequential: if correct, it means the largest gains and most dangerous overextension are still ahead, and investors who exit now could miss a final leg that exceeds current highs significantly. His warning about the 2027 correction is equally important — the eventual bust in AI-facing semiconductors could rival dot-com era drawdowns in magnitude, and investors who remain fully invested without a defensive framework risk catastrophic losses. The "don't go broke trying to call the pop" framing is the central practical lesson: the cost of being too early on the short side is just as real as the cost of holding too long.
