# Shameful Secret Heart

## Key Points
- SpaceX filed for an IPO targeting a valuation of up to $2 trillion — roughly 100 times revenue — for a company that is currently losing money, which the hosts attribute almost entirely to an 'Elon Musk premium.'
- SpaceX's S-1 prospectus opens with 12 pages of rocket and Mars imagery but pivots to enterprise AI as the core medium-term revenue thesis, despite the AI business only being stood up a few months before the filing.
- SpaceX has approximately $18 billion in annual revenue run rate, generated primarily from satellite launches and Starlink subscriptions.
- The Nasdaq 100 agreed to add SpaceX within 15 days of its IPO (and the Russell 3000 within 5 days), despite SpaceX's free float being only about 5% of shares at launch due to lock-ups — a rules waiver that has angered many market participants.
- Elon Musk controls 85% of SpaceX voting power through super-voting stock, can appoint a majority of directors unilaterally, owes no fiduciary duty to bring SpaceX business opportunities he could pursue elsewhere, and can only be sued for securities fraud in Texas state court.
- The IPO is expected around June 11 (a Thursday), with approximately $75 billion of stock to be issued — making it one of the largest IPOs on record.
- Robinhood announced agentic AI trading that lets users connect AI agents to brokerage and credit card accounts via natural-language instructions, executing trades such as 'buy oversold stocks and sell when they revert to the mean.'
- Matt Levine argues AI agents will replace the wave of single-strategy thematic ETFs as the universal wrapper for retail trade ideas, since zero-commission trading removes the transaction-cost barrier that previously made high-frequency algorithmic strategies impractical for retail.
- The Enhanced Games, a pro-doping athletic competition partly backed by Peter Thiel, held its first event in Las Vegas — setting one world record in swimming — but three of the winning athletes were 'clean,' and Enhanced Games shares fell roughly 40% after the event.
- Fernando Tatis Jr. lost his lawsuit against Big League Advance on procedural grounds (untimely objection to arbitration), meaning he still owes approximately $34 million — 10% of his $340 million Padres contract — to the firm that gave him a $2 million advance when he was a minor leaguer.
- Fernando Tatis Jr.'s father (Fernando Tatis Sr.) had career earnings of approximately $20 million, raising questions in media coverage about whether the son genuinely needed the $2 million advance or was making a poor financial hedge.
- Matt frames athlete income-share agreements as venture-style equity portfolios: sometimes the player never makes the majors and the investor loses everything; sometimes (as with Tatis) the player signs a $340 million deal and the return is extraordinary.

## Overview
This episode of Money Stuff: The Podcast opens with Matt Levine and Katie Greifeld catching up after separate absences — a car crashing into Matt's house and Katie's trip to Ireland — before diving into the anticipated SpaceX IPO. The filing reveals a company with roughly $18 billion in annual revenue run rate, primarily from satellite launches and Starlink service, but whose prospectus is heavily reframed around enterprise AI despite the AI business only gaining real traction very recently. The hosts dissect the peculiar "U-shaped" narrative of the S-1: space, then AI, then Mars colonies, and note that SpaceX's implied valuation of up to $2 trillion — around 100 times revenue for a money-losing company — is almost entirely an "Elon Musk premium." They examine the fast-tracking of SpaceX into major indexes (Nasdaq 100 within 15 days of IPO, Russell 3000 within 5 days) despite a free float of only about 5% at launch, and discuss the price dynamics this will create as index demand collides with limited supply.

The governance structure of SpaceX gets particular attention: Elon Musk holds 85% voting control via super-voting stock, can appoint more than 51% of directors regardless of vote outcomes, faces no fiduciary duty obligations to bring SpaceX corporate opportunities he could instead pursue elsewhere, and can essentially only be sued for securities fraud in Texas state court. The hosts see this as a deliberate corrective to what happened with Tesla in Delaware, and speculate it may set a new precedent for founder-controlled IPOs. They also note that for index fund investors who dislike the governance or politics, the only current recourse is ESG funds or a hypothetical "S&P 500 ex-Elon Musk" index — which Matt argues would sell like hotcakes.

The second major topic is Robinhood's announcement of agentic AI trading, allowing users to connect AI agents to brokerage accounts and credit cards, with the agents executing trades or purchases based on natural-language instructions within set financial limits. Matt argues this represents the next evolution beyond the zero-commission trading revolution, enabling retail investors to implement complex strategies (mean reversion, dip buying, technical triggers) without transaction cost friction. He muses that AI agents will ultimately replace the proliferation of single-strategy ETFs as the universal wrapper for trade ideas, though he notes coordination risk if large numbers of retail investors program identical strategies.

The episode closes with two lighter stories: the Enhanced Games — a pro-doping sports competition backed in part by Peter Thiel — which held its first event in Las Vegas with mixed results (one world record set, three "clean" athletes winning events, and the stock falling 40% the next day); and the Fernando Tatis Jr. lawsuit against Big League Advance, the company that paid him $2 million for 10% of his future MLB earnings when he was a minor leaguer. Tatis ultimately lost on procedural grounds (failing to object to arbitration in time) and still owes roughly $34 million to Big League Advance despite his $340 million Padres contract, prompting a broader conversation about whether these athlete income-share agreements are legitimate equity investments or predatory loans.

## Implications
The SpaceX IPO and its index fast-tracking saga illustrates a structural shift in how founder-controlled companies can access public markets with essentially no governance constraints. If the IPO succeeds at a multi-trillion-dollar valuation, it will validate an entirely new template for founder-friendly public offerings — one where CEOs face no meaningful shareholder checks, no Delaware fiduciary accountability, and no obligation to channel business opportunities to the public company. Matt's observation that every other founder-CEO will want to copy the Elon Musk pay and governance structure if this goes well is not hyperbole; it echoes exactly what happened after Tesla's compensation package battles, where boards at other companies used the outcome as a benchmark. For passive index investors, the implication is uncomfortable: the more the market rewards companies with unchecked founder control, the more those governance structures will be embedded in the very indexes that ordinary retirement savers cannot opt out of.

The Robinhood agentic trading announcement and the Tatis income-share lawsuit together point to a broader theme the hosts return to throughout the episode: financial instruments that democratize access also redistribute risk in ways that are not always obvious to the participants. Zero-commission trading made agentic strategies viable for retail; now retail investors can program bots to execute hundreds of trades a day with no visible friction, even as invisible transaction costs (payment for order flow, bid-ask spreads) remain. Similarly, athlete income-sharing agreements looked like democratized venture capital when players needed cash, but the asymmetry of information — sophisticated institutional investors evaluating player talent against players who may not fully understand the math — can produce outcomes that feel exploitative even when they are technically legal and contractually sound.
