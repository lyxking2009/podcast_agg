# Gita Gopinath on Why Interest Rates Have Surged All Around the World

## Key Points
- The era of 'secular stagnation' and low-for-long interest rates has definitively ended, driven by three converging forces: higher inflation expectations, large and persistent fiscal deficits (the US is running roughly 7% of GDP), and the AI investment boom which has shifted r-star upward from near zero pre-pandemic to around 1 percentage point now.
- AI is pushing up interest rates through two distinct channels simultaneously: a financial markets channel (AI companies now account for roughly 50% of investment-grade corporate bond issuance year-to-date and nearly 40% of junk-rated issuance) and a real economy channel (AI build-out consumes energy, skilled labor, land, and construction capacity, creating inflationary pressure).
- The composition of marginal buyers of US Treasuries has changed fundamentally — central banks have stepped back from QE and are now reducing holdings (including the Bank of Japan), replaced by more volatile non-bank financial institutions and private investors, which structurally increases rate sensitivity and yield volatility.
- Foreign holdings of US equities have reached $40 trillion, a historic high and roughly twice as large relative to the rest of the world's GDP as it was at the peak of the 2000 dot-com bubble, meaning the entire global investment community is deeply exposed to a US equity and AI-driven narrative.
- Global capital demand is structurally elevated across multiple categories simultaneously: AI investment, defense buildout, energy security and renewables, healthcare for aging populations, and national self-sufficiency spending — while the only major offsetting supply of savings comes from aging demographics.
- Gopinath introduced the concept of 'BLISS' (Big Lasting State Support) to describe how governments have habitually backstopped economies since the pandemic, but warns that sharply reduced fiscal capacity means governments cannot replicate the roughly 25% of GDP in advanced-economy support provided during COVID in the next crisis.
- A debt crisis in an advanced economy borrowing in its own currency would not look like an emerging-market foreign-currency default; instead it would manifest as a credit crunch, sharp rise in borrowing costs across asset classes, a collapse in investment, and a prolonged debt overhang that persistently slows growth.
- Any explicit central bank yield curve control in the current environment — buying long-term debt to cap rates — would quickly cause inflation expectations to drift upward, raise nominal and real rates, and destroy the credibility of central bank independence, making such a strategy self-defeating.
- China's large trade surplus is not a sign of overwhelming export dominance but rather a reflection of a collapse in domestic investment following the property market crash (investment in China actually declined in 2025) and suppressed consumption; Gopinath does not believe the 'China produces everything' scenario is sustainable as trading partners will impose tariffs.
- The AI productivity scenario matters enormously for fiscal sustainability: analysts project AI could add two percentage points per year to productivity growth (from a current baseline of roughly two percent), but Gopinath notes there is currently no empirical evidence of such a wave materializing, making the risk of AI disappointment a significant tail risk for global debt markets.
- Western economies have been resilient partly because pandemic-era fiscal support left household and corporate balance sheets stronger than before COVID, but this source of strength is now largely depleted and cannot be replicated at the same scale going forward.
- Gopinath warns that policymakers are increasingly likely to resort to unorthodox measures — price controls, financial repression — as fiscal capacity shrinks and political constraints make entitlement cuts or austerity nearly impossible to implement in elected democracies.

## Overview
Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal welcome Harvard economics professor and former IMF First Deputy Managing Director Gita Gopinath to discuss the global bond market selloff and the structural forces driving interest rates higher across developed economies. Gopinath explains that the surge in yields is not a transitory phenomenon but rather a genuine regime change — a confluence of higher inflation expectations, persistently large fiscal deficits, an AI-driven investment boom, and a fundamental shift in who holds government debt. Together, these forces have ended the era of "low for long" interest rates that defined the pre-pandemic decade.

Gopinath breaks down how the AI buildout is affecting both real and nominal interest rates through two distinct channels: a financial crowding-out effect where massive corporate bond issuance by AI companies competes with sovereign debt, and a real-economy channel where AI investment consumes physical inputs — land, power, skilled labor, construction capacity — creating inflationary pressure. She argues that the good news is that rising r-star driven by genuine productivity gains helps address fiscal problems over time, but the bad news is that the US fiscal deficit is also pushing r-star higher without necessarily generating compensating growth. She also highlights the dramatic shift in the composition of marginal buyers of US Treasuries, from stable foreign central banks to more volatile non-bank financial institutions, which amplifies rate sensitivity.

The conversation ranges widely across the sources of sustained capital demand: AI investment, defense spending, energy security buildout, demographic-driven healthcare costs, and the global retreat from pure efficiency-based trade toward national self-sufficiency. Gopinath introduces the concept of "BLISS" — Big Lasting State Support — to describe how governments worldwide have sustained demand through repeated large-scale interventions since the pandemic, and warns that diminished fiscal capacity means this backstop will be far weaker in the next shock. She also discusses how a debt crisis in an advanced economy that borrows in its own currency would likely manifest not as a sudden default but as a credit crunch, rising borrowing costs, and a slow erosion of growth.

The hosts and guest conclude by identifying AI productivity as the central variable on which the sustainability of the global fiscal and financial system now hinges. If AI delivers meaningful productivity gains — potentially adding two percentage points per year to growth — many of the structural imbalances become more manageable. If the productivity boom fails to materialize, government debt burdens will become increasingly unmanageable and market pricing of sovereign risk will deteriorate sharply. Gopinath also touches on China's trade surplus dynamics, the limits of central bank yield curve control, and the unprecedented degree to which global equity portfolios are concentrated in US AI-linked stocks.

## Implications
The conversation underscores that the global interest rate environment has entered a fundamentally new regime with potentially long-lasting consequences for asset allocation, fiscal policy, and economic governance. If AI productivity gains do materialize at scale, the current elevated rate environment may prove manageable — even as a catalyst for bringing debt-to-GDP ratios down — but if they do not, governments that have grown dependent on BLISS-style backstops will face a reckoning with markets that are increasingly less forgiving of fiscal profligacy. The shift from central bank buyers to volatile private-sector holders of sovereign debt means that any policy misstep or confidence shock could transmit quickly and harshly into borrowing costs, with limited room for the kind of unconventional interventions that provided relief in prior crises.

For investors and policymakers alike, the episode highlights a paradox: markets are priced for a benign AI-driven productivity boom while simultaneously revealing the structural vulnerabilities that would make a disappointing AI outcome deeply destabilizing. The $40 trillion concentration of global equity wealth in US markets, combined with the 50% AI share of investment-grade bond issuance, means that a repricing of AI growth expectations would simultaneously hit equity valuations, corporate credit spreads, and sovereign bond markets — a correlated shock across asset classes for which many developed-economy governments have little fiscal buffer remaining.
