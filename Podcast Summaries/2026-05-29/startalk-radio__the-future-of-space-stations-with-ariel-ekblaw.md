# The Future of Space Stations with Ariel Ekblaw

## Key Points
- The International Space Station is on track for a controlled deorbit into the Pacific Ocean by the end of 2030, and NASA is transitioning to purchasing services from commercially developed platforms rather than owning and operating the next station.
- Ekblaw's TESSERAE technology uses magnetized, self-assembling tiles based on buckyball geometry that pack flat for launch and autonomously assemble in orbit, enabling structures far larger than conventional rocket-fairing-limited modules.
- TESSERAE prototypes were successfully tested aboard the ISS during Axiom Space's Axiom Mission 1, demonstrating autonomous tile assembly and self-correcting docking mechanisms in microgravity.
- Commercial successors to the ISS — including Axiom Station and Orbital Reef — still rely on the traditional axial module model with limited crew capacity; Aurelia Institute's work aims to move beyond this architectural paradigm.
- Microgravity manufacturing enables uniquely superior products: near-perfect ball bearings, retinal tissue that grows without gravitational deformation, and pharmaceutical crystals (including cancer drug Keytruda) of higher purity than Earth-grown equivalents.
- Biotech and pharmaceutical research in orbit benefits from the absence of convection, sedimentation, and buoyancy forces that interfere with Earth-based experiments, opening new possibilities for protein folding and drug development.
- Orbital and lunar data centers are emerging as a serious proposal, leveraging the vacuum of space for passive cooling and continuous solar energy exposure as AI-driven power demand strains terrestrial grid infrastructure.
- Space-based solar power concepts — collecting solar energy in orbit and beaming it to Earth as microwaves — are discussed as a long-term energy solution that bypasses the atmospheric and day/night limitations of ground-based solar.
- Ekblaw's team at Aurelia Institute is conducting trade studies evaluating more than 50 distinct habitat concepts, drawing design inspiration from science fiction sources including Neal Stephenson's Seveneves and Larry Niven's Ringworld.
- Orbital debris management is identified as a prerequisite for sustainable expansion in low Earth orbit, and multiple clean-up and mitigation strategies are in active development across the industry.
- Accessibility in space habitation is a focus for Ekblaw, who notes that conditions such as wheelchair reliance may confer physical advantages in microgravity, and that Aurelia has partnered with AstroAccess to prepare orbital environments for people with disabilities.

## Overview
In this episode of StarTalk Radio (Season 17, Episode 32), Neil deGrasse Tyson is joined by co-hosts Chuck Nice and Gary O'Reilly along with guest Ariel Ekblaw — founder and CEO of the Aurelia Institute and former director of the MIT Space Exploration Initiative — to chart the trajectory of human life and industry in low Earth orbit. The conversation centers on the looming transition away from the International Space Station, whose planned controlled deorbit into the Pacific Ocean by the end of the decade sets the stage for a new era of commercially operated platforms. Ekblaw explains what it means to move from the government-built ISS model to a marketplace of privately developed stations, and what Aurelia Institute is doing to accelerate that transition through radically new architectural approaches.

A significant portion of the discussion focuses on Ekblaw's signature TESSERAE (Tessellated Electromagnetic Space Structures for the Exploration of Reconfigurable, Adaptive Environments) technology — modular, self-assembling tiles inspired by the geometry of buckyballs that can pack flat for launch and autonomously assemble in zero gravity. Ekblaw walks the hosts through how this design, which successfully underwent testing aboard the ISS during Axiom Mission 1, could produce space stations far larger than anything achievable by launching pre-fabricated cylinder modules through a rocket fairing. She draws on biomimicry and geodesic dome principles to argue that nature-inspired architecture is uniquely suited to the constraints of orbital construction.

The hosts probe the economic and scientific rationale for maintaining human presence in orbit, and Ekblaw delivers a roster of compelling use cases: manufacturing near-perfect ball bearings in microgravity, growing retinal tissue free from gravitational sagging, crystallizing pharmaceuticals such as Keytruda with higher purity, and conducting biotech research unimpeded by convection and sedimentation. The conversation then broadens to include speculative but credible future applications — orbital data centers cooled by the vacuum of space and powered by abundant sunlight, space-based solar power arrays beaming energy back to Earth, and strategies for managing the growing hazard of orbital debris. Ekblaw frames all of this within an optimistic but grounded timeline for what low Earth orbit could look like within the next two decades.

## Implications
This episode arrives at a pivotal inflection point for human spaceflight, as the end of the ISS era forces a fundamental question about what orbital infrastructure should look like and who should build it. Ekblaw's work represents a broader shift in thinking — away from government-owned monolithic stations and toward a diverse, commercially operated ecosystem of purpose-built platforms. If self-assembling architectures like TESSERAE prove scalable, they could dramatically reduce launch mass requirements and make truly large-scale orbital habitats economically viable for the first time, compressing timelines that currently seem measured in decades. The pharmaceutical and biotech manufacturing use cases in particular suggest that the economic case for sustained orbital presence may not rest on adventure tourism or national prestige but on high-value, Earth-return products that simply cannot be made on the ground.

The episode's discussion of data centers and space-based solar power reflects how rapidly orbital infrastructure is being re-imagined as critical to Earth-side industries rather than as an isolated scientific outpost. As AI workloads drive unprecedented energy demand, the prospect of combining passive vacuum cooling with uninterrupted solar exposure is shifting from science fiction thought experiment to active investment thesis. At the same time, the orbital debris problem looms as an existential constraint on all of these ambitions — a point Ekblaw's framing implicitly acknowledges by treating debris management as a prerequisite rather than an afterthought. Together, these threads make the case that the next chapter of space exploration will be defined less by the heroism of individual astronauts and more by the engineering of orbital real estate.
