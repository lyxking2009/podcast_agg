# Remembering the Life and Work of Jonathan Clements

## Key Points
- Jonathan Clements was a Wall Street Journal personal finance columnist for nearly 20 years and founder of Humble Dollar, and is credited by Jason Zweig as being second only to Jack Bogle in pushing index fund investing into mainstream American consciousness.
- Clements wrote two to three hundred columns advocating for index funds over active management, generating significant hate mail and hostility from fund managers whose fees and livelihoods he was challenging.
- William Bernstein credits Clements for launching his own career: Clements discovered Bernstein's early web-based investing research in the 1990s and began quoting him in the Wall Street Journal, which opened doors to his published books and financial advisory business.
- Clements received a terminal cancer diagnosis and was initially given five to twelve months to live; he ultimately survived longer than predicted and joked to Zweig that he was 'already playing in overtime.'
- Clements curated The Best of Jonathan Clements, a self-published collection of 60 columns from over 1,000 written, organized by Zweig and Bernstein, which raised approximately $60,000 for charitable purposes plus substantially more through the Bogle Center.
- The charitable initiative funds a research partnership with J-PAL (a behavioral economics institute at MIT co-run by Nobel laureate Esther Duflo) to pilot programs encouraging low-income Boston high school students to open Roth IRAs and develop savings habits.
- Clements' philosophy on money evolved from debt repayment and portfolio building to a three-level framework: possessions rarely bring lasting happiness; shared experiences with loved ones create memories; and using money to create meaning through giving is the highest use.
- Bernstein describes Clements as having a very high hedonic set point and near-zero neuroticism score, which allowed him to face his terminal diagnosis with genuine humor and peace rather than a brave front.
- Clements accelerated his own engagement and marriage to his partner Elaine after his diagnosis, attended his son's London wedding, and organized family trips with siblings and his mother -- all while managing chemotherapy, radiation, and surgical procedures.
- His posthumous memoir Money and Me, publishing May 26, 2026, is described as a biography and philosophical reflection on his life's journey through money and happiness, with only 10-15 percent of the book addressing his terminal illness.
- Zweig notes that Clements received thousands of letters and emails from readers -- dying patients, caregivers, the bereaved, and the afraid -- whose most common comment was 'he was my friend,' despite none of them having ever met him.
- Bernstein's most memorable observation about Clements: 'The most powerful force in the financial universe is amnesia' -- a line that encapsulates both Clements' humor and his lifetime message about investor behavior.

## Overview
This special edition of Masters in Business is a tribute to Jonathan Clements, the legendary Wall Street Journal personal finance columnist and founder of the Humble Dollar, who passed away after receiving a terminal cancer diagnosis. Host Barry Ritholtz is joined by two of Clements' closest friends and colleagues -- Jason Zweig, Wall Street Journal columnist, and William Bernstein, neurologist-turned-investment-author -- to reflect on his life, his influence on personal finance journalism, and his forthcoming posthumous memoir, Money and Me, publishing May 2026.

The conversation covers Clements' singular role in championing index fund investing for ordinary Americans, a cause he took up before it was mainstream and held with unwavering integrity despite receiving hate mail and hostility from active fund managers whose livelihoods he was challenging. Both Zweig and Bernstein credit Clements as second only to Jack Bogle in pushing the indexing message in front of retail investors, and note that the savings in fees his advocacy helped generate run into the hundreds of billions of dollars. The group also discusses his evolving philosophy -- from practical portfolio building to a deeper exploration of the relationship between money and happiness, drawing on hedonic psychology research.

The episode also details the legacy project Clements initiated before his death: The Best of Jonathan Clements, a self-published compilation of 60 of his best columns, all proceeds going to fund research with the MIT-based behavioral economics group J-PAL on encouraging low-income young adults to open Roth IRAs and develop long-term investing habits. The book raised approximately $60,000, with additional donations through the Bogle Center adding substantially more to fund a pilot program with Boston-area high school students. Clements' final memoir, Money and Me, is described as a joyful rather than somber account of his journey through money, happiness, and mortality.

Throughout the episode, Zweig and Bernstein describe a man of extraordinary positivity and equanimity -- someone who joked about his diagnosis as "a great marketing strategy," accelerated his son's wedding plans, and continued writing until his final days because, as Bernstein put it, "he could no sooner stop writing than he could stop breathing." The word "joy" is used by Zweig to characterize Clements' approach to dying, and the conversation is explicitly framed not as a mournful tribute but as a celebration of a life fully and meaningfully lived.

## Implications
This episode serves as a portrait of one of the most consequential but least celebrated figures in the democratization of personal finance. Jonathan Clements' decades-long advocacy for low-cost index funds, conducted with intellectual integrity against significant professional pressure, contributed materially to the now-dominant shift of hundreds of billions of dollars from high-fee active management to passive investing. His career illustrates how consistent, principled journalism aimed at ordinary investors -- not institutional players -- can reshape an entire industry over time.

The conversation also highlights the underappreciated dimension of Clements' later work: applying behavioral and hedonic psychology research to help people understand that money's highest purpose is not accumulation but the enabling of a life defined by experiences, relationships, and meaning. His final book and the J-PAL research initiative he inspired represent a direct extension of that mission to populations -- low-income young adults -- traditionally ignored by the wealth management industry. In that sense, Clements' legacy spans from changing how Wall Street operates to funding academic research aimed at the people farthest from Wall Street's benefits.

> "The most powerful force in the financial universe is amnesia. People forget it." -- William Bernstein

> "He could no sooner stop writing than he could stop breathing." -- William Bernstein

> "The single most common thing that readers said about Jonathan was, 'He was my friend.' And they said that even though none of them had ever met him." -- Jason Zweig
