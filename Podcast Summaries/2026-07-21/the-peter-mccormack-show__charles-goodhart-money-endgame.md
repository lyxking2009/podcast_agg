# #194 - Charles Goodhart: The Money Endgame - Debt, Inflation & Central Bank Failure

**Show:** The Peter McCormack Show
**Date:** 2026-07-21
**Guest:** Charles Goodhart, Economist (LSE Financial Markets Group)
**Duration:** 60 min

## Key Points

- Britain's decline did not start in 2008 — it has deeper structural roots in demographic and fiscal trends.
- The post-war economic boom (1950-2020) may have been a 'temporary lucky era' driven by favorable demographics, falling dependency ratios, and globalization.
- The next 30-40 years could be far harder than people expect due to ageing populations, falling birth rates, and rising dependency ratios.
- Governments face a fiscal crisis they don't want to admit — public debt, defence spending, climate costs, and political short-termism are compounding.
- Central banks may struggle to keep inflation under control as politicians cannot win elections by telling voters the truth.
- Democracy itself may block the long-term planning countries need — short electoral cycles prevent necessary structural reforms.
- Immigration, housing policy, land tax, AI's impact on white-collar jobs, and the 'triple lock' pension system are all part of the crisis.
- GDP does not equal welfare or happiness — the Western world may never return to the conditions that made 1950-2020 so prosperous.

## Overview

Charles Goodhart joins Peter McCormack for a sweeping conversation about the end of the monetary and fiscal era that defined the post-war world. Goodhart argues that the 1950-2020 period was uniquely prosperous due to favorable demographics, falling interest rates, and globalization — conditions that are now reversing. Ageing populations, rising dependency ratios, and mounting public debt are creating fiscal crises that politicians are unwilling to address honestly. The conversation covers immigration, housing, land taxation, AI's impact on white-collar employment, the triple lock pension system, and whether democracy can accommodate the long-term planning needed to navigate these structural challenges.

## Implications

The 'temporary lucky era' thesis challenges the assumption that historical returns are a reliable guide to the future. Fiscal sustainability risks are underpriced in sovereign bond markets. The demographic reversal thesis has implications for housing, pensions, healthcare, and immigration policy. Investors should consider how structural changes affect long-term asset allocation.
