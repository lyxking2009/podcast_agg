# James Bosworth on What Comes After Lula's Stunning Win — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Lula da Silva defied polls and prediction markets to win Brazil's presidential election, defeating Flavio Bolsonaro in a runoff. James Bosworth of the Stimson Center attributes the upset to Bolsonaro's scandals and weak candidacy after the first round made him the focus, while Lula's improved social media and ground game mobilized voters. The episode also covers Venezuela's political stalemate, oil industry caution, and Trump's influence across the region.

## Key points
- Lula won despite prediction markets giving Bolsonaro as high as an 87% chance to win; Bosworth gave Bolsonaro a 64% chance the night before but acknowledges the markets were overconfident and missed key dynamics.
- Flavio Bolsonaro carried severe negatives: his father Jair was convicted for a 2022-2023 coup attempt; Flavio faced money laundering accusations, ties to Banco Master's Daniel Vocaro, WhatsApp requests for millions of dollars, a scandal involving pharmaceutical lobbyists and prostitutes, and his escape plane was linked to drug trafficking and money laundering investigations.
- The election's shift was explained by the two-round structure: the first round was an anti-incumbent referendum on Lula, while the second round turned the spotlight onto Bolsonaro, allowing Lula to catch up and win.
- Lula's campaign improved dramatically in the second round: he got his first smartphone, launched 'Lula's Army' WhatsApp group with over 10 million members (more than 5% of voters), and used 30-second debate clips optimized for social media.
- Turnout shifts were small but decisive: less than 2% change in turnout plus 1-2% shift from non-voters/blank ballots, but in an election won by 4 million votes, these shifts flipped the outcome.
- The Bolsonaro political brand may be weakening: party members suggest Tarcísio de Freitas or another right-wing candidate would have beaten Lula, and there is pressure to avoid running a Bolsonaro in 2030.
- Religious voting split sharply: evangelicals largely voted for Bolsonaro, Catholics for Lula, as evangelical growth in Brazil approaches parity with Catholicism, driven by aggressive recruiting and conservative social policy alignment.
- Trump's influence defines Latin America regardless of election outcomes: every leader must respond to tariffs and pressure, but Bosworth argues anti-incumbent sentiment, not ideological alignment, drives electoral swings; higher fuel costs from the Iran war hurt incumbents broadly without a specific penalty for pro-Trump leaders.
- Marco Rubio is pushing for Venezuelan elections within six to nine months, while Delcy Rodriguez likely wants to delay until 2028-2029; a status of forces agreement under discussion would allow US military training and support, not a permanent base.
- Maria Karina Machado would win a free and fair Venezuelan election with better than 90% probability, especially if expats vote; if elected, she would need to consolidate military loyalty, fix the economy and currency, attract oil investment, and restore democratic checks and balances.
- Oil majors remain cautious on Venezuela: Chevron is in, but Exxon and Conoco will not commit without rule-of-law certainty for long-term contracts; smaller companies are entering for quick deals, which won't drive rebuild.
- Upcoming elections to watch include local contests in Ecuador (amid El Niño blackouts), Guatemala, and especially Argentina, where Javier Milei's re-election prospects will signal the next pendulum swing in Latin America.
