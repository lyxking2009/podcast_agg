# Things You Thought You Knew – Is Everything Light?

**Host:** Neil deGrasse Tyson
**Co-Host:** Chuck Nice

## Key Points

- **Albedo measures how much light a surface reflects back into space**, ranging from 0 (perfectly absorbing) to 1 (perfectly reflecting). The Moon appears bright only because it's against a dark sky — it actually has a very low albedo of just 7-12%, while fresh snow has high albedo and asphalt has very low albedo. This concept helps explain why darker skin with more melanin absorbs more light and stays cooler under the Sun.

- **Earth appears significantly brighter when viewed from the Moon** than the Moon appears from Earth due to differences in albedo and size. Earthshine is the phenomenon where sunlight reflected off Earth faintly illuminates the dark portion of the Moon, creating the 'old Moon in the new Moon's arms' effect.

- **Light pollution is rapidly degrading astronomical observation capabilities** in urban areas, causing the loss of fainter stars, the Milky Way, and deep-sky objects. The International Dark-Sky Association works to preserve dark skies through smart lighting design, while other forms of pollution include radio interference and satellite streaking across telescope images.

- **All electromagnetic waves — from radio to gamma rays — are fundamentally the same phenomenon: light**. This revolutionary understanding emerged in the 19th century, expanding the definition beyond just visible light. Frequency determines energy level, with higher frequencies carrying more energy, explaining why UV light can damage skin while visible light cannot.

- **William Herschel's 1800 experiment** with a prism and thermometers led to the discovery of infrared radiation by detecting heat beyond the red end of the visible spectrum. This groundbreaking finding proved that invisible "calorific rays" existed, laying the foundation for modern astrophysics and everyday technologies like remote controls and thermal imaging.

- **The electromagnetic spectrum nomenclature reveals historical understanding**: ultraviolet literally means "beyond violet" (the highest-frequency visible light), while terms like "gamma rays" represent the highest-energy electromagnetic waves with no hard cutoff — just increasingly rare, energetic photons.

- **Practical applications of electromagnetic spectrum knowledge** include microwave ovens using waves about 12 cm long, sunscreen working by absorbing or reflecting UV light before it damages skin cells, and satellite pollution mitigation through darkening techniques, orbital predictions, and software removal.

## Overview

This StarTalk Radio episode takes listeners on an illuminating journey through the physics of light, beginning with the deceptively simple question of what makes things appear bright or dark. Neil deGrasse Tyson and Chuck Nice explore the concept of albedo — how much light surfaces reflect — using examples from the surprisingly dim Moon to the brilliant appearance of Earth as seen from space. The conversation evolves into a broader discussion about light pollution and its impact on astronomy, touching on radio interference and the growing problem of satellite megaconstellations streaking across telescope images. The hosts then tackle the fundamental question posed in the episode title: "Is Everything Light?" They trace the historical expansion of our understanding from visible light to the entire electromagnetic spectrum, using Tyson's memorable potato analogy to explain how radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays are all variations of the same phenomenon. The episode culminates with the story of William Herschel's pioneering 1800 experiment that discovered infrared radiation by placing thermometers beyond the red end of a prism-split rainbow, proving that invisible "calorific rays" existed and fundamentally changing our understanding of light and the universe.
