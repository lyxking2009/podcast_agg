# Embracing the Bond Market: Masters in Business with Robin Wigglesworth — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
Robin Wigglesworth, editor of FT Alphaville, discusses his new book 'A Fabulous Debt,' tracing how bonds built the modern world from Venice's 1171 war loan to modern securitization and AI-driven debt. He argues the bond market is the engine of global capitalism, financing infrastructure, wars, and innovation while also enabling fraud and crises. He remains relatively relaxed about US debt (about 3.5% of GDP in interest costs) but warns that increasingly debt-financed AI spending echoes past credit-fueled bubbles.

## Key points
- Venice in 1171 issued one of the first transferable government bonds: a forced war loan with 5% interest and tradable receipts. After losing the war, the debt became perpetual, and Venetians discovered bonds could serve as income streams and collateral, sparking wider use across Italian city-states.
- Dutch water boards in the 1600s used bonds to finance dikes and flood control. Some perpetual bonds from that era still pay interest today, including one from 1624 owned by the New York Stock Exchange that pays €13 annually, demonstrating the longevity of bond financing.
- Britain's consolidated annuities (consols) allowed the country to finance the Napoleonic Wars at low cost. Historian Thomas Babington Macaulay called the national debt a 'fabulous debt,' arguing that instead of ruining the nation, it increased liquidity, provided collateral, and strengthened the economy.
- Alexander Hamilton's 1790 debt assumption and creation of the US Treasury market restored American credit. This enabled the Louisiana Purchase, where Barings and Hope & Co underwrote $15 million in bonds, doubling US territory and cementing Hamilton's legacy as a financial founding father.
- The Rothschild family, led by Nathan Meyer Rothschild, pioneered the first truly international bond market by issuing bearer bonds that could be redeemed in multiple European cities. They also pressured governments to improve transparency, laying groundwork for modern sovereign debt markets.
- Jay Cooke was a brilliant financier who mass-marketed US Civil War bonds to ordinary citizens, helping the North win. He later overextended in Northern Pacific Railway bonds and his bank's failure in 1873 triggered the Panic of 1873, known then as the Great Depression.
- Railroad bonds financed the transcontinental railroad, creating a mania and subsequent bust. The Panic of 1873 shows credit-fueled infrastructure bubbles can cause severe economic damage, but the underlying assets (railroads) often provide long-term societal benefit.
- Lewis Tappan, a prominent abolitionist, founded the Mercantile Agency, an early credit reporting firm that evolved into modern credit ratings. John Moody later created letter grades, providing a useful shorthand for credit risk, though rating agencies have faced conflicts of interest.
- Louis Ranieri pioneered mortgage securitization at Salomon Brothers, which enabled the liquid 30-year fixed-rate mortgage. Despite the 2008 crisis tarnishing securitization's reputation, Wigglesworth argues it remains a valuable financial innovation when used responsibly.
- During the 2008 crisis, actual losses on rated RMBS and CMBS were less severe than the price collapse suggested, but the episode exposed rating agency conflicts and damaged trust. Investors now treat ratings as a starting point rather than a definitive measure.
- Global debt now stands at approximately $370 trillion. US federal debt has doubled in the last decade to about $40 trillion, but interest costs are around 3.5% of GDP—below the 5% level historically associated with trouble (e.g., Canada's 1995 debt crisis).
- Debt scolds often draw a false analogy between government and household debt. Macaulay argued over 200 years ago that a society in debt to itself is different, and Wigglesworth notes the US has significant capacity to tax and grow its way out of current debt levels.
- The AI boom is increasingly debt-financed: hyperscalers issued about $500 billion in bonds in 2025 after $200 billion the prior year, with growing off-balance-sheet liabilities. This echoes past credit-driven capex cycles like railroads and cable, which ended badly economically but eventually benefited society.
- Despite concerns about dollar dominance, Wigglesworth sees strong inertia and no viable alternative to US Treasuries. Euro, Japanese, and Chinese markets lack the depth and trust needed to replace the dollar, so talk of rapid de-dollarization is overstated.
