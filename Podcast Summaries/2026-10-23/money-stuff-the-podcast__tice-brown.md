# Tice Brown — Money Stuff: The Podcast

## TL;DR
Matt Levine interviews Tice Brown, founder of Woodmont Partners, a family office that invests in unconventional assets. Brown discusses his Bitcoin treasury company PIPE investment turned activist campaign, including his email to Levine about liquidating the company and the mechanics of MNAV and non-cross-collateralized leverage. He also explains his quantitative art dealing, defeasance of $200 million in distressed real estate bonds, and his start with $8,000 borrowing from Arkansas banks.

## Key points
- Tice Brown runs Woodmont Partners, a family office organized as single-member LLCs, with capital allocated mainly to real estate across 12 states, plus art, public market investments, and other 'weird assets'.
- Brown describes the boom in Bitcoin treasury companies (DATs): small public shells raised cash through PIPEs to buy crypto and often traded at large premiums to net asset value, with MicroStrategy being the most famous example.
- Brown invested in a PIPE for a Bitcoin treasury company, writing a couple million dollar check; he later became an activist after the stock traded at a discount to book value, emailing Matt Levine about liquidating it.
- He explains MNAV versus price-to-book: a company with $1 billion of Bitcoin and no debt at 96% MNAV trades at $960 million, but the same MNAV with $900 million of debt implies 60% price-to-book, creating levered upside if Bitcoin recovers.
- Brown initially hedged his Bitcoin treasury position with short Bitcoin when Bitcoin was around $117,000, then removed the short as Bitcoin fell and bought more stock to obtain non-cross-collateralized leverage on Bitcoin.
- The company later pivoted toward AI/data centers, reducing Bitcoin correlation, which Brown did not want; he had hoped buybacks would close the discount and increase NAV per share.
- Brown says the trade is the reverse of MicroStrategy's 'accretive dilution': buying back stock at a discount increases per-share NAV, analogous to selling shares at a premium.
- He also invests in art through an art dealership, buying at auction or estate sales and using quantitative analysis (e.g., price per square inch) to exploit wide bid-ask spreads; he cites buying and selling a seven-figure artwork in the same year for 6-7x return.
- Brown defeased $200 million of 'weirdo bonds' across six real estate situations, replacing mortgage obligations with Treasury securities to release properties, earning returns from complexity and low competition.
- He describes buying non-cash-flowing real estate equity from estate sales at discounts, defeasing loans, and dealing with messy issues like a tenant takeover by an MS-13 affiliate, earning about 5x on a six-figure investment.
- Brown started with $8,000 and borrowed from small Arkansas banks that offered 90-97% LTV loans on 'busted weird real estate'; he attributes this to Arkansas's entrepreneurial lending culture and his Harvard credentials.
- He contrasts Berkshire Hathaway's Nebraska insurance float, which can invest in equities, with Mark Walter and Todd Boley structures that use annuity liabilities and loans; he notes the capital-charge games with short-term related-party loans.
- Matt and Tice discuss the EA (Electronic Arts) bond defeasance fight: EA issued low-rate 30-year bonds, got LBO'd, bondholders want 101 put, but EA defeased instead; Matt's gut is EA likely wins.
- On SpaceX/Tesla merger, Matt says having one company reduces conflicts over AI capital allocation compared with two, but acknowledges SolarCity precedent and need for a special committee.
- On QQQ valuation, Tice notes the P/E has fallen about 40% since July because earnings rose sharply, comparing it to a crash in valuation multiple; Matt cautions that some earnings are from valuation gains on holdings and CapEx borrowings.
