# The Forgotten Godmother of Economic Literature — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Odd Lots discusses Maria Edgeworth, a best-selling novelist of the late 1700s and early 1800s who outsold Jane Austen but is largely forgotten today. Guest Gina Smilak argues Edgeworth was the first woman to write about economics, translating Adam Smith's ideas into fiction and corresponding with David Ricardo and Thomas Malthus. The episode explores how her pedagogical style and the later mathematization of economics erased her from history, while her ideas—such as utility-based value and crisis intervention—remain relevant.

## Key points
- Maria Edgeworth was a prolific Anglo-Irish novelist active from 1795 to about 1815; in her lifetime she outsold Jane Austen, was called 'the lion of the season' by Lord Byron, and was read by British royals and across the British Empire.
- Edgeworth wrote in the immediate wake of Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) and had a front-row seat to early economics because her father was friends with Smith's circle; she aimed to translate new economic ideas for ordinary readers, especially in the context of the Industrial Revolution and post-revolutionary democratic ferment.
- Her novel Castle Rackrent borrows its title directly from Smith's concept of 'rack rent'—a rent too high to allow tenant profit—and applies Smithian ideas about rents, wages, and worker incentives to practical Irish estate management.
- Edgeworth maintained a lively correspondence with David Ricardo, in which she pushed comparative advantage to its extreme by suggesting Ireland specialize entirely in potatoes; Ricardo responded that such specialization was risky due to potato crop failure, complicating his own published theory, but this nuance never entered the economic canon.
- She was an early critic of the labor theory of value, arguing through characters like a lawyer in Castle Rackrent that non-physical, service-oriented labor also creates value; this anticipated the later marginalist utility theory of value that became central to modern economics.
- Edgeworth's views evolved after witnessing the Irish potato famines: she moved from strict laissez-faire opposition to poor relief toward endorsing government spending and handouts during extreme crises, foreshadowing Keynesian ideas by about 80 years.
- She published some works anonymously and sometimes allowed male relatives to take credit for her more academic economic essays; after a man falsely claimed authorship of Castle Rackrent, she insisted on publishing under her own name for fiction but still hid her economic essays.
- Her legacy was further erased by the professionalization of economics in the mid-1800s, when the Political Economy Club excluded women and economists adopted mathematical methods; her nephew F.Y. Edgeworth, inventor of the Edgeworth box, was part of this marginalist revolution.
- Edgeworth's pedagogical style—embedding economic lessons in fiction—was initially popular but later contributed to her obscurity as literary tastes shifted toward more naturalistic storytelling like Jane Austen's, though she remained influential in Ireland.
- The episode draws parallels between the early Industrial Revolution and today's AI-driven economic flux, suggesting Edgeworth's mission to educate people for economic flexibility and understanding remains relevant.
