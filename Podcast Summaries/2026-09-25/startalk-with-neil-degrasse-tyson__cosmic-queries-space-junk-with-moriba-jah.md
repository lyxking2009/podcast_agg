# Cosmic Queries – Space Junk! with Moriba Jah — StarTalk with Neil deGrasse Tyson

## TL;DR

Neil deGrasse Tyson and Chuck Nice put audience questions to astrodynamicist and space environmentalist Moriba Jah on the fast-growing problem of orbital debris: who owns it, who is liable when it falls, whether space traffic laws can be written in time, and why the junk itself may be the best sensor for space weather.

## Key points

- Moriba Jah’s career origin story: born in San Francisco to a Haitian mother and Sierra Leonean father, raised in Venezuela, military school, then four years guarding nuclear missiles in Montana — where he noticed dots crossing the sky that vanished mid-pass and learned they were human-made objects reflecting sunlight before entering Earth’s shadow. Tyrannosaurus: that curiosity became the field of astrodynamics.
- Defining the problem: after Sputnik in 1957 the need for a space environmentalist began to grow — Tyson jokes that it is ‘a bad thing that we have to have you’, and Jah agrees, comparing it to the modern social media manager role.
- The scale question the episode poses: how much stuff and how many satellites are really up there, how many objects are in low Earth orbit, who is responsible for them, and what happens to parts that fall out of orbit.
- Falling debris and liability: the discussion covers government space projects’ debris, who answers for debris that lands, and whether the US is guilty of it — plus the idea of a ‘great junkyard in the sky’ parked at a Lagrange point.
- Collision risk and regulation: satellites colliding with each other is treated as a live risk, and the episode lands on the absence of binding space-traffic law as the core governance gap entering a new era of launch volume.
- Light pollution: the conversation covers how orbital objects brighten the sky and degrade astronomy for both hobbyists and researchers, and whether stargazing becomes materially harder.
- The ‘junk as sensor’ proposal: Jah argues space junk can be used as a proxy for space weather the way weather balloons and buoys track Earth weather — there are four things that motivate motion in space (gravity, and everything else: radiation, electromagnetic effects, particle bombardment), and by following where the junk goes one can invert the problem to infer space weather. He notes nobody has funded it yet.
- Careers and citizen science: he calls for amateurs to keep contributing (historically comets), notes there are more amateur than professional astronomers, and points to coordinated asteroid occultation campaigns — lining observers up on Earth to watch a star dim and thereby size an asteroid — as work amateurs can still do.
- On the James Webb / Big Bang clickbait: Tyson argues the Big Bang is supported by so many independent lines of evidence that mismatched galaxy models mean the galaxy models — not the Big Bang — need revision, and calls the framing ‘a very different clickbait’ from ‘we need to rethink the Big Bang’.
- He is also worried about Vera Rubin Observatory data being contaminated by satellite constellations while searching for asteroids — ‘every one of Elon Musk’s satellites coming across’.
