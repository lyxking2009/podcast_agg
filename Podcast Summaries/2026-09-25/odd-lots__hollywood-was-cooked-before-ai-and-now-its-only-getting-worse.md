# Hollywood Was Cooked Before AI, and Now It's Only Getting Worse — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

Odd Lots’ first-ever live Los Angeles show, recorded at the Vermont Theater, with writer Hayes Davenport (Eastbound and Down, Family Guy; co-host of Hollywood Handbook) on the collapse of LA production — shoot days have roughly halved since 2016 — and why AI is arriving on top of a business that was already structurally broken.

## Key points

- The metric that matters is LA shoot days: about 40,000 in 2016, levelling to ~37,000 by 2019, collapsing during the pandemic, recovering to ~37,000 in 2022, then 25,000 in the 2023 strike year, 23,000 the year after, and under 20,000 last year — roughly half the 2016 peak.
- The industry’s mantra in LA was ‘survive till ’25’ — and shoot days fell again anyway, which is why Davenport treats the AI threat as landing on an already-wounded business rather than as the original cause.
- The 2016–2019 decline had its own cause: the streaming build-out (Netflix, Amazon) offset by the simultaneous shrinking of network and cable production. Davenport’s first jobs were Comedy Central shows; ‘Comedy Central does not really do new shows anymore’.
- Writers Guild membership grew sharply in the late 2010s as new streaming shows brought newcomers in — and it is precisely those newcomers who now face a market with half the production, so people are moving home and taking survival jobs.
- The ‘race to the bottom’ on production incentives is explicit: Eastbound and Down shot in North Carolina until the governor ended the tax break, then the team moved to South Carolina (Vice Principals, then Righteous Gemstones). Pilots shoot in Vancouver; Fox game shows and single-cam comedies now shoot in Dublin, Ireland, flying in American hosts and contestants.
- LA itself has abundant studio space built for the 2016 peak — including the large complex on Santa Monica and the 101 financed around 2019 — which now faces weak demand, creating problems in the studio real-estate economy.
- On where work is going: the ‘Mike White model’ (one writer creating a prestige show alone, as with The White Lotus) is cheaper and increasingly preferred by studios, while rooms shrink — what used to be a seven-writer break-off room on Family Guy is now a large room in absolute terms.
- Post-strike rules changed the calculus: if a room is triggered it must be a certain size for a certain number of weeks, which pushes studios toward mini-rooms and single-writer models.
- Davenport frames the influencer/creator economy as part of LA’s economy but not its industry — it imports subscriber money from around the world into the local economy, yet LA does not ‘own’ it the way it owned the global export of its movie imagery.
