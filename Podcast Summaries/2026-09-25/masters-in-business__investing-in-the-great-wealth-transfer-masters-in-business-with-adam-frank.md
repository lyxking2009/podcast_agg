# Investing In The Great Wealth Transfer: Masters in Business with Adam Frank — Masters in Business

## TL;DR

Barry Ritholtz's guest is Adam Frank, head of wealth planning and advice at J.P. Morgan, whose team oversees about $1.4 trillion and whose combined firm asset-and-wealth business runs over $7 trillion. Frank's argument is that the wealth management industry still over-weights relative investment performance while under-weighting the things that actually determine outcomes — purpose, estate and transfer-tax planning, succession and family dynamics. He reframes the "great wealth transfer" less as a $50–75 trillion event and more as a relationship problem: if an advisor doesn't know the clients' children, the money leaves when the parents die.

## Key points

- **Career path.** Psychology BA at Penn, JD at Yale, clerked for Judge Jacob Mishler in the Eastern District of New York (with a rotation into the Southern District of Florida), then Sullivan & Cromwell and Schulte Roth & Zabel focused on high-net-worth clients and closely held businesses, running estate litigation — "families fighting over who mom loved best." He was hired into Bear Stearns in 2001 to build its wealth planning group and moved to J.P. Morgan when JPM acquired Bear in March 2008.
- **The planner's skill set.** The team is made up of practising trust-and-estate and tax lawyers, CFP professionals and CPAs — people who can talk to wealthy clients about something other than the next stock or bond idea: their kids, their goals, their estate documents, their ownership structure.
- **Measured culture change at Bear.** Fee-based revenue was about 3% of private-client net revenue when he arrived in early 2001 and about 37% by the end of 2006 — driven by hiring planners and changing how advisors interacted with clients, not by a product launch.
- **The industry's blind spot is purpose, not product.** Relative performance versus an index is what the industry optimises and what regulation pushes advisors toward. Frank's counter-question: why do you need to beat the S&P? Once clients see that their stated goals (retiring well, three homes, kids' education, grandkids, philanthropy) are funded by a plan far below their maximum return, the pressure to chase returns releases.
- **Taxes move the needle more than 50 basis points of alpha.** Capital gains at ~24%, tax transition management for low-basis concentrated positions, loss harvesting, direct indexing, asset location (high-turnover strategies into retirement accounts), and muni-versus-ordinary-income choices. Frank frames the advisory fee as an overall advisory fee, not an investment-management fee.
- **Transfer-tax exemptions are state-level traps for the "not really rich."** The federal lifetime exclusion (~$30M for a couple, portable between spouses) means many families assume no exposure — but state exemptions bite far lower: New York $7.35M (and not portable), Massachusetts ~$2M, Oregon $1M, Illinois ~$4M. Retitling three accounts can save seven figures in state tax with no change to the client's life.
- **QSBS is systematically missed.** Qualified small business stock can exempt up to $10M of capital gain; Frank notes many CPAs never raise it. One worked example: $2.38M of tax saved.
- **Concentrated risk is the hard conversation.** He cites the Nifty Fifty, GE, Kodak, IBM and AT&T — and Microsoft through the 1999–2000 tech wreck — where employees paid tax at exercise or vesting and then watched the stock fall below the tax bill. Most entrepreneurs and C-suite executives believe their own company is the exception; some recognise the concentration and want out but are constrained by 10b5-1 plans, investor perception and board optics.
- **Preservation is a different mind-set from accumulation.** Owner-operators are wired to take risks to grow; the shift to preserving and eventually spending capital is difficult, and he says the best time for a plan is a few years before the event, the second best time is now. "It's not what you get, it's what you keep."
- **Bear Stearns, firsthand.** Frank was at 383 Madison during the March 2008 auction weekend — Chris Flowers and J.P. Morgan in the data room, an all-hands call with Jamie Dimon and Bill Harrison on Sunday night, employees wheeling boxes out on Aeron chairs, and a client who came in the Friday before to *move accounts to* Bear. He sold his own Bear stock at $3.79 while the deal price was $10. His stated lesson: diversification preserves wealth even though it does not build it, and relationships — not returns — kept clients after the collapse.
- **The great wealth transfer, reframed.** Transfer is horizontal first (spouse to spouse), then down. A large share will be consumed by healthcare, including at-home care. The bull market of the last ~13 years concentrated a lot of wealth in older people. The practical implication is unchanged: advisors who don't build G2/G3 relationships lose the assets to a self-directed platform or a competitor the heirs already know — which argues for multi-generational advisory teams.
- **Inter vivos gifting is growing but starts small.** Annual-exclusion gifts (~$19,000 per recipient, so ~$38,000 per couple) to kids, spouses and grandkids let clients watch portfolios keep growing while they begin giving. Frank says shift into larger gifts only after the client has seen that work for a couple of years.
- **Long-horizon planning against a short-horizon tax code.** He advises planning for the tax code you have, building flexibility into trusts (who gets the money, when, who can give it), reviewing the plan every three to five years, and checking it on any major life event — and stresses that documents fail less often than people do: trustees and heirs have to want the job, be capable of it, and share the client's values around money.
