Professor Brett Scharffs argues that human dignity is the universal foundation for building cultures of peace, transcending cultural and ideological divides. Drawing on his recent address to the UN Human Rights Council, he explains how dignity underpins human rights, religious freedom, and peaceful pluralism, and discusses how it can guide technology, institutional inclusion of religious actors, and the virtues necessary to sustain peace.

## Key points
- Brett Scharffs was invited to evaluate a unanimous UN Human Rights Council resolution on building cultures of peace, marking a rare consensus across diverse member states.
- Scharffs' work on religious freedom revealed that human dignity serves as a shared, bridge-building concept across different legal and cultural systems, enabling dialogue on rights and pluralism.
- Peacemaking, as Scharffs defines it, requires active cultivation of relationships and institutions that honor dignity, differing from merely preventing conflict.
- The UN resolution on cultures of peace emphasizes proactive measures—education, dialogue, and cooperation—to embed peace in societal structures, not just ceasefires.
- The Punta del Este Declaration, issued in 2023 by over 80 human rights experts, traces the Universal Declaration of Human Rights back to Eleanor Roosevelt’s framing of inherent dignity as the moral anchor that made cross-cultural agreement possible in 1948.
- Scharffs applies a dignity framework to AI, distinguishing between dignity-enhancing uses (e.g., assistive tech for disabilities) and dignity-degrading uses (e.g., deepfakes that humiliate or manipulate), urging ethical design.
- A culture of peace requires virtues of the head (wisdom, understanding), heart (compassion, empathy), and hand (practical skills for nonviolent resolution), all rooted in recognizing the other’s dignity.
- Cultures of peace are undermined by leaders who benefit from division, fear, and dehumanization, as well as by systemic neglect of moral and spiritual dimensions.
- Secular institutions often sideline religious actors in peace processes, but Scharffs argues this is a mistake because religious communities possess moral authority, grassroots networks, and a deep stake in reconciliation.
- Scharffs closes with a hopeful call to action: no one is powerless to promote dignity in their daily interactions, and his optimism stems from witnessing countless individuals and communities choosing peace despite systemic obstacles.
