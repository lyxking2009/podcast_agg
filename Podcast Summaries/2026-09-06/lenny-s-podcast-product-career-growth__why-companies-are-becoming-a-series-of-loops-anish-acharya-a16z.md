# Why companies are becoming a series of loops | Anish Acharya (a16z) — Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth

## TL;DR
Anish Acharya, general partner at a16z, argues that fears of a "permanent underclass" from AI are overblown; the technology is widely distributed, and many jobs are not intelligence-bound. He explains how companies will increasingly be organized as cascading loops of AI agents, with humans providing intuition for breakthrough ideas, and highlights major consumer opportunities around happiness, connection, and entertainment. He advises product people to build and ship more using AI models.

## Key points
- Anish dismisses the 'permanent underclass' meme as a Silicon Valley dark fantasy, noting that by almost every measure opportunities, technology access, and ambition have never been better. The previous era of network effects led to centralization, while current AI stack has many players across labs, open-weight models, and coding agents like Claude Code, Codex, Lovable, Replit, and Wobby.
- Empirical data contradicts job loss fears: radiologists were predicted to be obsolete for 20 years, yet job postings remain high, and programmer jobs are also strong. Anish says recursive self-improvement (RSI) is often misused; what is happening is autocatalytic effects, using new tech to improve processes, not true recursive improvement. Fast takeoff is unlikely because economic diffusion is slow and many problems are not intelligence-bound (e.g., Domino's or FedEx with a data center of PhDs would not become exponentially dominating).
- Inside companies, Anish finds employees more excited than worried; example: Kavak, a used-car company in Mexico, runs a 'Jedi Academy' teaching all employees including mechanics to build AI agents, culminating in shipping a real production agent. The big change is using AI vs reorganizing the company; electricity took 40 years to reorganize factories. CEOs like Sundar Pichai want to build bigger companies, not layoffs; a Google executive told Anish road maps that used to take 2 years now take 3 months, and the hardest problem is deciding what to add.
- Anish introduces the concept of 'loops': after prompts and agents, loops are sets of agents performing tasks. Coding is the best example because of high-quality models and established loops (bug report → repro → fix → review → ship). Business loops across marketing, sales, support, legal will cascade from per-person to company-wide. Humans remain critical for out-of-distribution thinking and jumping to the next hill, because the loop climbs to a local maxima then plateaus.
- In a growth team, instead of brainstorming and prioritizing experiments, every variant can be generated and measured automatically; statistically significant winners are merged and shipped, then a long-term holdout continues. The loop climbs to local maxima, and human intuition is needed to land at the base of the next hill. This pattern repeats across company functions.
- Go-to-market teams are using Codex more than engineering teams; they prefer to focus on core strengths like relationship building while AI handles admin. PMs can say yes to every idea and simulate outcomes with tools like Simily, shifting from prioritization to experimentation. This may reveal that many PMs aren't actually zero-to-one thinkers but can find fulfillment working on others' good ideas.
- As verifiable tasks become automated, rate-limiting factors shift to non-verifiable skills like judgment and creativity. When a model gets stuck, ask what you know that it doesn't; Kavak's agent per customer calls a human when stuck, the human coaches it, and the agent captures traces to learn for next time. This turns exceptions into training data.
- Competitive dynamics may not shift radically: industries like pizza chains will all adopt AI, so relative positions may remain stable because they are not intelligence-bound. Founders should ask: if models were infinitely intelligent and astonishingly cheap, how would we reorganize the company?
- There is a split between frontier and open-weight models based on upside. Frontier models are irrationally priced (e.g., paying 100x more for one IQ point) but rational for unbounded upside like drug discovery. Bounded upside jobs like legal or finance should use Pareto-efficient open-weight models. Companies will use both architectures.
- Anish practices 'model sommelier' by shipping something with every new model. Qwen 38B excels at long-horizon creative tasks (he made Star Wars documentaries), while GLM 53 is neurotic and precise for product work. He recommends building silly, unimportant projects as a chassis to test models, because building is the new reading.
- The big consumer opportunity is 'loop make me happier': people want to spend time, not save time. Basic needs include connection, love, progress, and fun. We have built technology for better spreadsheets but not to extend the soul. This is a product design challenge, not a model capability issue. Three areas: coding agents (e.g., Wabi), personal agents (e.g., Grok Bot, ChatGPT work, Instinct), and entertainment/companionship (e.g., Suno, companion apps). Early days; challenges include expensive models, chat interface not suitable for mass market, and over-focus on productivity.
- AI amplifies agency and identity by unbundling skill from desire—you can make music without knowing piano or make software without programming. This could drive GDP growth from 2% to 10-20% and increase ambition. Making important things cheap, like healthcare and education, will improve AI's PR; education faces the strongest competition in 200 years, and status can be unbundled from credentials.
- On OpenAI slowing development, Anish is skeptical of the 'too dangerous to release' narrative; confounding factors include marketing, GPU shortage, and desire to keep proprietary advantage. He does take offensive cyber seriously but thinks industry trends show no single runaway winner.
- Moats are often discovered, not designed (Jesse from Decagon). Cursor was criticized for lacking a moat but captured reasoning traces and trained its own models. Classic moats—network effects, scale, brand, proprietary data—still apply. Distribution has become harder because platforms guard their networks, so word of mouth is key; nobody has a growth problem, they have a product problem.
- Expensive consumer software is a new opportunity: think of the Birkin bag version of a product at $1,000/month. Anish's investing philosophy has inverted: old days too-ambitious ideas were rejected, now too-small ideas are not engaged; $100m seeds can be productive.
- Advice to product people: make more things, ship once a week, use models as a chassis to learn. He built a Mother's Day slide deck from text messages and photos, which brought joy. Just ship something, even if unimportant, and share it.
