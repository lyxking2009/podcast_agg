# Talk Your Book: Why Rising Rates Won't Hurt You Anymore

**Show:** Animal Spirits Podcast
**Date:** 2026-06-01
**Duration:** ~32 min

## Key Points

- Interest rates are the dominant driver of municipal bond price volatility, with credit risk playing a secondary role; the muni market follows treasury rates but with a lag rather than tick for tick.
- Rising yields should be reframed as an opportunity rather than a threat — with 10-year treasuries near 4.58% and 30-year bonds above 5%, fixed-income investors now have meaningful income after years of near-zero rates.
- The claim that "muni bonds are only for high-income investors" is a misconception; on an after-tax basis, munis can be attractive for any investor paying income taxes, especially in longer maturities.
- All U.S. states except Vermont are required to maintain balanced budgets, providing a structural fiscal discipline advantage over many other sovereign debt issuers.
- Municipal debt is structured to amortize in line with the useful life of the underlying asset (typically 30 years), avoiding the refinancing crises and maturity walls that have troubled other government debt markets.
- Tax loss harvesting is especially powerful in the muni market through separately managed accounts (SMAs), because the diversity of bond attributes (issuer, coupon, maturity) allows investors to capture losses while sidestepping the 30-day wash-sale rule.
- Tax-equivalent yield comparisons favor municipals for higher-bracket investors, and the after-tax advantage grows in longer maturities relative to comparable corporate bonds.
- Franklin Templeton offers muni exposure via three wrappers — ETFs, open-end mutual funds, and SMAs — with SMAs providing the most tailored tax management.

## Overview

This episode of Animal Spirits features hosts Michael Batnick and Ben Carlson in conversation with Ben Barber, Director of the Municipal Bond Department at Franklin Templeton. The discussion centers on how to think about municipal bonds in a higher interest rate environment and challenges several common assumptions investors hold about the asset class. Barber explains that while the muni market broadly tracks the treasury market, it does so with a lag and not one-for-one, and he identifies interest rates — not credit risk — as the primary driver of bond price volatility.

A central theme is reframing rising rates as an opportunity. After years of near-zero yields that starved income-seeking investors, current yield levels on 10-year and 30-year treasuries represent a meaningful entry point for fixed income. Barber argues that higher yields create a more attractive setup for new buyers, and that investors who avoided bonds during the low-rate era should reconsider their exposure now that income is available again.

The episode also dismantles the perception that municipal bonds are a niche product only relevant to the ultra-wealthy. Barber makes the case that on an after-tax basis, munis are competitive — and often superior — for any taxpaying investor, particularly at longer maturities where the after-tax yield comparison against corporate bonds tilts in munis' favor. He highlights two structural strengths that make municipal credit fundamentally sound: balanced budget requirements (in 49 of 50 states) and debt amortization schedules tied to the useful life of underlying assets, both of which reduce the systemic risks seen in other sovereign debt markets.

## Implications

The conversation underscores a durable shift in the fixed-income landscape: yields are high enough that bonds are again worth owning for income, not just as a hedge. For investors who have been equity-heavy due to the inadequacy of bond yields in the post-2008 era, the current environment may warrant revisiting allocation. Municipal bonds in particular offer a tax-efficient path to capturing these higher yields, and the structural safeguards Barber describes — balanced budgets and amortizing debt — suggest credit fundamentals remain sound even if rate volatility persists.

The emphasis on separately managed accounts and tax loss harvesting points to a broader trend toward personalized, tax-aware fixed-income investing. As yields remain elevated, the ability to harvest losses across a diverse pool of bonds without triggering wash-sale rules becomes a meaningful source of after-tax alpha. Investors and advisors should watch whether the spread between muni and treasury yields continues to normalize, and whether demand for tax-efficient income vehicles accelerates as higher rates make the asset class more broadly relevant.
