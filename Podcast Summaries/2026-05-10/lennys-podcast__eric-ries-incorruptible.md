# Eric Ries on Building Incorruptible Companies: Protecting What You've Built

## Key Points

- **Only 20% of venture-backed founders remain CEO 3 years after going public due to standard governance structures that prioritize shareholder primacy**
- **The 'harder is easier' principle states that being principled in decision-making creates unexpected rewards through increased trustworthiness**
- **Companies need both ethos (internal alignment and character) and integrity (structural resistance to corruption) to remain mission-aligned**
- **Public Benefit Corporation (PBC) filing is a simple two-page legal document that protects companies from fiduciary duty lawsuits when prioritizing mission over pure profit maximization**
- **Mission guardians (like Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust) are essential structural elements that ensure companies stay aligned with their original purpose**
- **Success becomes a liability because it makes companies targets for acquisition or pressure to prioritize short-term profits over long-term mission**
- **The current shareholder primacy model is only 40 years old, not a natural law of capitalism - historically companies were required to serve beneficial purposes**
- **Mission-driven companies experience organizational 'flow state' with faster decision-making and higher alignment compared to traditional companies**

## Overview

Eric Ries, author of 'The Lean Startup,' discusses his new book 'Incorruptible' which addresses how successful companies lose their way and what founders can do to protect their mission. He explains that company corruption isn't about legal issues but about structural forces that drag organizations toward mediocrity and away from their founding principles. Through stories of companies like Nova Nordisk, Cloudflare, and Anthropic, Ries demonstrates how specific governance structures and cultural practices can create 'incorruptible' companies that resist both internal temptation and external pressure.

## Implications

Founders who don't implement mission-protective governance structures early will likely lose control of their companies within a few years of going public. The conversation suggests that standard venture capital practices and legal structures actually work against long-term company success and founder retention. Companies that implement these protections see better long-term performance, higher employee trust, and more sustainable growth. The rise of AI companies using alternative governance structures indicates a growing recognition that traditional corporate governance is inadequate for managing powerful technologies.
