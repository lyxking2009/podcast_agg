# #36 Normalizing Inequality with G. Cristina Mora and Tianna S. Paschel — The Edge

## TL;DR

UC Berkeley sociologists G. Cristina Mora and Tianna S. Paschel join The Edge to discuss their book Normalizing Inequality: How Californians Make Sense of the Growing Divide, built on 136 hours-long interviews with middle-class Californians in Los Angeles County and the Central Valley. Their finding is that people are not fooled — they can recite the structural causes of inequality — yet they consistently minimise those forces using three identifiable strategies, and in doing so quietly reproduce the conditions they deplore.

## Key points

- Method: over five months, Mora, Paschel and a research team conducted 136 hours-long conversations with residents of Los Angeles County and the Central Valley earning between 80% and 120% of their counties' median income. They then analysed transcripts closely, attending even to pauses and 'ums' as signals of shame or discomfort. The project took eight years.
- This is deliberately qualitative work: there is no shortage of statistics on inequality, so the professors wanted candid interview data, including under-researched perspectives from the Central Valley and attention to how race shapes people's understanding of inequality. They were not testing hypotheses but identifying patterns.
- The motivating puzzle, in Mora's words, came from her children's questions on the drive to school: why do we live in one of the most expensive places on the planet, with so many resources, in such a progressive area, and yet all these people are also on the streets?
- Statistical backdrop: if California were a country it would have the fourth-largest economy in the world, yet its cost-of-living-adjusted poverty rate ties Louisiana's as the highest in the US. One-quarter of the nation's unhoused people live on California's streets and in shelters. It has the largest multiracial population in the country alongside some of its most segregated school districts. The richest 0.1% average about $12.9 million in adjusted gross income against $51,300 for the middle 20% — roughly 250 times as much.
- Strategy one — 'exceptional framing': interviewees were largely aware of the inequality around them and could rattle off structural explanations, yet simultaneously believed they personally could surmount the same steep odds and be upwardly mobile.
- Strategy two — spatial comparison: subjects minimised California's problems by measuring the state against places they believed were worse, such as the economic instability of an immigrant relative's home country or the racism of the Deep South. Even interviewees who admitted they had barely travelled outside California made these comparisons.
- Strategy three — bounded blame, described by the authors as a 'soft way of pathologizing' people in the most marginalised classes as the reproducers of their own conditions. It surfaced especially around homelessness and undocumented immigration, where people would acknowledge stacked odds and then still suggest individual actions, values or constitutions perpetuated the struggle.
- What interviewees omitted is as revealing as what they said: Los Angelenos rarely considered conservative, rural, poorer regions like the Central Valley when describing their state; paeans to multiculturalism frequently mentioned Mexican food but not Black cultural contributions; and non-Asian interviewees answered broad questions about immigration by focusing on Latinx immigrants despite California's substantial Asian diaspora.
- On race: Paschel says she had not anticipated how shushed conversations about race and immigration would be — she recalls an interviewee's voice dropping many decibels merely to say the word 'white' in public. In a separate poll by the professors, only 40% of respondents strongly agreed that racism is alive and well in California.
- On immigration: interviewees praised immigrants as hardworking embodiments of the American dream but would not acknowledge the exploitative labour practices or difficult immigration system confining them to those jobs, and many were ambivalent about undocumented immigration — sympathetic yet worried about criminality or newcomers consuming resources.
- On the future: Central Valley residents were notably more sanguine, expecting their region to inherit the growth they saw elsewhere. Black and Latinx interviewees were gloomier, doubting people like them could remain in California. Predictions about the state's ethnic future split between a multiracial outcome and visions modelled on San Francisco or Silver Lake — comparisons the authors note skew wealthy and white or Asian — with more conservative respondents voicing fears of a 'Latino takeover'.
- The book's conclusion compares California's economic disparities to its earthquakes: residents notice but are not rattled, going about their days as if nothing happened.
- The authors' own positionality is part of the argument: both are women of colour who grew up working-class, and they incorporated their families' stories of striving toward the California dream. Mora writes about her father, who never finished elementary school, and her own path from a majority-Latinx high school in LA County to feeling out of place at Berkeley; Paschel's mother, a prison guard, moved the family from Flint, Michigan through Sacramento, Bakersfield and Fresno into the middle class while still encountering anti-Black racism.
