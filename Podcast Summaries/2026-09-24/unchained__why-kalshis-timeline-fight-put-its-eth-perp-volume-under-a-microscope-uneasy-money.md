# Why Kalshi's Timeline Fight Put Its ETH Perp Volume Under a Microscope: Uneasy Money — Unchained

## TL;DR

Austin Griffith joins Laura Shin and Taylor Monahan as Uneasy Money's new third co-host, and the crypto segment dissects an analyst's allegation that Kalshi's ETH perpetual volume is fake. The panel's conclusion is nuanced: wash trading by Kalshi itself is implausible for a firm making CFTC disclosures, but 'incentivised volume' from hyper-efficient market makers paid per cross is a much more likely and largely legal explanation. The test they land on is whether an unaffiliated user still gets the best fill in the market — if so, the liquidity is real even if the optics are poor.

## Key points

- Setup: Kalshi 'got themselves into timeline warfare' after hiring crypto-native people who argued on X, drawing a data-driven attack from a critic who analysed all publicly available on-chain and API data and alleged wash trading — fake volume in both the perp markets and the prediction markets.
- The headline numbers: the ETH perp showed roughly $3 million in open interest against around half a billion dollars in 24-hour volume. Per CoinDesk's sampling, Kalshi's ether perpetual produced a volume-to-open-interest ratio of about 61 — roughly 61 contracts changing hands for every contract left open — and recurring fixed-dollar trades appeared on 43 of 46 sampled dates since June.
- Griffith's core claim: 'the only possible way that this makes any sense is if it's incentivized volume'. He stresses that not everything has to be wash trading, and that the hardest thing to understand about crypto is that incentivised volume is not the same as fraud.
- Why Kalshi itself is an unlikely culprit: the company is engaged with the CFTC and makes disclosures, so running its own wash-trading bots in a back room while simultaneously informing regulators would be 'completely insane'. Monahan and Griffith both treat that as close to impossible.
- The more plausible mechanism: a negative-rebate or pay-per-volume incentive scheme. Griffith notes that the people who would generate half a billion in volume off three million in open interest are hyper-efficient market makers, whose job is precisely to trade as efficiently as possible — 'if you got two market makers and put them in a room and said hey guys I'll pay you $1 every time you cross each other, those guys are going to rip your face off.'
- The decisive distinction: wash trading versus incentivised liquidity. If the market makers only ever cross each other's orders, stay delta-neutral and post on a fixed millisecond cycle, that is wash trading and the CFTC will not be impressed. If they simply do not care who they trade with and their job is to generate volume with the least capital, that is fine for users because a maker is always on the book to cross a taker.
- The FCoin precedent is used as the tell for manipulation: an order book where every cross mysteriously happened around 150 basis points from mid-market. Here the evidence suggests crossings happen at mid-market, which does not match the manipulation pattern.
- The user-impact test Griffith proposes: incentivised volume is real 'if the impact on an unaffiliated user is they can come to that venue and get a good fill — the best possible fill in the market'. Volume optics become largely irrelevant if an outsider can express a view cheaply.
- The market-quality tension: deeper markets produce more accurate information, but if liquidity is anchored to the status quo rather than to information, an insider's trade moves the price less and the signal-to-noise ratio degrades. The panel notes a sweet spot — enough liquidity that a private hunch does not move the market, but not so much that real information cannot move it.
- Kalshi's public response compared its books against several venues including Binance, Bybit and Crypto.com. The panel finds this self-defeating, joking that if you find yourself on the same side as Crypto.com's market-structure exemplar, something has gone badly wrong. Crypto.com's own reply, in contrast, was judged 'impeccable'.
- On-chain transparency is a partial advantage: with Polymarket you can see the full receipts rather than trusting a company's published API, and the panel notes both books are fairly open. But being on-chain does not preclude wash trading or fake volume — it only removes the excuse that you cannot check.
- Enforcement outlook: the panel expects any genuine wash trading to be easy for the CFTC to identify because the regulator has receipts, and warns that Kalshi has invited scrutiny by picking a fight on the timeline.
