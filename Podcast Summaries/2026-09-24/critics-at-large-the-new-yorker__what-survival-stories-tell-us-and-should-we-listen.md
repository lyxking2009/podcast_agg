# What Survival Stories Tell Us (and Should We Listen?) — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR

Vinson Cunningham, Naomi Fry and Alexandra Schwartz use Brad Pitt's new survival film Heart of the Beast as a hook for a wide-ranging essay on why tales of human resilience in nature remain so persistent. They trace the man-versus-nature formula from Robinson Crusoe through children's literature, 1970s nature writing and reality television, and land on the question the title poses: in a hyper-developed age, why do we keep returning to stripped-down stories of survival, and should we trust what they tell us?

## Key points

- The episode is built around Heart of the Beast (2026), directed by David Ayer and starring Brad Pitt as career soldier James Belmont, who crash-lands in the Alaskan wilderness with a combat K9 named Odin. The dog actor Uber is prominent enough that Pitt reportedly wanted the dog to be number two on the call sheet, with Uber's children Sika, Riker and Hondo subbing in for the fastest runs.
- The panel opens with a self-deprecating thought experiment on what each of them would bring to a survival situation, which sets up the episode's thesis that survival stories are as much about the reader or viewer as about the survivor.
- The formula: 'Something has gone horribly wrong. And here you are needing to make it through and just keep surviving.' Settings range from the desert island to the remote woods to lost at sea.
- Survival texts canvassed: Daniel Defoe's Robinson Crusoe (the ur-text), Robert Zemeckis's Cast Away as its modern retelling, Sophie Elmhirst's bestselling A Marriage at Sea, J.C. Chandor's All Is Lost (with Robert Redford as the only actor), 127 Hours and the real Aron Ralston, The Revenant, Rescue Dawn, Into the Wild, Lost, and the original reality text Survivor — now in its fifty-first season.
- Children's literature is identified as an especially rich vein: Gary Paulsen's Hatchet, Jean Craighead George's My Side of the Mountain, Scott O'Dell's Island of the Blue Dolphins, William Steig's Abel's Island (Robinson Crusoe with a pampered mouse), The Boxcar Children and Lord of the Flies.
- The episode notes reality television has become a huge zone for survivalist obsession and that the genre is going very strong rather than fading.
- Scholarly framing is brought in via Ian Watt's The Rise of the Novel: Studies in Defoe, Richardson and Fielding, tying the rise of the survival narrative to the rise of the novel form itself.
- The reading list also includes Daniel Zalewski's New Yorker piece 'The Ecstatic Truth' and Gilligan's Island as an acknowledged ancestor.
- The recurring tension the hosts circle is why a hyper-developed, comfort-saturated audience keeps returning to stripped-down tales — the thrill of the wild delivered to the comfort of the couch — and what that appetite says about contemporary anxieties.
