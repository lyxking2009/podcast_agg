# AI Is Upending the Lives of People Who Do Social Media Professionally — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

A live Odd Lots show in Los Angeles with Taylor Lorenz and Rachel Carton on how AI is reshaping the creator and social-media-marketing economy. The sharpest finding is organisational rather than technological: AI is arriving in marketing teams through bosses who outsource their own taste to chatbots, while consumers keep punishing brands for visible AI use and happily consume AI 'slop' they do not recognise. The hosts and guests debate whether AI kills how-to creators, why the anti-data-centre and AI-x-risk camps have not merged, and why anthropomorphising AI is the real policy hazard.

## Key points

- Recorded live in Hollywood as Odd Lots' first-ever Los Angeles live show, framed around the influencer/creator economy and 'people with 8 million followers on Instagram that none of us have ever heard of'.
- Rachel Carton (author of the newsletter 'Link in Bio', a trade publication for social teams) says AI is reaching marketing teams mainly through their bosses — she interviewed about a hundred marketers for an article titled 'Does Your Boss Have AI Brain?'
- Carton's examples: a social manager whose hard-won funny meme was run through Claude by the boss to ask 'is this funny?'; another marketer whose boss only buys ideas that came from Copilot, so the marketer mocks ideas up inside the Copilot UI just to get them approved. Her summarising quote from those interviews: 'demoralising as hell'. Her takeaway was not to ban AI, but that AI is being implemented as a free-for-all with no training or guardrails — in contrast to her Cond� Nast days, when onboarding Asana consumed months of formal training.
- Carton on why brands still get punished: consumers read the effort a brand puts into its social output as a proxy for the effort it put into the product. If a familiar fast-food chain uses AI-generated imagery, customers ask what other shortcuts it is taking. Nike is cited as a brand whose world is built on creative investment.
- Taylor Lorenz (tech journalist, founder of UserMag) argues the opposite on consumption: people actively enjoy AI-generated content when they do not know it is AI — YouTubers reading AI scripts draw comments praising the 'amazing analysis'. The line is being tricked, not AI itself. She cites the 'Fruit Love Island' / AI fruit-slop meme: the same person who denounces an AI-generated restaurant menu forwards AI fruit slop to friends because a meme is low-stakes and carries no craft expectation.
- On who survives: Lorenz says how-to content creators are most exposed because AI is simply a better authority for questions like how to fix a dishwasher — the original wave of democratised media (early YouTube makeup and how-to videos) is the directly threatened category. Creators making deeply parasocial, non-replicable commentary about their own lives and opinions keep their value.
- Lorenz's counter to AI doom: 'it cannot even edit a podcast' — the tools fail at concrete production tasks, so the existential timeline is overstated.
- On AI politics: Lorenz calls data centres 'so July', noting political consultant Morris Katz's argument that data centres are a 2026 election-cycle issue rather than 2028. She observes the anti-data-centre camp and the AI-x-risk camp have very different interests and have not coalesced, though she suggests they might.
- Lorenz's central warning is about anthropomorphism — assigning agency to computer programs. She argues it is useful shorthand for describing how systems behave but dangerous in liability and policy debates, because these are not living beings: 'just because something kills us doesn't mean it's smarter than us... a virus can kill a lot of people. It's not smarter than us.'
- On productive AI use: Carton is most interested in agencies using AI to mock up a real-world idea so a boss with 'a lack of imagination' can visualise it — the output is then made for real with human craft. She does not recommend using AI to generate captions or post ideas.
- On attention economics: Carton is not worried about chatbot time cannibalising scrolling time — she thinks chatting with a chatbot is a functionally different activity from DMing a real friend, and that the bigger risk is people wanting to log off. Lorenz predicts the current phone form factor dies relatively soon, with wearables replacing it, and that the screen-time moral panic fades with it.
- Joe Weisenthal presses a counterpoint: social platforms can be hostile (he is told he is an idiot and that people used to like him), while Claude never insults him — a much more pleasant experience that he fears many people will not have the 'iron' to resist.
