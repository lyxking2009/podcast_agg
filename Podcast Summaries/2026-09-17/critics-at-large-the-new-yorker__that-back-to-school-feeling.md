# That Back-to-School Feeling — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR

The hosts of Critics at Large respond to listener voicemails about the back-to-school experience. High school teacher Jesse seeks texts to combat Gen Z apathy; the hosts recommend shock-value novels like Bret Easton Ellis's Less Than Zero and Miriam Toews's A Complicated Kindness, plus teaching classics like The Great Gatsby. New teacher Lily gets suggestions for media depicting teaching's intensity, including The Holdovers and The Class, while poetry student Harry receives poems by Langston Hughes, Ariana Reines, and Louise Glück to persuade poetry skeptics.

## Key points

- Jesse, a high school English and drama teacher in Ontario, reports rampant apathy among Gen Z students and asks for texts to reignite passion for literature.
- Naomi Fry recommends Bret Easton Ellis's 1985 debut novel Less Than Zero (referred to in the episode as 'Lesson Zero') as a shock-and-awe strategy; its depiction of drugs and ambiguous sex can surprise students who think books are boring, but she cautions it may be best for 12th graders or an elective due to content.
- Vinson Cunningham argues that texts requiring a teacher's guidance can create surprise and engagement, citing an English teacher who used 'blue talk' to explain a scene in The Odyssey, making students realize literature can address taboo topics.
- Vinson also recommends Richard Wright's Black Boy for its shocking content and classroom discussion potential, and calls The Great Gatsby the best book to teach 10th grade, full of symbols.
- Alex Schwartz suggests A Complicated Kindness by Canadian author Miriam Toews, a 2004 coming-of-age novel about 16-year-old Nomi in a repressive Mennonite community, praising Toews as a writer 'totally without bullshit' who can engage students through honesty and humor.
- The hosts agree that teacher passion and willingness to be a 'dork' or perform enthusiasm is crucial to model excitement and create a classroom where students feel allowed to be excited.
- Lily, a new teacher, asks for media depicting the highs and lows of teaching; Vinson recommends Black Swan as an allegory for the psychodrama of teaching performance and The Holdovers for its portrayal of emotional connection and care in teaching.
- Alex recommends the 2008 French film The Class (Entre les murs), which won the Palme d'Or and realistically portrays a Parisian teacher's triumphs and serious mistakes with a diverse, underprivileged class.
- Naomi highlights The Simpsons episode with Lisa's substitute teacher Mr. Bergstrom (voiced by Dustin Hoffman) as a touching example of a teacher recognizing a student's potential, giving Lisa a note reading 'You are Lisa Simpson.'
- Vinson references Merve Emre's essay 'Are You My Mother?' on transference and countertransference in the classroom, noting that eros, love, and mutual interest are part of teaching dynamics.
- For Harry, a poetry graduate student who wants to convince friends that poetry is worthwhile, Vinson reads the opening of Langston Hughes's 'Theme for English B,' arguing the poem creates a conversational space where the poet's voice meets the reader despite temporal and racial distance.
- Naomi reads from Ariana Reines's poem 'Lull' (from the 2011 collection Mercury), which features a scene with Joan Didion and explores body image and womanhood, recommending it as accessible, talky, and detail-rich poetry.
- Alex reads 'The Red Poppy' from Louise Glück's The Wild Iris, a poem in the voice of a poppy that contrasts mind and body, showing how poetry can be intellectually demanding and emotionally immediate.
- The episode closes with hosts paying tribute to departing editor Alex Barasch.
