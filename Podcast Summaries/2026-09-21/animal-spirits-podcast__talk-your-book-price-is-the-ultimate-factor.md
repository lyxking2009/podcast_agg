# Talk Your Book: Price Is the Ultimate Factor — Animal Spirits Podcast

## TL;DR

Bill Mann, CIO at Motley Fool Asset Management, joins Michael Batnik and Ben Carlson to discuss momentum investing. Mann argues momentum is underallocated relative to value and explains why price is the ultimate factor, distilling all market information and behavioral biases. He outlines Motley Fool's rules-based passive ETFs that track momentum in high-quality companies, and discusses the challenges of preparing for downturns, including the amplifying effects of AI on herding.

## Key points

- Bill Mann highlights that momentum strategies are significantly underallocated among advisors compared to value strategies, despite both being persistent factors driven by behavioral anomalies like underreaction and overreaction.
- Mann defines momentum as tracking parts of the market showing outsized performance relative to their usual behavior, not simply chasing the hottest stocks, which can lead to overlap with value strategies in certain market environments.
- He argues that price is the ultimate factor because it distills all fundamental analysis, market sentiment, and information flow; stocks gain momentum because companies are performing well and the market is recognizing that, with behavioral biases adding excess.
- Momentum is amplified by faster information spread via social media, AI, and online communities, which may lead to more frequent herding and potentially larger momentum crashes, as suggested by a University of Washington study showing 70 AI models give nearly identical answers.
- Motley Fool Asset Management's momentum ETFs are rules-based passive strategies that rebalance quarterly, focusing on high-quality companies from The Motley Fool's research universe, with no discretionary overrides, helping investors stick to long-term discipline.
- Mann explains the 'Noah principle'—building an arc before the flood—as systematically preparing for market downturns rather than constantly predicting them, because economist predictions have been unreliable (e.g., calling 15 of the last 5 recessions).
- The current market is extremely concentrated, with 44% in technology and energy now effectively a derivative bet on data centers and AI; heavy debt financing in the AI buildout adds risk of defaults even if the AI thesis is ultimately correct.
- Mann expects a transition from tech-heavy momentum to defensive sectors will be painful because correlations tend toward one in crises, but momentum strategies will rotate to stocks showing relative resilience, though they are not designed to avoid losses entirely.
- Momentum strategies are unlevered and typically have lower downside capture than the overall market, but during sharp market declines they still lose money, just less than the market, by shifting to names that are declining the least.
- Michael and Ben note that technological advancements accelerate market cycles and herding, making long-term thinking harder; they advocate rules-based passive strategies as a way to force investors to stay disciplined and avoid overreacting to short-term moves.
