# OpenAI & Meta Distinguished Eng (IC9): The Psychology Behind Tech Career Peaks | Philip Su — The Peterman Pod

## TL;DR

Philip Su, a former Distinguished Engineer at Facebook and OpenAI, reflects on the psychological challenges of reaching the highest levels of a tech career. He shares how promotions initially served as quantifiable success metrics, but the thrill diminished as expectations rose and the nature of work shifted from technical to people-oriented. Philip discusses his decision to take a voluntary demotion from IC9 to IC7, the impact of company growth on generalist skills, and the importance of finding meaning beyond promotions and financial goals, advising listeners to consider the 'day after' achieving their biggest ambitions.

## Key points

- Early in his career, Philip treated promotions as quantifiable achievements akin to grades, driving his ambition up to level 7 at Microsoft, but the thrill faded as each level brought higher expectations and uncertainty about his ability to deliver.
- The nature of work changes significantly at senior IC levels: beyond IC6, the focus shifts from coding and technical execution to team direction, product strategy, influencing other organizations, and dealing with more meetings and people-oriented tasks, which Philip found less enjoyable.
- Philip realized there is an asymptote in career growth; not everyone can continue climbing due to limited opportunities and individual talent limits, and he stopped seeking promotions after level 7, with later promotions to 8 and 9 coming as surprises during his early Facebook days when levels were not even on his radar.
- After reaching IC9, Philip asked for a demotion to IC7 because he was exhausted by the constant pressure and 'Eye of Sauron' feeling, and he recognized that his generalist skills were less valued in a large company like Facebook, where specialists thrived; he spent six months fixing bugs, an area he was not skilled at.
- Philip struggled emotionally with the demotion, feeling wistful when former subordinates rose above him, and he likened the experience to 'getting not famous is horrible,' though he did not begrudge others their success.
- Motivation shifted from chasing promotions to mission-driven work: during the intense six-week sprint to ship the first Facebook video calling feature, he worked from morning to night without regard for levels, driven by direct user impact, small team accountability, and the thrill of building something new.
- Philip advises that career advice is not useless even if ignored initially; it primes you to recognize lessons when consequences occur, and it makes you think more carefully about future decisions, even if you don't follow it immediately.
- Regarding the FIRE movement, Philip notes that personality matters: some people thrive on endless leisure, but he needs ongoing goals and purpose; the excitement lies in pursuing a goal, not in reaching it, and he warns that hitting the 'R' in FIRE can be a dangerous moment for goal-oriented people.
- He recommends the book 'Four Thousand Weeks' by Oliver Burkeman, which frames life as roughly 4,000 weeks and encourages visualizing your biggest goal and then imagining the day after achieving it; if you have no answer, reconsider your goals.
- Philip discovered that regular socialization is crucial for his happiness; work provided mandatory social interaction, and during periods without work, he only truly enjoyed himself when other former coworkers were also between jobs, leading to regular activities like skiing three times a week.
- He regrets some periods of over-availability to his employer at the expense of family, admitting he would have prioritized family more if he could redo his career, even if it might have impacted his career success, noting that the tradeoff would have been worth it.
- Philip observed that higher-level roles require broad impact and constant pressure, which suited his personality less over time; he felt his realistic appraisal of his abilities showed he could not perform at the level of some IC7s he observed, contributing to his decision to step down.
- The growth of Facebook from a small startup to a large company changed his environment: as a generalist, he could no longer do diverse tasks like negotiating contracts or talking to press, and the company needed domain experts, making him feel less useful.
