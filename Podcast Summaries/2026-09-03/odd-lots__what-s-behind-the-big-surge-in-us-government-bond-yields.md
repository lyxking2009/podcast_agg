# What's Behind the Big Surge in US Government Bond Yields — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Darrell Duffie explains the surge in US Treasury yields as primarily a supply-demand imbalance: the Treasury market has grown to $31 trillion with $2 trillion annual deficits, while foreign central banks have stopped buying, forcing domestic investors to demand higher compensation. He argues inflation expectations are not the main driver, and discusses Treasury buybacks—Scott Bessent's expansion is a signaling exercise, not a needle-mover—and the challenges of shrinking the Fed's balance sheet due to banks' appetite for reserves. Key recommendations include shortening Fed asset duration and giving the Fed tools to control its balance sheet.

## Key points
- The surge in bond yields is driven by the sheer volume of government debt relative to GDP, now over 100% for major economies; the US Treasury market grew from about $18 trillion to $31 trillion over the past decade.
- Foreign central banks have essentially stopped buying Treasuries because they already have what they need, shifting the burden to domestic discretionary investors (mutual funds, hedge funds, banks, insurers, pension funds) who require higher yields for additional duration.
- Inflation risk is not the primary driver: forward implied inflation from real/nominal bonds is stable; Duffie cites the fiscal theory of the price level but says supply relative to demand is foremost in investors' minds.
- Hyperscaler debt issuance (projected $1 trillion in the next couple of years) is small compared to the Treasury's $32 trillion and $2 trillion annual deficits; the Treasury is crowding out corporate issuance, not the other way around.
- The Fed is studiously avoiding fiscal dominance and won't engage in yield curve control; the last time ended acrimoniously in the 1950s Fed-Treasury accord, and the Fed wants no repeat.
- Scott Bessent's expansion of Treasury buybacks is more about signaling that yields are too high than about addressing liquidity concerns; the market reaction reversed quickly because Treasury firepower is small relative to the market.
- The original Treasury buyback program was designed to clean up off-the-run, illiquid old notes and bonds clogging dealer balance sheets, improving liquidity and potentially making money for taxpayers (buy low, sell high). Research with NY Fed economists suggests this is effective.
- Bessent's buyback expansion (initial $4-8 billion) is a 'micro twist' and unlikely to move long-end yields; the real action is in new issuance decisions.
- If Treasury shifts more issuance to short-term bills to suppress long-end yields, it increases interest rate expense volatility and rolls over debt more frequently; average maturity is currently about six years, still within norms.
- The Fed's balance sheet reduction is constrained by liabilities: assets and liabilities must shrink together; the only significant reduction possible is reserves, but banks like reserves because they pay market interest, satisfy liquidity regulations, and are the 'Swiss army knife' of finance.
- Reserves exhibit a ratchet effect (as documented by Viral Acharya and Raghu Rajan at Jackson Hole 2017): banks get addicted to holding more reserves and resist giving them up, causing volatility if pressured.
- Kevin Warsh's task force (with Jeremy Stein, Raghu Rajan, and Karen Dinan) is likely to recommend reducing the Fed's long-term Treasury holdings and replacing them with bills to reduce interest expense volatility, and letting MBS roll off.
- Duffie recommends the Fed doesn't necessarily need to shrink its balance sheet, but should develop tools to control it to avoid being cornered by Congress; such tools exist in theory but have not been developed in practice by the Fed.
- Term premium is easily measured but decomposing it into expected short rates vs risk premium is hard; the volume of Treasury issuance elevates the entire curve, more so in the future if fiscal deficits don't decline.
- Bessent's activism mirrors the 1992 Soros attack on the British pound: governments cannot fight bond markets; even the US Treasury cannot set yields if the market decides they should be 6%.
