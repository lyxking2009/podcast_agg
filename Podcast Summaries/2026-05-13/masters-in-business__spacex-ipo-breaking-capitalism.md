# Masters in Business — At The Money: Is SpaceX IPO Breaking Capitalism?

## Key Points

- The IPO market has fundamentally shifted from raising growth capital to providing liquidity for private equity investors. Modern companies stay private longer, capturing most growth in private markets before going public primarily to let insiders cash out.
- SpaceX represents a unique IPO structure as a multi-business conglomerate including Twitter/X, Grok/XAI, Starlink internet service, and near-monopoly on US space launches, yet only floating 5% of shares while getting accelerated entry into major indices.
- Nasdaq 100 changed its rules to fast-track SpaceX inclusion from 6 months to 15 days and artificially inflate its weighting by treating 5% float as 15% for index purposes, creating guaranteed massive buying pressure that distorts price discovery.
- The average IPO has grown from $120 million in the dot-com era (with 30-40% float) to trillion-dollar companies today with minimal float, fundamentally changing the risk-reward profile for public investors who now buy after most growth has been captured.
- Index inclusion rule changes create differentiation between previously similar passive strategies, forcing investors to have opinions about index methodology rather than treating all indexing as equivalent passive investing.
- The guaranteed buying from index funds creates known market distortions, with massive purchasing at market close 15 days post-IPO that gets front-run by traders, undermining natural price discovery mechanisms.
- Future unlock events every 6 months will trigger additional index rebalancing as float increases, creating recurring distortions rather than one-time effects, with the index buying 3x whatever the actual float percentage becomes until reaching 33%.

## Overview

Barry Retolts and Dave Nadig examine how the modern IPO market has fundamentally broken from its original purpose of raising growth capital to become a mechanism for private equity liquidity. The conversation centers on SpaceX's upcoming IPO as a case study of these broader structural problems. Nadig argues that companies now stay private through their high-growth phases, accessing private credit and equity markets, only going public when they need to provide exits for accumulated investors. The discussion reveals how Nasdaq 100 changed its inclusion rules specifically to accommodate SpaceX, allowing accelerated entry and artificial float weighting that guarantees massive index buying. This creates predictable market distortions that undermine price discovery while benefiting insiders. The conversation evolves from diagnosing the problem to exploring solutions, with Nadig proposing stricter rules including one-year trading periods, profitability requirements, and true free-float weighting to restore market integrity.

## Implications

For founders and entrepreneurs, this signals that private markets now offer the primary path to scaling businesses, but public markets have become less attractive for genuine capital raising. Product managers and engineers at public companies should understand that their equity compensation is increasingly subject to index-driven volatility rather than fundamental business performance. Investors need to recognize that passive indexing is no longer uniform - different indices now make very different active bets on IPO timing and weighting, requiring more sophisticated portfolio construction decisions.
