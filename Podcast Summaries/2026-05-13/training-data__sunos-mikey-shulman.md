# Training Data — Suno's Mikey Shulman: Everyone Can Make Music Now

## Key Points

- Suno is a 'consumer creative entertainment platform' where 90% of daily users create rather than just consume — the act of creation itself is the entertainment, paralleling gaming more than streaming.
- Suno models sound waves directly, without any musical priors (no knowledge of notes, instruments, or genres) — this enables entirely new sounds and genre mashups that wouldn't be possible with music-theory-based approaches.
- The company made a contrarian bet on full songs with lyrics over background music or crisp instrumentals — prioritizing narrative and emotional engagement over audio quality, which initially sounded 'like crap' but drove retention.
- Music generation is not a scale problem in the LLM sense — models are relatively small, there are no benchmarks, and user preference data (RL alignment) is the real moat.
- Suno has a $300M revenue run rate, but most people still don't know about it — the product is 'very crude' with 'a lot of room to run.'
- The company partnered with Warner Music rather than fighting labels, co-building products that deepen artist-fan connections — arguing 'digital musical experience has not changed for 25 years.'
- Near-term roadmap: social co-creation, personal voice integration (users singing in their own voice), interactive concerts, and music video generation.
- Mikey Shulman: physics PhD in quantum computing → Kensho → Suno. Co-founders met through jam sessions — initially thought music generation was too hard.

## Overview

Mikey Shulman, CEO and co-founder of Suno, joins the Sequoia Training Data podcast to explain his vision for AI-powered music creation. Shulman makes the case that Suno is not a music company trying to make better songs — it's a 'consumer creative entertainment platform' where the act of making music is itself the product. 90% of Suno's daily users create something, not just listen. Shulman walks through the surprising technical approach: instead of encoding music theory (the 12 Western tones, instrument types, genres), Suno models raw audio waveforms directly. This lets users create sounds that have never existed — trap with sitar, microtonal compositions, country with 808s. The trade-off was choosing full songs with lyrics (harder) over clean instrumental audio — a bet that paid off because people connect with storytelling. The conversation covers Suno's partnership with Warner Music (counterintuitive for a company some assumed would fight labels), the $300M revenue run rate, and the roadmap ahead: personal voice cloning, interactive concerts, and music videos. Shulman also reflects on why few consumer AI founders exist — it's harder to imagine AI in play/creation than in enterprise automation.

## Implications

For founders building consumer AI: Suno proves that the 'creation as entertainment' model works — users don't need a utilitarian reason to engage with AI. For music industry: The Warner partnership shows labels can co-opt AI rather than fight it, but the real disruption is in how music is made and experienced, not distributed. For AI engineers: The absence of traditional benchmarks in creative domains means user preference data is the only moat — invest in feedback loops, not evaluation metrics. For investors: Consumer AI creativity apps have longer runways to product-market fit but potentially larger TAMs than enterprise automation plays.
