# Odd Lots — Samanth Subramanian on the Undersea Cables That Keep the Internet Alive

## Key Points

- Undersea fiber optic cables carry the vast majority of global internet traffic, not satellites, because of the sheer volume of data we consume daily - Netflix streams, Zoom calls, cloud services, and now AI training data. Satellites simply cannot handle this capacity.
- The physical infrastructure of undersea cables remarkably resembles historical shipping lanes, connecting major economic centers. Modern cables follow nearly identical routes to telegraph cables from the 1800s due to geographic constraints and existing infrastructure.
- Cable funding has evolved through three distinct eras: government-owned telecom consortiums (1980s-1990s), private investor-led models (2000s), and now big tech dominance where Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft fund two-thirds of new cables using their own cash reserves.
- Geographic choke points like the Strait of Hormuz and Egyptian cables create massive vulnerabilities. A malicious actor could potentially cripple large portions of the global internet by targeting these bottlenecks, forcing traffic to reroute through much longer paths.
- Cable repair remains surprisingly old-school: ships use grapnel hooks to drag the seafloor hoping to snag broken cables, then splice them together in onboard clean rooms. About 100 cables get cut annually, mostly by accident from ship anchors and fishing boats.
- Big tech's infrastructure ownership creates new power dynamics, especially for developing countries like West African nations that must choose between data privacy demands and internet access when negotiating with companies like Meta for cable landing rights.
- Geopolitical tensions have fractured cable cooperation - no new cables have been laid directly between China and the US in years due to sanctions, forcing traffic through inefficient routes via Philippines and Singapore, potentially leading to internet bifurcation.
- The AI boom has accelerated cable demand just as the industry worried about overcapacity. Military organizations maintain completely separate cable networks that carry no public internet traffic.

## Overview

The conversation opens with hosts Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal marveling at maps of undersea cables that resemble shipping lanes, setting up the central theme about the physical reality behind our digital world. Guest Samanth Subramanian, author of 'The Web Beneath the Waves,' traces his fascination from reading a 1990s Neil Stephenson essay to witnessing Tonga's internet blackout after a volcanic eruption. The discussion evolves from technical aspects - how hair-thin glass fibers carry light pulses across ocean floors - to the economic transformation of who funds these $500 million projects. Subramanian details the shift from government telecom consortiums to private investors to today's big tech dominance, where four American companies control two-thirds of new cable infrastructure. The conversation then explores vulnerabilities: geographic choke points, surprisingly antiquated repair methods, and the geopolitical fracturing of cable cooperation between the US and China. The hosts and guest examine how this infrastructure concentrates power in the hands of tech giants, particularly affecting developing nations' leverage in negotiations, before concluding with the AI boom's acceleration of cable demand and the separate military cable networks that exist parallel to civilian infrastructure.

## Implications

For founders and engineers, this reveals critical infrastructure dependencies that could affect global services and highlights the outsized influence of hyperscalers on internet topology. Product managers should understand that geographic routing decisions are increasingly driven by big tech's cable investments rather than pure efficiency. The concentration of cable ownership in four tech companies creates both opportunities for partnership and risks of dependency. Geopolitical cable restrictions demonstrate how technical infrastructure becomes entangled with foreign policy, potentially affecting service reliability and data routing. The AI boom's impact on cable demand suggests infrastructure bottlenecks could emerge as companies scale AI services globally. For anyone building international services, understanding these physical constraints and ownership dynamics is crucial for reliability planning and geopolitical risk assessment.
