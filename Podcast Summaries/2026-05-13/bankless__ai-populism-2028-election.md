# Bankless — Will AI Populism Decide the 2028 Election? | Jasmine Sun

## Key Points

- AI populism is rapidly rising as a political force in America, ranking 29th out of 39 issues but growing faster than any other concern, as diverse factions unite against AI as an elite political project to be resisted.
- Politicians like Bernie Sanders are betting heavily on AI populism messaging because AI connects to top voter concerns like economy, jobs, and cost of living - it's not separate but tied to these core issues that haven't changed in decades.
- Unlike crypto's failed populist moment, AI has distinct advantages: it's 30-40% of GDP growth, affects most Americans' work lives, has widespread consumer adoption (ChatGPT), and crucially, AI leaders themselves validate the risks by saying jobs will disappear.
- Political violence has emerged targeting AI leaders, including Molotov cocktail and bullet attacks on Sam Altman's home, driven by young, chronically online individuals who believe democratic channels have failed and direct action is their only leverage.
- A dangerous political binary is forming between techno-capitalist accelerationists and broad populist decelerationists, potentially forcing moderates who want guided technology development to pick extreme sides in 2028.
- The job displacement debate centers on whether AI breaks the historical link between labor and humans - while past automation created new jobs for displaced workers, AGI promises to do both cognitive and eventually physical labor without human involvement.
- Jasmine expects 5-10% of easily automatable jobs (software engineering, copywriting, digital marketing) to face near-term disruption, with affected workers likely struggling to retrain, creating lasting political resentment rather than smooth transitions.
- The core issue isn't just wealth inequality but power inequality - when people have no democratic input into AI development that affects their lives, and see billionaires buying political influence, they turn to protests and worse as their only remaining leverage.
- Policy solutions should include longer unemployment insurance (12-24 months vs 6), universal healthcare for more freelancers/entrepreneurs, apprenticeships over broken college system, and potentially shorter work weeks to maintain human purpose and political leverage.
- A grand bargain requires legitimate bargaining channels between AI companies and affected workers, similar to 20th century union negotiations with automation, ensuring productivity gains are shared rather than concentrated among capital owners.

## Overview

This episode explores the rapidly emerging phenomenon of 'AI populism' - a growing political movement that views AI not as neutral technology but as an elite project to be resisted. Jasmine Sun, who writes about AI and politics for The Atlantic and Substack, traces how AI has become the fastest-rising political issue in America, jumping from obscurity to widespread concern as diverse coalitions unite against it. The conversation examines why politicians like Bernie Sanders are heavily investing in anti-AI messaging, connecting it to core voter concerns about jobs, economy, and elite power. Unlike the failed 'anti-crypto army' moment, AI populism has staying power because AI leaders themselves validate the risks, openly discussing mass job displacement. The discussion reveals a troubling trend toward political violence against tech leaders and the formation of a dangerous binary between accelerationists and decelerationists that could dominate 2028 politics. Sun argues this stems from people feeling powerless in a system where they have no democratic input into AI development affecting their lives, while billionaires translate wealth into direct political influence. The conversation concludes with potential policy solutions and the need for new social contracts that ensure AI's benefits are shared rather than concentrated.

## Implications

For founders and engineers, this signals a dramatically shifting political landscape where building AI technology will increasingly be viewed through a political lens rather than purely technical. Product decisions about automation, job displacement, and consumer access will carry political weight. For PMs, understanding AI populism is crucial for anticipating regulatory changes, public sentiment, and market reactions. The emerging binary between accelerationists and decelerationists suggests that taking a moderate 'guided technology' stance may become politically untenable, forcing companies to pick sides. The discussion of violence against AI leaders highlights real security concerns for those building high-profile AI companies. Most critically, the conversation suggests that technical communities need to engage more proactively with distributional questions and democratic governance of AI rather than leaving these issues to politicians operating on their own incentives.
