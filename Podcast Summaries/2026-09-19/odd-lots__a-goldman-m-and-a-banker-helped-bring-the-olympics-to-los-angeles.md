# A Goldman M&A Banker Helped Bring the Olympics to Los Angeles — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal record live on stage at the Future Proof conference in Huntington Beach with Gene Sykes — co-head of global M&A at Goldman Sachs, chair of the US Olympic and Paralympic Committee, and an IOC member — who argues the 2028 LA Games will be the first genuinely privately funded Olympics, and that the current M&A boom is the biggest in history because AI infrastructure demand has replaced private-equity exits as the market's engine.

## Key points

- Sykes wears three hats simultaneously: a Goldman Sachs partner of many years focused on technology, media and healthcare M&A; chair of the board of the US Olympic and Paralympic Committee; and an IOC member. He led LA's original bid for the 2024 Games, which was awarded the 2028 edition.
- Asked what makes a successful Games, Sykes refuses a cost frame and defines success experientially: a Games everyone remembers as an incredible experience they could only have had in one place.
- Scale is the episode's most vivid passage: combining the Olympic and Paralympic Games, LA will host 17,000 athletes and roughly 900 medal events, and sell about 15 million tickets. The 2026 World Cup, by contrast, had 104 matches across 16 host stadiums and sold 6.5 million tickets.
- Sykes also notes a fact he says most people miss: the Olympics is the one major sports property that skews more toward women than men.
- On funding, Sykes says the Games are 'essentially privately funded' — no government money to stage them, with the federal government involved only on security and security logistics, which matters when 206 countries send teams.
- LA does not need to build: the city has the deepest existing sports infrastructure of any host, and athletes will be housed at UCLA rather than in a purpose-built village. Sykes calls it the ideal setting and therefore far easier to plan.
- On cost and infrastructure complaints, Sykes points to 1984: Angelenos predicted traffic chaos that never materialised, and he argues people behave rationally when told in advance what is happening and when. HOV lanes will become Olympic lanes.
- Ticketing will run through a single app, with half the tickets already sold and the next tranche released next year. Sykes does not apologise for high prices, calling them market-priced for a premium, compressed experience.
- Olympic media economics are pre-baked: the IOC sells global media rights years ahead and NBC holds US rights through 2036 at $1.5bn or more per Games. IOC sponsors include Coca-Cola, Visa and Samsung; LA organisers added Starbucks, Uber and Google.
- Sykes highlights the Olympic rule that keeps venues 'clean' — no corporate advertising anywhere in the field of play, unlike the heavily commercialised World Cup.
- M&A: Sykes calls this the biggest M&A year in the history of the world, well beyond the COVID-inflated 2021 peak. The composition has shifted too — private-equity-owned companies were 40% of the market for years and are only 30% this year, because strategic demand tied to AI is so much stronger.
- The same AI-infrastructure phenomenon drives everything: semiconductors, power generation, natural resources, every industry building the plumbing for AI. Boards are biased pro-deal now, a condition Sykes warns will not last.
- On AI startups, Sykes says 80% of founders are building to be acquired, and only the ~20% with something genuinely differentiated become durable independents — using SpaceX as the example of a company that succeeded by defying an incumbent regulatory environment. He expects Anthropic and OpenAI to stay independent because they are simply too big to buy.
- Asked whether an S-1 would have to disclose catastrophic AI risk, Sykes says lawyers will insist on complete disclosure: 'Big, crazy, scary things like that will be present in S1 language for sure.'
- On the dotcom parallel, Sykes concedes nobody knows whether too much infrastructure is being built, but draws a sharp distinction: the 2000-01 build-out was funded by VCs, while today's is funded by the most successful technology platforms in the world, who until this year had hundreds of billions in free cash flow.
- Enterprise AI adoption data point: Goldman's own analysis finds 2% of companies report a positive EPS impact from AI, while perhaps 20-30% have implemented strategies. Sykes describes Goldman itself as full of prompts, with its youngest employees acting as native AI operators.
- On M&A as a craft, Sykes argues AI does not make bankers smarter but faster and more imaginative — more information and better information producing more confident judgement, in a business still built on trusted relationships and synthesis of independent variables.
- On media and AI, Sykes splits clients into two camps: anxious incumbents (music rights holders worried about misuse of seasoned catalogues) and confident creators who see AI as a force multiplier for creativity they could not previously execute.
- He argues back catalogues become more valuable, not less: AI will find more uses for existing content, and familiarity and seasoning retain value because algorithms can match it to audiences better. He credits TikTok's algorithm — built by Zhang Yiming's China teams — as the standout case of a Chinese company creating something the rest of the world genuinely wants.
- The closing riff: 'M&A' is really just 'A' — true mergers of equals are rare. Sykes concedes the M for mergers describes same-size combinations, but says the merger part of the structure never lasts, as leadership and influence consolidate into one set of hands within a year.
