# RWH072: The Making of A Money Master w/ Rob Vinall — The Investor's Podcast (We Study Billionaires)  - The Investor’s Podcast Network

## TL;DR

Rob Vinall, founder of RV Capital, has generated a 15.5% annualized return (cumulative >1,200%) since launching the Business Owner Fund in 2008. His approach centers on buying founder-led businesses with expanding moats at cheap prices, targeting a 15% 'owner return' from earnings growth and cash returns. He discusses his evolution from mechanical value investing to betting on exceptional managers, his recent pivot into Chinese consumer/tech stocks and beaten-down software, and how surviving a 47.6% drawdown in 2022 and a cancer scare reinforced his long-term, purpose-driven investing philosophy.

## Key points

- Rob Vinall grew up in Seaford, a small coastal town in the UK, with no financial background; his grandfather was a passionate stock picker. He attended Christ's Hospital, a means-tested school founded in the 1550s, and later studied modern and medieval languages at Cambridge, where the weekly analytical deep dives into authors and philosophers directly parallel his company analysis process.
- He joined Goldman Sachs Asset Management in 1997 but disliked being a cog in a large machine; he moved to a sell-side telecom analyst role in Frankfurt in 1998. His primary motivation was financial independence, not extreme wealth.
- During the dot-com crash, telecom stocks often fell 99% or to zero, with some trading at a $10M market cap while holding $100M in cash. Vinall began investing a few thousand euros in these net cash bargains, compounding rapidly. Reading Ben Graham's The Intelligent Investor with friends revealed the same phenomenon after the 1929 crash.
- Concentration came naturally to Vinall because he had limited capital and few good ideas, leading to a portfolio of around 10 stocks. When he started the fund, his mentor Norman Rentrop advised him to continue exactly as he had been doing.
- He launched the Business Owner Fund on September 30, 2008, with roughly €7–8 million from about seven investors, working from home with no Bloomberg terminal. The Lehman crisis hit weeks later, but he remained calm because Graham's Mr. Market concept explained the panic, and his home environment insulated him from market hysteria.
- Vinall targets a 15% 'owner return,' defined as the sum of cash returned to owners (dividends/buybacks) and earnings growth, ignoring changes in valuation multiples. He abandoned mechanical P/E and price-to-book metrics after realizing they had little predictive power for business quality.
- His investing evolution moved through three phases: great prices, great businesses, and finally great managers. He now focuses on managers who make the business their life's work, often founders or very long-tenured leaders, because he believes the odds are massively stacked in favor of that small cohort.
- Vinall highlights Warren Buffett's hiring of Ajit Jain partly because he liked him immediately, arguing that visceral liking can be a powerful indicator. He advises investors to cultivate the values they seek in managers, because they will naturally be drawn to people who exhibit those values.
- To screen managers, he first eliminates companies with revolving-door MBA management, then seeks founders or long-tenured leaders, but not all founders are good. He looks for how they think, what motivates them, their long-term plans, and the company's story; he prefers scruffy, introverted, non-charismatic founders and likes to meet them in non-business settings.
- Carvana's Ernie Garcia is a prime example of a founder making the business his life's work. Vinall remains a strong admirer despite Carvana's 98% drawdown in 2022, recalling Garcia's fierce competitiveness in a chin-up contest with Cliff Sosin and his dismissal of a 20% 'Goldilocks' growth rate, saying that CEO wanted to be home by 6 o'clock.
- Vinall's thinking on moats has evolved: he prefers a smaller but expanding moat over a larger but shrinking one, because large moats can breed complacency, especially in rapidly changing industries.
- Since 2024, about one-third of Vinall's portfolio is in Chinese stocks, including Luckin Coffee, Tencent, H-World, and Yum China. He has traveled to China annually for 15 years and after returning in 2023 observed rapid innovation and world-class companies; these businesses are founder-run, have wide moats, and offer earnings growth of at least 10% plus 5% cash returns, hitting his 15% owner-return target.
- Andreas Lechner became co-managing director of RV Capital in 2022 due to Swiss regulatory requirements. A brilliant mathematician and concentrated investor, he and Vinall collaborate on research but make investment decisions independently. Lechner is more skeptical of China, though Vinall says he may be coming around.
- Vinall bought Constellation Software in March 2025 after the software selloff, attracted by its company-wide ownership culture, low stock-based compensation, and high owner earnings. Current CEO Mark Miller started the first company acquired by Constellation's founder Mark Leonard, who stepped back for health reasons but remains a presence.
- He describes the current market as one of the weirdest since the late 90s: the S&P 500 hits new highs driven by a few semiconductor stocks, while many software and internet companies trade 50–60% below their highs. This creates ideal conditions for buying durable businesses with temporary setbacks.
- In 2022 the fund lost 47.6%, partly due to Vinall's own mistakes, and in July he was hospitalized with a stomach tumor initially thought terminal. It turned out treatable, and the experience reinforced his love for investing as being 'in the middle of a movie' he didn't want to leave. He made small changes like deleting pointless emails but kept his broad relationships.
- Vinall hosts an annual gathering in Engelberg, Switzerland, each January for shareholders and others, which has been running for about 15 years. He works alone due to his beta+ personality but values sociability, and sees RV Capital as purpose-driven, helping investors, companies, and fellow investment managers.
- He disagrees with Charlie Munger's comment that wealthy parents must 'learn to fail gracefully' regarding children losing drive. Vinall points to Ernie Garcia and Will Lundeene as examples of wealthy families producing driven, humble, and motivated individuals.
