## Key Points

- AI functions in law two ways: as an efficiency tool (like document automation) and as a "thought partner" — the second is genuinely new and more transformative
- The Jevons Paradox applies to legal services: as AI reduces per-unit costs of legal work, overall demand may rise as previously uneconomical work becomes viable
- A concrete example — due diligence project costs dropped 70% with AI, making work worthwhile that clients previously couldn't justify
- Despite AI productivity gains, hourly rates at top law firms rose ~10% in 2025, significantly outpacing inflation — the value of legal expertise is appreciating even as labor inputs decrease
- Token-based pricing models (replacing hourly billing) remain uncertain but are a likely evolution as AI becomes more embedded
- Junior lawyers will shift from repetitive grunt work to more substantive tasks earlier in their careers, though whether AI simulations can fully replace the learning value of repetitive work is debated
- Lowenstein Sandler uses Harvey (a legal-specific AI platform) alongside Claude; the firm prioritizes security and knowledge management over proprietary model training
- Building proprietary legal AI models is prohibitively expensive — even Kirkland Ellis's investment appears insufficient for independent model development

## Overview

Gary Wingins, Chair of Lowenstein Sandler (a major US law firm), joined Odd Lots hosts to discuss the paradoxical possibility that AI actually increases the total volume of legal work. The central insight is a Jevons Paradox dynamic: AI makes legal services cheaper per unit, which unlocks latent demand from clients who previously couldn't afford legal help. The episode reframes the AI-in-law narrative from "lawyers will lose jobs" to "the total market for legal services may expand significantly." Wingins also addresses firm strategy — using best-in-class commercial AI tools (Harvey, Claude) rather than building proprietary models — and the implications for how junior lawyers develop their skills.

## Implications

- Law firms that use AI to expand market access may grow faster than those that use it merely to cut costs
- Hourly billing may survive longer than expected, but token/outcome-based pricing is coming
- Junior associate training pipelines need redesign — the learning value of repetitive work must be replicated by simulation or mentorship
- Security and knowledge management are the real bottlenecks for AI adoption in law, not the AI capability itself
- Large-scale proprietary legal AI is not viable for most firms — the market will consolidate around platforms like Harvey
