# Bhaskar Sunkara Thinks the Market Has a Place Under Socialism — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Bhaskar Sunkara, co-author of The Blueprint, explains his vision of market socialism, which combines worker-owned cooperatives, public banks, and socialized land/housing with markets for consumer goods. He argues that this model is both more egalitarian and roughly as efficient as capitalism, while avoiding the failures of Soviet central planning. The discussion covers financing new enterprises, benchmark wages, housing policy, education, and the left's internal debates over markets and state intervention.

## Key points
- Sunkara argues that market socialism is technically possible and desirable for egalitarians, countering the historical association of socialism with bread lines. He distinguishes this model from Soviet-style central planning and from social democracy.
- The Blueprint proposes eliminating wage labor by making all firms worker-owned cooperatives. New businesses would obtain loans from public banks, and workers would be member-owners receiving a base wage plus residual profits, with managers elected and pay differentials negotiated.
- To prevent low-road competition based on underpaying workers, the model includes benchmark wages or minimum pay scales by profession, similar to Australia's award system or Nordic centralized bargaining. This encourages investment in labor-saving technology and high-road efficiency.
- Firm failure is explicitly embraced. Sunkara notes that state socialist economies suffered from soft budget constraints because political legitimacy depended on full employment, keeping inefficient firms alive. Market socialism would allow hard budgets and business failures as a sign of economic health.
- All land and housing would be socialized, but rents would still be set by markets. The state would directly commission construction where demand is high, with no zoning or community board barriers, and would smooth rapid rent increases to maintain secure tenure.
- Sunkara argues that public education disparities are rooted in wider social inequality rather than just school quality. A more equal society with full employment and a stronger safety net would improve outcomes, and public sector workers could be paid more in exchange for performance-based firing.
- He contends that social democracy became politically unstable because capitalists retained control over investment; when workers gained power, capitalists could threaten to disinvest. Worker ownership offers a more stable left alternative, as attempted in Sweden's Meidner Plan.
- Sunkara defends consumer choice and individual preferences as compatible with socialism, rejecting the idea of modifying people to eliminate diverse wants. He believes markets are an objective good and a necessary coordinating mechanism.
- On AI and planning, he argues that even superintelligent AI cannot solve the economic calculation problem because wants and trade-offs constantly change and markets provide dynamic incentives. He aligns with Hayek on the calculation debate.
- Asked about antisocial behavior in public housing or transit, Sunkara says public goods must be protected and there is a role for policing and enforcing decorum. He advocates focusing on the person engaging in antisocial behavior, offering state programs rather than purely punitive responses.
- The hosts note that the book intentionally omits transition strategy. They discuss how moving to such a system would face opposition from current property owners and how democratic elections could reverse socialist policies after a few years.
