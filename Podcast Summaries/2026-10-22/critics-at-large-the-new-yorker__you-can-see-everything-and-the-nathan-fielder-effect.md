# “You Can See Everything” and the Nathan Fielder Effect — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
Critics at Large hosts Vinson Cunningham, Alex Schwartz, and Naomi Fry discuss the cultural phenomenon of You Can See Everything, the Nathan Fielder–Lance Oppenheim documentary about Elizabeth Holmes’s final days before prison, which made $15 million in its opening weekend. They debate whether the film reveals Holmes as an unreachable liar or turns Fielder’s method back on himself, and why his simulation-heavy, socially awkward persona now feels central to an era of AI and blurred reality.

## Key points
- The film documents Elizabeth Holmes, convicted Theranos founder, in her final 34 days before reporting to prison in 2023, living with partner Billy Evans and their two children in Southern California; it was directed by Lance Oppenheim and Nathan Fielder.
- The movie made $15 million in its opening weekend and became a cultural phenomenon, with sold-out screenings and intense spoiler warnings from the filmmakers urging critics not to reveal the second half.
- Hosts agree the film raises big questions about truth, trust, and the knowability of other people, functioning less as conventional documentary than as a philosophical treatise.
- Vinson Cunningham is frustrated, arguing that Elizabeth Holmes is simply a bad liar and the film's mysteries are obvious; he asks 'Who gives a shit?' about whether she knows Billy was paid.
- Alex Schwartz argues the film is fundamentally about Nathan Fielder being 'Fielder-ized': placed in the role of his own subjects, becoming a proto-journalist who gets spun around by Holmes and Evans.
- Naomi Fry counters that the film is compelling because it explores why people believed Holmes and how she might believe her own lies; she finds the enforced intimacy between filmmaker and subjects interesting.
- The second half of the film introduces Amanda Seyfried, who played Holmes in The Dropout, to help Fielder reenact and interpret scenes, blending documentary with meta-commentary on acting and interpretation.
- Nathan Fielder's persona is analyzed: his trademark flat, socially awkward character has evolved from Nathan For You's business-prank show to The Rehearsal's elaborate simulations, now to this documentary where he confronts a 'worthy target.'
- Alex Schwartz connects Fielder's work to the current cultural obsession with simulation, AI, and the collapse of certainty about what is real, calling him a structuralist whose films deconstruct reality.
- The hosts discuss spoiler culture; Richard Brody's recent piece argues spoiler warnings act as marketing and formal play, and the hosts decide to spoil everything for a full discussion.
- Billy Evans is described as a 'reality TV performer' and 'WWE heel,' citing his compliments of Charlie Kirk and his claim that Prince Harry was paid for documentaries; Lance Oppenheim interjects that those are not real documentaries.
- Fielder uses a clip of Abbott and Costello's 'Who's on First?' to test Holmes's comprehension of wordplay; she claims not to get it, which Vinson reads as real annoyance at being called weird.
- The hosts also note Fielder admits he doesn't know what happened to him during the experience, which Alex believes, while Naomi finds the 'I don't know what I think' stance disingenuous for such a deliberate film.
