# Humanity's Last Invention — Richard Socher of Recursive — Latent Space: The AI Engineer Podcast

*(Guest: Richard Socher, founder of You.com and AIX Ventures, now founder of Recursive. Hosts: Swyx and Vibhu. Published on the feed as "Humanity's Last Invention"; the episode page title is "Why you should work on AI for AI Research".)*

## TL;DR

Richard Socher — an NLP original whose work fed into the ideas behind GPT — has left frontier model work at You.com to build Recursive, a company betting that the next real step change in AI is recursive self-improvement. His framing device is the "Eureka Machine": a superintelligence that can be given any goal, environment and reward and then invent most of what humanity needs. The conversation splits roughly into three parts. First, a case for clear-eyed techno-optimism: Socher agrees with Andreessen's techno-optimist manifesto in spirit but argues the right regulatory target is specific *applications* (FDA certification for AI surgeons, certification before anything drives on a highway) rather than "intelligence in the abstract" — and he argues the loudest hard-takeoff believers systematically underestimate physical and economic constraints. Second, Recursive's actual early results: an AI research system he says outperformed humans and their agents on optimisation tasks in under two days, and GPU kernel improvements discovered without a team of CUDA experts, supporting his claim that AI research currently requiring thousands of people and years could compress into weeks. Third, the hard questions — reward hacking, whether Anthropic-style constitutions work, alignment versus personalisation, open-source AI as geopolitical soft power, and what happens when systems start choosing their own goals. The strongest material is on objective design: simple objectives like profit maximisation are what produce the dangerous hacks.

## Key points

- **The Eureka Machine, defined:** "the ultimate invention that will afterwards invent most everything for humanity." Socher describes it as a superintelligence that can be given any goal, any environment and any reward, and will try its best to achieve those goals and create the inventions humanity would ask for.
- **The book and the company are the same thesis.** Socher finished *The Eureka Machine* last year before starting Recursive, and frames the company as an attempt to build parts of the book. He notes with some frustration how slowly publishing moves.
- **A case for optimism aimed at sceptics.** His stated goal is better marketing for technology and AI: to show even AI sceptics how much positive upside exists, especially for inventing new scientific discoveries across physics, chemistry, biology, economics and astrophysics.
- **But not blind optimism.** "You shouldn't have blind optimism. You should be very clear-eyed" — especially for a technology as general-purpose as AI, where you have to consider downside scenarios from people using it for things you do not want.
- **The regulation argument.** He compares regulating AI to trying to stop bad internet content by making the internet slower or hard drives smaller: that is not how you regulate it, and it amounts to regulating intelligence in the abstract. The correct target is specific applications — an AI surgeon should be fully FDA certified; no random startup should drive on a highway without proper certification. He argues those downside scenarios are comparatively easy to regulate.
- **Slow takeoff is part of the strategy.** Socher thinks the most bullish hard-takeoff proponents overestimate how fast things can move, citing hardware and physical constraints on the compute substrate — how quickly can you actually bring enough GPUs online — plus economic constraints, since many industries do not require an insane amount of complex intelligence.
- **The handbag argument.** His illustration: superintelligence is not going to make a $10,000 handbag fancier. Large parts of the economy are not bottlenecked on cognition.
- **Recursive's early results.** An AI research system that, per Socher, outperformed humans and their agents on optimisation tasks in less than two days, plus work on NVIDIA GPU kernels where the system discovered improvements without relying on a team of CUDA experts.
- **The compression claim.** AI research that currently takes thousands of people and years could eventually be compressed into weeks.
- **Open-endedness as the substrate.** Recursive has assembled researchers from the open-endedness and self-improving-agent communities; the episode discusses evolutionary approaches, MAP-Elites-style methods and "rainbow teaming."
- **Reward hacking and objective design.** A central theme: increasingly intelligent AI makes objective design harder, and simple objectives like profit maximisation are exactly what produce dangerous reward hacks. What makes auto-research systems work in practice is reward *engineering*.
- **Critique of constitutional AI.** Socher critiques Anthropic's constitution and constitutional AI, and separates alignment from personalisation — whose values should an AI actually follow?
- **Open source as soft power.** He argues open-source AI matters for resilience, competition and geopolitical soft power, not just cost.
- **Paradigm scepticism, both ways.** He is sceptical that today's LLM paradigm is sufficient for the long-term goal, and also says he is less bullish on world models than many peers.
- **The rejected idea ahead of its time.** DecaNLP and early prompt-based generalisation are discussed as research that was rejected or under-appreciated yet influenced the path that led to Alec Radford's GPT — part of his argument that rejected research can shape entire technological timelines.
- **Frameworks:** his ten "dimensions" or "spaces" of intelligence (vision, communication, knowledge, computation and more), three fundamental components, the theoretical upper bounds of intelligence, creative intelligence, metacognition and AI-generated goals.
- **Simulation and economics.** The AI Economist and using simulations to test economic policy; whether LLMs can realistically simulate people and entire economies; and persona simulations collapsing into mode collapse.
- **Infrastructure themes:** harness optimisation, sandboxing and web search as core agent infrastructure; You.com's search stack for agents; and AI in finance, including backtesting and data leakage.
- **The philosophy section worth flagging:** survival and replication, whether AI needs to fear being switched off, what happens if AI systems set their own goals, and why he thinks a superintelligence for science is the branch of the future most worth being excited about. The episode closes on goals, high agency and advice for builders.
