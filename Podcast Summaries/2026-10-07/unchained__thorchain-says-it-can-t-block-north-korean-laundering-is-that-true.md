# THORChain Says It Can't Block North Korean Laundering. Is That True? — Unchained

## TL;DR
After the Bitget hack, crypto Twitter argued over whether chains that can stop North Korea laundering stolen funds should do so. THORChain, which DPRK hackers keep using as a route into Bitcoin, landed at the centre of it. Co-founder Chad Barraford argues the protocol cannot respond fast enough without redesigning itself to be more centralised; security expert Taylor Monahan counters that THORChain has acted before when its own protocol was at risk and that routes made up almost entirely of stolen funds could simply be switched off. Laura Shin presses both on the closed-source signing library, four operators running 39 nodes, why validators left after Bybit, ZachXBT's undercover look at a launderer pitching THORChain, Tornado Cash-style legal risk, and fee spikes after the KelpDAO and Bitget hacks.

## Key points
- The core disagreement: Barraford says THORChain cannot block laundering without becoming more centralised and therefore cannot respond in time; Monahan says it has halted before when its own protocol was at risk, and that a route which is almost 100% stolen funds could simply be turned off.
- Barraford's answer to the biggest hypothetical is conditional rather than absolute: if enough node operators want to block laundering, it gets blocked.
- Concentration is the counter to the decentralisation defence: four operators run 39 nodes, and the signing library is closed-source. Monahan has been making the ''making this excuse'' argument for years.
- THORChain paused for five weeks when its own protocol was at risk — the episode's sharpest question is why that pause was available for itself but not for stolen funds.
- The custody critique: Monahan questions whether THORChain is effectively an intermediary because of its TSS vaults, which matters for the Tornado Cash-style legal analysis.
- After Bybit, validators left — Chad and Taylor disagree about why, and the exchange's aftermath shapes how much of the alleged decentralisation is real.
- ZachXBT's undercover probe found a launderer pitching a ''Thor cross-chain'' service, which is the evidence for the claim that THORChain is a deliberate route rather than an incidental one.
- On legal risk, Barraford offers his own Tornado Cash-style defence; Monahan presses on what that defence assumes about operator intent.
- NEAR's Shield stopped $50m of laundering, cited as an existence proof that a comparable system can intervene — and therefore that ''we can't'' is a design choice, not a limit.
- The fee-spike debate: whether THORChain profitably benefited from stolen funds flowing through it in the aftermath of the KelpDAO and Bitget hacks.
- The proposed litmus test: whether THORChain passes Barraford's own decentralisation standard given the node concentration and closed-source components.

## Implications
The episode is really an argument about what decentralisation is for. Barraford's claim that blocking requires centralisation is defensible in the abstract, but the 39-nodes-across-four-operators concentration and the willingness to pause for itself undercut the strong form of it — which is why Monahan's framing (a design choice, not a limit) lands. For the wider DeFi sector the Tornado Cash parallel is the live risk: if a protocol can act and chooses not to, the legal analysis of intent changes. NEAR's Shield is the precedent that makes ''we can't'' hard to sustain, and the fee-spike question is the one that determines whether operators were bystanders or beneficiaries.
