# Trading From The Short Side with Danny Moses, Vincent Daniel & Porter Collins — RiskReversal Pod

## TL;DR
Danny Moses, Vincent Daniel, and Porter Collins of Seawolf Capital discuss their framework for short selling, from identifying mispriced and miscategorized companies to managing risk around squeezes and catalysts. They share case studies including FICO, Tesla, Upstart, and Carvana, emphasizing that shorting requires deep sector expertise, respect for borrow costs, and strict risk management. They also compare shorting stocks versus using options and hedging with ETFs, and caution against fighting meme-stock manias.

## Key points
- The panelists (Danny Moses, Vincent Daniel, Porter Collins) founded Seawolf Capital and previously worked with Steve Eisman (of The Big Short); they are known for short selling but emphasize they do much more, including long positions and thematic investing.
- Short selling is extremely difficult and stressful; Danny Moses suffered a panic attack on the day of the 2008 short ban, and the panelists note that the psychological pressure of being short while others are long is intense.
- Most hedge funds short structurally to maintain low net exposure and justify higher fees. Large funds often use ETFs because building meaningful short positions in small-cap names is impractical, and they can charge premium fees for a 'hedged' portfolio even if shorting is not profitable.
- When evaluating a short candidate, check short interest as a percentage of float (20% is high), historical valuation and sector relative valuation, and the specific catalyst. Valuation alone is rarely a good short thesis over long periods.
- High short interest can cause violent short squeezes. Long investors should study the short thesis and recognize that short covering provides embedded buying power if the thesis is wrong.
- The cost to borrow a stock (often 3% to 7% annually) and any dividend payments on the shorted stock must be included in the trade's economics; these costs erode returns over time.
- Thematic shorts involve identifying sectors being disrupted, such as retail fads (Crocs, Lululemon, Krispy Kreme) or technology companies threatened by AI; disruption is more common in tech than in hard assets like refineries or mines.
- Look for 'miscategorized' companies that pretend to be something they are not. Upstart is a prime example: it markets itself as an AI lending platform but is really a financial company whose success depends on access to capital and securitization markets, not just revenue growth.
- The FICO short was triggered when FHFA director Bill Pulte announced support for competition (VantageScore) to break FICO's monopoly on mortgage credit scores. FICO traded at ~$2,400 (50-60x earnings) and fell to ~$500; the panelists shorted at the top, covered some on the way down, and re-shorted on rallies.
- Position sizing and risk management: if a short thesis remains intact, a rally to a 'difficult level' can be an opportunity to add, but stop losses are essential because shorts can theoretically lose infinite money while longs can only lose 100%.
- Tesla short thesis: Street earnings expectations collapsed from ~$10-12 per share for 2027 (in mid-2022) to $2.00-$2.50 for next year. Regulatory credit income was 100% of operating income and is being phased out, and Tesla is fundamentally a car company with razor-thin margins, not a robotics/AI company. The stock is hard to short because it is a cult.
- Vincent Daniel dislikes options because market makers know retail wants to limit downside and charge elevated premiums; options also truncate duration and require precise timing. Danny Moses uses options selectively, such as buying puts on Goldman Sachs when the risk/reward was skewed.
- Hedging strategies include shorting sector ETFs (e.g., XLE) against energy longs or using broad indices to hedge idiosyncratic single-name positions. Sometimes simply not owning a bad stock (like Oracle within a tech basket) is equivalent to a short from an opportunity-cost perspective.
- Meme stocks like AMC, GameStop, and Carvana are dangerous shorts because of high short interest and retail-driven squeezes. Carvana's profitability depends on selling loans to entities affiliated with Mark Walter at above-market prices; if that mechanism breaks, earnings could collapse, but the stock can rally on sympathy (e.g., with CarMax).
- Gold and bitcoin are viewed as hedges against monetary debasement. Rather than shorting the S&P 500 (which has gap risk), the panelists prefer owning metals or other debasement hedges because the market is structurally biased upward.
- Time frame is less important than risk/reward and whether the fundamental thesis is intact. If a short is not moving but the thesis holds, they may hold; however, they use stop losses based on price levels rather than arbitrary time limits.
- The Goldman Sachs short was based on peak valuation (3x tangible book) and limited upside, combined with the panelists' deep expertise in financial services. They preferred a single-name short over XLF because XLF contains Berkshire Hathaway, Visa, and Mastercard, which diluted the trade.
