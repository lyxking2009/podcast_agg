# At The Money: What the Best CEOs Actually Do — Masters in Business

## TL;DR
Nitin Nohria, former Harvard Business School dean, explains that CEOs create the architecture for decisions rather than making all decisions themselves. Drawing on 20 years of data and HBS new CEO workshops, he notes CEOs account for about 15% of company performance variance, spend 72% of time in meetings, and over 60% of M&A fail. He stresses communication, avoiding corner office isolation, and telling truth in bad times to build credibility.

## Key points
- CEOs are responsible for all company decisions but make very few directly; their real job is creating conditions—resource allocation, strategy, culture, execution processes, and standards—so others decide well.
- New CEOs in HBS workshops average $17 billion in revenue and 25 years of executive experience, yet describe the role as the steepest learning curve of their lives.
- Surprises for new CEOs include managing a board of 10-12 directors instead of one boss, words being amplified and scrutinized, information arriving packaged with agendas, and time being the scarcest resource.
- Individual CEOs account for about 15% of company performance variance over 20 years, nearly as much as the industry effect (15%), while company-specific factors account for 25% and macroeconomic/year effects 5%.
- The CEO's job is not to make all decisions but to create the conditions for others to decide well; how CEOs respond—what they pay attention to, when they intervene, and how they run meetings—shapes decision quality.
- Meetings consume 72% of a CEO's total work time, making the choice and conduct of meetings central to effectiveness.
- CEOs spend 36% of their time reacting to unfolding events, despite the common expectation of proactive agenda-setting.
- More than 60% of corporate M&A fail to deliver value, driven by bidding wars that lead to overpaying (winner's curse) and underestimating post-merger integration, evidenced by employees still identifying with legacy companies years later.
- Communication must travel intact beyond the room; a study found employees' understanding of CEO priorities often misaligned with the CEO's intended messages, so CEOs should keep messages simple, repeat them, use stories, and check front lines.
- Corner office isolation occurs because CEOs become efficient by meeting in their offices, risking loss of ground truth; best CEOs discipline themselves to regularly visit plants, stores, and customers in unorchestrated settings.
- Credibility is built by telling truth in bad times; Larry Culp at GE confronted reality about the company's problems while instilling hope that they could be fixed, which builds trust for good times.
