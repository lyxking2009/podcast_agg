# John Armitage: How He Invests, Building Egerton, and What Worries Him About AI — In Good Company with Nicolai Tangen

## TL;DR
John Armitage — co-founder of Egerton Capital, Nicolai Tangen's former boss and the investor Tangen credits with teaching him the most — gives a master class in how a long-only equity firm has stayed sharp for 30 years. His core discipline is simple and unfashionable: start by asking whether the idea is worth the time, demand valuation protection on the downside, distrust accounting that flatters results, and read the incremental margin. He is withering about EV/EBITDA, about management that never admits a mistake, and about the paralysis that comes from absorbing too much information. On AI he is a sceptic of a specific kind: use it to short-circuit tasks, never let it think for you.

## Key points
- A good company is not a good investment, and a moat without good management is a trap: he cites Boeing under its previous CEO and Rolls-Royce before its current chief, which he calls a ''burning platform''. Culture, capital allocation and moats are the three legs; Intel lost its way because it ''didn't dare to innovate''.
- First filter: is it worth the time? Many businesses are fairly priced, and there is no point spending time on something fairly priced. Second: can I actually understand it? A drug-company CFO told him phase-3 trials are treated as ''a series of options'' — if the CFO cannot assess them, an outsider cannot.
- He hunts inherent operating leverage — what would make a business massively over- or under-perform expectations — because without grasping that you are ''on a hiding to nothing''.
- Pricing power is the capability that matters most: it is the ability to raise price despite competition, and if you can price above cost inflation you structurally achieve margin growth.
- The firm is not style-defined, and he half-wishes it were because style-defined managers keep clients better. With cyclicals, ''you often feel the best before a downturn.''
- His personal definition of a good holding: ''a company where if the stock market closed I wouldn't feel worried'' — valuation protection on the downside plus management that is realistic rather than relentlessly positive.
- On modelling: ''the models are for juniors.'' Too much detail means garbage in, garbage out; you must isolate the few absolute key factors. Airbus is planes sold times price — but the price is contractual and private, so you work from incremental margins and inferences. Reinsurance contracts are also private, so you study how reserves have developed. For Nvidia the key variable is memory cost inflation against a 75% gross margin; for Visa, with 67% margins, the top line and costs are legible.
- Incremental margin is the line that matters: a very high incremental margin relative to the stated margin produces big beats or big misses, and the market rarely models the upside.
- On the balance sheet he watches inventories (they affect recognised revenue and cost), terms of trade, receivables and payables, whether cash actually rose, and artificial boosts such as factoring receivables.
- Judging management: do they admit mistakes? He holds up Michael O'Leary of Ryanair, who ''you'd think... just made a series of mistakes'' despite building the world's most profitable airline. He cites Pat Gelsinger at Intel adding stock-based comp back to earnings as a realism red flag: ''so stock-based comp is not a cost?''
- Red flags: consistently missing expectations, and a picture always presented slightly too positively. From Altice (a French cable company turned leveraged telecom roll-up) he learned to sell when a model has no room for error — he exited on a slight miss purely because there was no top-line growth to absorb a mistake.
- ''Paralysis by analysis'' is a bigger risk today than it used to be because information has proliferated; you must cover every base because rivals will act on things you miss, but over-absorbing imprints noise on the brain.
- Valuation: he uses P/E, free-cash-flow multiples and EV/EBIT, and ''hates with a passion'' EV/EBITDA — depreciation is a real cost, which is especially ruinous for neoclouds whose largest cost is depreciation. He would pay about 30x next year's earnings for a good company growing at a decent double-digit rate. He admits valuation has been ''too important'' in his career and has cost him growth stocks.
- On permanent capital: he suspects lock-ups are a trap that removes the tension that keeps you sharp — ''I worry it would make me relax and be complacent.''
- AI: he wants it to short-circuit routine work but worries about intellectual atrophy — people who stop doing mental arithmetic lose the ability; the goal is help, not a substitute for thinking.
- World view: markets are high, US bond issuance is high, there are two wars and oil is up, yet equities are near records. The only plausible explanation is AI-driven growth and strong US earnings: without them ''the world would be in a different place.''

## Overview
A rare full-length interview with John Armitage, host Nicolai Tangen's former boss and the person he says taught him most about investing. Tangen frames it as a master class in three parts — how to look at a company, how to build a position, and how to run a firm for 30 years without losing your edge — and Armitage answers in the language of a practitioner who has done it since the 1990s. The conversation moves from the definition of a good company, through pricing power, cyclicality, modelling and the balance sheet, into management judgement and his dislike of flattering accounting, then into valuation, permanent capital and the current market backdrop. Along the way he is candid about his own biases (valuation has cost him growth stocks) and about the modern disease of information overload. ## Implications
For practitioners the interview is a checklist. Screen for pricing power and structural margin growth; demand a legible path to downside valuation support; treat incremental margin as the swing factor in earnings surprises; and refuse to pay for flattering accounting policies. Armitage's explicit scepticism of EV/EBITDA is a pointed warning for the AI-infrastructure complex, where depreciation is the largest cost. His admission that being valuation-disciplined has cost him growth stocks is a fair statement of the trade-off rather than a boast, and his view that lock-ups remove discipline is a useful counterpoint to the standard long-duration pitch. His market read — that records in equities rest entirely on AI capex and US earnings — is a compact statement of what has to keep going right.
