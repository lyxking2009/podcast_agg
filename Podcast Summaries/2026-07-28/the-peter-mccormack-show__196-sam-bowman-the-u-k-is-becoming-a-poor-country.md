# #196 - Sam Bowman - The U.K. Is Becoming a Poor Country

**Show:** The Peter McCormack Show
**Date:** 2026-07-28
**Transcript source:** show_notes

## Key Points
- Bowman's central claim: Britain has endured 'two lost decades' of stagnant median wages and is now poorer than it should be despite having talent, capital, and ideas
- The 'Housing Theory of Everything': Britain's failure to build enough housing in its most productive cities is the root cause behind a cascade of problems — unaffordability, delayed family formation, reduced labour mobility, and weaker productivity
- Homes have shifted from being shelter to being speculative financial assets, which Bowman links to falling birth rates and generational wealth divides
- Stamp duty is singled out as 'Britain's worst tax' because it punishes moving house, locking people into the wrong homes and jobs and reducing mobility
- Britain's planning system is described as broken and captured by a 'vetocracy' — diffuse veto power that lets small groups block nationally beneficial projects; one planning application reportedly cost £297 million
- Post-war centralisation of the British state stripped local governments of the incentive and fiscal upside to support growth in their own areas
- Persistent inflation is framed as corrosive to social trust, and near-zero growth turns politics into a zero-sum fight over a shrinking pie
- The civil service and political incentives reward risk-avoidance and passing legislation over taking the risks needed to actually build things
- Britain risks losing its position relative to peers: GDP per capita is about to be overtaken by South Korea and Slovenia, and Poland could overtake the UK within roughly a decade
- AI is discussed as a potential productivity lever, but Bowman is skeptical Britain can capture the gains given its energy costs and infrastructure constraints
- Despite the bleak diagnosis, the episode closes on qualified optimism that unlocked economic growth would resolve many of Britain's other political and social problems

## Overview
In this roughly two-hour conversation, Peter McCormack sits down with Sam Bowman — an economist associated with Works in Progress and a prominent voice on UK stagnation — to dissect why Britain has grown so much poorer relative to its peers over the past two decades. Bowman's argument runs through the episode: Britain stopped building, over-centralised its state after World War II, and has since presided over two lost decades of flat median wages, all while housing, energy, and infrastructure investment have been effectively banned by a dense web of regulation and local veto power. The conversation's spine is what Bowman calls the 'Housing Theory of Everything' — failing to build enough homes in Britain's most productive cities distorts the whole economy: it discourages people from moving to where the jobs are, turns homes into speculative assets rather than places to live, and is linked to falling birth rates. Bowman walks through mechanisms including stamp duty and the planning system, citing a £297 million cost for a single planning application as evidence of a 'vetocracy.' Later segments broaden into political economy: how persistent inflation erodes public trust, how a zero-growth economy turns politics zero-sum, and how civil service incentives favour writing new rules over taking the risks needed to build things. The episode closes by weighing whether Britain can still recover, touching on the energy debate and whether AI-driven productivity gains can be captured given the country's infrastructure limitations.

## Implications
Bowman's framing — that the UK should behave like a catch-up economy focused on deploying existing technology and infrastructure more efficiently rather than a frontier economy — has significant implications for British policy debates. It reframes housing and planning reform as the central lever for reversing wage stagnation, and implicitly criticizes cross-party consensus policies (strict planning controls, stamp duty, diffuse veto rights) as structurally responsible for national decline. The warning that peer countries like Poland, South Korea, and Slovenia are set to overtake UK GDP per capita within a decade raises the stakes for the domestic political debate over growth versus other priorities. If Bowman's 'vetocracy' diagnosis is correct, meaningful reform requires re-centralising risk-taking capacity and reducing the number of actors who can block development.
