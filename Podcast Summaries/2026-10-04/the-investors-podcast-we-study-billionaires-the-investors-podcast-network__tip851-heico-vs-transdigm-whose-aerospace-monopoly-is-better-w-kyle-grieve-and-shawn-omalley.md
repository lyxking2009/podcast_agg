# TIP851: HEICO Vs. TransDigm: Whose Aerospace Monopoly Is Better? — The Investor’s Podcast (We Study Billionaires)

## TL;DR
Kyle Grieve and Shawn O’Malley pit HEICO and TransDigm — two aerospace-component suppliers with dominant aftermarket positions and unusual pricing power — against each other on both quality and value. Both are exceptional, near-identical businesses: they sell certified replacement parts for aircraft that stay in service 30-50 years, grow through serial acquisition, and enjoy regulatory barriers to entry. The difference is philosophy. TransDigm acquires sole-source suppliers and then raises prices aggressively over the part’s life; HEICO engineers FAA-approved alternatives priced 30-50% below the OEM. The hosts conclude HEICO has the higher-quality business by a razor-thin margin, but the market knows it — at ~30x EV/EBITDA versus TransDigm’s ~18x, HEICO is too expensive to justify a buy today.

## Key points
- **The pitch:** HEICO vs. TransDigm, judged on quality and value. Coming in, Shawn assumed one would be the obvious winner; after diving in, they found the businesses “much closer in terms of quality” than expected, and had to nitpick subtle nuances to separate them. The one place they are not close is valuation.
- **Recurring aftermarket revenue is the core:** Aircraft stay in service 30-50 years and parts must be replaced during maintenance. Aftermarket is ~55% of TransDigm’s business and ~59% of HEICO’s (including repair & overhaul) — a highly forecastable annuity.
- **TransDigm’s model:** Founder Nicholas Howley built the company around acquiring suppliers that are already the sole certified source of a part, then extracting value through three levers — cut costs, raise prices, and win new business. Revenue compounded ~17% and EBITDA ~18% for over two decades; profit margins ~21%. The hosts call the pricing approach “a bit predatory” and note it invites regulatory scrutiny.
- **HEICO’s model:** Also does OEM and aftermarket, but its edge is the FAA Parts Manufacturer Approval (PMA) path — it re-engineers functional equivalents and deliberately prices them 30-50% below OEM prices. Kyle calls HEICO “the generic drug company of the aerospace industry”; the PMA process takes 2-5 years per part, which is itself a moat. Profit margins ~18%; more than 113 acquisitions since 1996 (~3/year), with 6 deals already in 2026.
- **Segments:** HEICO — Flight Support Group ~67% of revenue (jet-engine and aircraft replacement components), Electronic Technologies Group ~33% (niche electronics: laser rangefinders, amplifiers, antennas, power supplies). TransDigm — Power & Control, Airframe, and a small non-aviation segment.
- **Industry economics:** Parts makers are cash cows — GE Aerospace posted ~27% margins over the last six months, versus ~18-21% for HEICO and TransDigm; airlines by contrast are poor businesses. Regulation (FAA) and per-part engineering/certification create high barriers to entry, so parts are not commodities. Anecdote from the book *The Compounders*: the Mendelson brothers asked guests to guess the price of a small bag of aircraft screws; they guessed $10,000, the real price was ~$90,000.
- **M&A comparison:** Both are serial acquirers. The hosts note serial acquirers eventually must do larger (more competitive, lower-return) deals as they scale, and debate whether HEICO’s smaller size gives it more runway — they ultimately call the TAMs roughly even.
- **Valuation:** HEICO trades at ~30x EV/EBITDA, TransDigm at ~18x. Daniel Mahncke’s earlier TransDigm intrinsic value of ~$1,100 is roughly where the stock trades today, implying returns near the mid-to-low-teens discount rate. Kyle’s HEICO base case — 14% revenue growth, 30% EBITDA margin, 28x EV/EBITDA — gives ~$550; applying a 20% margin of safety yields ~$354, an ~8% return that falls short of the portfolio’s 12% hurdle.
- **Verdict:** HEICO wins on business quality (better model, stronger balance sheet, family alignment) but is priced for perfection; TransDigm may offer more upside at two-thirds the multiple but is on the wrong side of the model they prefer. Neither is added to the Intrinsic Value Portfolio today — HEICO goes on the watchlist for a 10-20% pullback.
