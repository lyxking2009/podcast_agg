# The grief, loneliness, and burnout sweeping through the tech industry right now | Molly Graham — Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth

## TL;DR
Molly Graham revisits her famous 'give away your Legos' career advice in the age of AI. She explains that while leaning into change and giving away work is still essential, handing tasks to AI is different because you keep oversight and mental load—so some Legos, like judgment, trust, taste, and vision, should not be given away. She also discusses the grief, loneliness, and burnout sweeping tech (burnout rose from 44% in 2025 to 55% in 2026) and argues leaders must normalize emotions, treat AI as an intern, and help people see their jobs will transform rather than disappear.

## Key points
- The original 'give away your Legos' advice, developed at Google and Facebook, says that in fast-growing companies you should hand off projects, teams, and responsibilities to others to make yourself irrelevant for your current role, freeing you to take on new challenges; it is fundamentally about coping with change and normalizing emotions.
- The core of the advice still holds: change is scary and hard, but leaning into it is essential; the future will be defined by people who learn, not people who know, and standing still at a successful company is actually falling behind.
- In the AI era, giving Legos to AI is not the same as giving them to a human because you cannot fully transfer oversight; you still own the final product and mental load, effectively turning everyone into a manager of robot 'interns' and increasing psychological burden.
- Contrary to the original advice, some Legos should not be given away in AI: tasks requiring judgment, trust, taste, vision, relationships, and defining what 'good' means should remain human; outsourcing them risks AI slop and loss of accountability.
- There is widespread grief, loneliness, and burnout in tech; Lenny's survey found 55% of tech workers report burnout in 2026, up from 44% in 2025, and more than half feel burned out as expectations rise without matching compensation.
- Happiness correlates with feeling AI-amplified: people who have figured out how to use AI to do more of what they love are happier, while designers are the least happy because AI makes design accessible to everyone, leading to more pressure and AI slop cleanup.
- The dominant fear narrative that AI will take everyone's jobs is overblown and toxic; AI-branded layoffs often mask overhiring mistakes, and actual data does not yet show massive job loss—engineering demand, for example, is higher than ever.
- Manoush Zomorodi's reframe is powerful: ask 'What would you do if you believed your job was always going to exist? It was just going to look completely different every six years.' This shifts people from protecting the past to reinventing their role.
- Treat AI as a junior intern, not a superintelligent employee; you would never copy-paste an intern's work to your boss, so you should not ship AI output without proper context, iteration, and accountability.
- Focus should shift from raw productivity (token counts, output volume) to efficiency and quality; engineering productivity is up but code rewrites have increased 8x, and AI slop creates extra work for others who must clean up after it.
- Leaders must role model accountability and define what good looks like; they should openly talk about grief and change, make space for emotions, and avoid propagating the fear narrative—management matters more, not less, in times of upheaval.
- Breaking down role walls is key: designers shipping code, marketers prototyping, and engineers steering rather than rowing are early signs; being willing to shed old job definitions and ask first-principles questions will define future success.
- The 'centaur vs reverse centaur' framework warns against becoming a tool for AI; we want AI to serve human goals and values, not humans becoming mere overseers executing AI's optimization.
