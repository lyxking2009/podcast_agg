# E253｜谁在给大模型出题、卖题、判卷？聊聊AI数据行业的野蛮生长 — 硅谷101

## TL;DR
This episode of 硅谷101 explores the fast-growing AI data industry, focusing on how companies like Scale AI, Mercor, and AfterQuery supply training data, rubrics, and reinforcement learning environments to large model labs. Guests 何允中 from Scale AI and 孙一铀 from UC Berkeley discuss the shift from basic annotation to expert-generated rubrics and RL environments, the rise of agentic data, and the challenges of benchmark pollution and data procurement. They emphasize that data vendors must continuously innovate and that many verticals remain underserved, while noting the high valuations and intense competition in the sector.

## Key points
- AI data companies are categorized by origin: recruitment-based firms like Mercor (which uses AI interviews to build expert networks), traditional crowd-sourcing vendors like Scale AI and Surge AI, synthetic data startups, and domain-specific players (e.g., embodied AI companies that sell data assets).
- The industry has shifted from pre-training data annotation to post-training needs: first to rubric data (expert-written scoring criteria) and now to RL environments (sandboxed tasks with tools, programmatic checks, and verifiers).
- AfterQuery, founded by three young people, raised Series A at $300M valuation in April 2025 and reached $3.2B by September—a 10x increase in under six months, setting a YC record for fastest unicorn.
- Scale AI was valued at over $29B when Meta invested last year, and Mercor reached $10B valuation in October 2024, reflecting massive investor interest in data infrastructure.
- Rubric data enables weak-to-strong supervision: an expert writes evaluation criteria that a weaker model can use to score a stronger model's output, crucial for RLHF in domains without simple objective answers.
- RL environments are now the frontier: they include tools, sandboxes, and verification mechanisms (programmatic checks, rubrics, preference models) to train agents on long-horizon, economically valuable tasks.
- ALE (Agentic Last Exam) is a large-scale agent evaluation benchmark led by UC Berkeley RDI and 300+ experts, currently with 150 public tasks across 50 industries, aiming for 1000+ tasks in V2.
- OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified scores due to test defects and data contamination; many benchmark improvements may reflect memorization or flawed tasks rather than true capability gains.
- Data contamination is hard to detect for fabricated data: an expert might submit synthetic tasks that appear plausible but are never actually executed, causing long-term corruption of benchmarks.
- Incentive design is a major challenge: giving authorship credit can motivate experts to submit fake data, while hourly pay limits access to top talent; proof-of-work mechanisms are being explored.
- Data procurement is expensive and difficult: game source code can cost 2-3 million RMB per item; medical data is sensitive; life sciences benchmarks cover less than 5-10% of known workflows.
- Synthetic data startups thrive in coding because software has abundant public data; small teams can deliver large volumes of synthetic RL environments quickly, but sustainability depends on R&D and procurement.
- Model labs often lack the incentive or trust to collect proprietary industry data directly (e.g., conflict of interest when deploying engineers to enterprises), so third-party vendors remain essential.
- Benchmaxxing is not inherently bad if the benchmark reflects real user experience or core cognitive abilities; but optimizing only for specific benchmarks without generalization is risky.
- The data business has lower valuation multiples because it lacks persistent leverage; vendors must continually invest in new domains and capabilities, unlike consumer platforms with accumulated user bases.
- Most real-world workflows can be framed as coding problems because agents use code as their 'hands and feet'; over 95% of tasks can be reduced to coding via APIs and tools.
