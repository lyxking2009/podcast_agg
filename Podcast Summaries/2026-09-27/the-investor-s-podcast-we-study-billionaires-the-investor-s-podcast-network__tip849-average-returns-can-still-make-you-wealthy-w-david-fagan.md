# TIP849: Average Returns Can Still Make You Wealthy w/ David Fagan — The Investor's Podcast (We Study Billionaires)  - The Investor’s Podcast Network

## TL;DR
David Fagan, a Nova Scotia CPA, argues that average investment returns can still build meaningful wealth if investors focus on 'total wealth performance'—getting money invested on time, optimizing the compounding engine, and minimizing tax drag and fees. Using client stories, he shows how cash sitting idle and tax inefficiency can erode returns significantly, and emphasizes the importance of behavior, savings discipline, and learning to invest before life's biggest financial decisions.

## Key points
- Total wealth performance consists of three parts: front-end performance (getting capital invested promptly), the compounding engine (the actual rate of return earned), and back-end performance (what survives after taxes, fees, structure, and friction). An investor's broker statement return often overstates real wealth creation because it ignores these surrounding factors.
- Saving is the irreducible first step in wealth creation from near zero. David's personal story of spending his entire $40 net worth as an eight-year-old on a broken Hot Wheels racetrack taught him the pain of waste and shaped his lifelong saving habit.
- Why some people save and others don't is deeply tied to lived experiences and psychology. The famous marshmallow test is often interpreted as innate willpower, but a more recent study suggests children who waited had learned that adults keep promises; their delay was rational based on trust.
- Delayed gratification is crucial but not universal; some people never outgrow wanting the 'shiny new thing' at 48. Understanding someone's past experiences—like losing money in the 1973-74 crash or making huge gains in real estate—can explain why their financial decisions seem rational to them.
- Availability of capital is a major front-end factor. A client with $4M in a well-allocated index portfolio and $1.5M in cash waiting for a 'better time to buy' reported an 18.2% return but actually earned closer to 15% when accounting for the idle cash, just because he wanted to time the market.
- Another client incorporated in 2024 and delayed setting up a self-directed investment account for 11 months; missing 2025's strong market returns on her investable assets could cost her about $3 million over 40 years at an 8% compounding rate. The lesson: delays in getting invested create silent gaps that cannot be recovered.
- Business owners who sell their companies late in life often face overwhelming challenges because they never learned to be investors. David shared a client who sold a 40-year business for a multi-million-dollar payout but later said, 'Sometimes I wish I hadn't sold,' because she had to suddenly learn about yields, withdrawal rates, and advisor conversations at the worst time.
- The Charles Schwab study 'Does Market Timing Work?' illustrates that even a perfect market timer (Peter Perfect) barely beats a consistent investor, while a late investor (Larry Linger) suffers massive opportunity cost from waiting. Wealth creation is less about perfection and more about participation.
- Net worth is an imperfect but practical scorecard during the accumulation phase. Over 15-20 years, the direction and velocity of net worth captures savings rate, spending, tax efficiency, business decisions, debt management, and lifestyle choices—far more than portfolio returns alone.
- Tax drag of 1-3% per year can dramatically reduce long-term wealth. David gave a Canadian example: a professional with $3.75M in fixed income earning 4% ($150,000 interest) actually faced $170,000 in taxes due to loss of the small business deduction, resulting in an after-tax return of nearly -0.5%—compounding in reverse.
- Financial independence is not just about money; it removes pressures and allows you to become a better version of yourself for others. One mastermind member noted that FI helped him be better for those around him, akin to having someone carry part of your load so you can focus on other things.
- Investing style should match personal temperament and goals. A young investor with $100,000 may concentrate to find a 10-bagger, while a $100M portfolio owner may be satisfied with 6% low-risk returns. Dollar-weighting returns matters because you 'buy groceries with real dollars,' not percentage points.
- Total wealth performance emphasizes knowing the tax rules and using them to your advantage. Unlike market returns, tax rules are generally known at the start of the year, making tax planning one of the few controllable levers to improve after-tax compounding.
- The conversation also highlights that learning to invest should happen gradually, not when a liquidity event (selling a business, inheritance, divorce) drops a large sum into your lap. Having an action plan before money becomes available prevents costly delays and emotional decisions.
