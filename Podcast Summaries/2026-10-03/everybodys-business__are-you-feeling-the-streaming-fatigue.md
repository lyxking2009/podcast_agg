# Are You Feeling the Streaming Fatigue?

## Overview

Bloomberg Businessweek's Everybody's Business, hosted by Stacey Vanek-Smith and Max Chafkin, is recorded on the road at Bloomberg's Screen Time conference in Los Angeles. The episode has two segments. First, Bloomberg entertainment reporter Ashley Carman walks through why money is pouring back into podcasting after the post-Serial/Spotify correction: video (YouTube is now the No. 1 podcast platform) and celebrity entry have changed the economics, and a new round of nine-figure talent deals is being signed. Second, Tubi chief content officer Adam Lewinson makes the case for the free, ad-supported streaming model - Tubi's scale (110M monthly actives, 13B hours streamed in the past year, four straight profitable quarters) and its "rabbit hole" fandom strategy - and pushes back on Ted Sarandos's claim that AVOD services cannot support ambitious programming. The through-line is an industry resetting itself around video, scale and attention, with the show closing on Hollywood's anxiety about AI-era disruption.

## Key Points

- Podcasting money is back. Giggly Squad (Hannah Berner and Paige DeSorbo) signed a four-year deal with SiriusXM worth a reported ~$40M per year, a marker of a new seller's market even for shows most listeners had never heard of.
- Carman: "The money is back" - largely because YouTube is now the number one podcast platform, so podcasting has become a video operation with access to far bigger video ad budgets.
- Celebrities are the second tailwind: A-list talent reading an ad on a podcast functions as a far cheaper substitute for an endorsement deal.
- The Serial-era model (expensive narrative journalism, audience decay between seasons) proved uneconomical; the dominant form now is the low-cost "chatcast," which is far more predictable to sell.
- Hard Fork's Casey Newton and Kevin Roose left The New York Times and took a new tech show ("Machine Gods") to NPR for a reported ~$5M/year. Context: public media lost federal funding, NPR raised $100M+ this past year, and its new chief content officer (ex-YouTube Originals) is buying outside talent to chase tech ad dollars.
- Discovery, not production, was the missing piece: Spotify spent more than $1B trying to crack podcast discovery (including a reported ~$200M acquisition) and largely failed; TikTok, Instagram and YouTube already have the discovery algorithms.
- Top-of-chart shows barely turn over - Joe Rogan remains No. 1, Crime Junkie is No. 2, Conan has been in the top 25 for years - so buyers are all fighting over the same handful of names and hoping new talent breaks through.
- Tubi is the largest free long-form streaming platform: 110M monthly active users, 375,000+ movie and TV episodes, 13B hours streamed over the past 12 months, and profitable for four straight quarters.
- Tubi's revenue is strictly ad-supported and consumption-based (free is not a tier or a gateway for them); its strategy is personalised "rabbit holes" into fandoms rather than monoculture moments - kids/family is its fastest-growing area, plus year-round horror, Black entertainment and YA.
- Lewinson disputes Netflix CEO Ted Sarandos's assertion that AVOD services are neither big nor profitable and cannot fund ambitious programming, arguing "quality is in the eye of the beholder" and that prestige TV at $10-20M per half-hour is strained under any business model.
- Tubi, part of Fox, is expanding into live monoculture moments (an NFL game this Thanksgiving, a World Cup segment) and argues its free ad tier gives it a head start as payers like Netflix experiment with ad-supported tiers.
- Tubi's brand skews edgy and young (median viewer ~6-7 years younger than other streamers) and its free Pride-month merch kept selling out without the blowback other brands feared.
- Closing reflection: entertainment executives are reorganising "every six months" out of fear of being left behind, even as audiences are not especially enthusiastic about AI; California's economy is now described as the "fourth biggest country" in the world post-AI.

## Implications

For podcast-industry watchers, the episode is a clean read on where the money actually is: video distribution and celebrity reach, not expensive audio-only narrative production. For media investors, the Tubi segment is a useful counterpoint to the "streaming must be subscription" consensus - a free, ad-supported service reaching 110M monthly users and posting four straight profitable quarters undermines the idea that AVOD is structurally unprofitable, while the discovery discussion suggests the durable moat is algorithmic distribution rather than content spend.
