# What Nvidia's Megaspeed Scandal Is Really All About — Odd Lots


## TL;DR
Bloomberg reporter Mackenzie Hawkins discusses the Megaspeed scandal involving Nvidia, detailing how the Singapore-based firm became the largest Southeast Asian buyer of Nvidia chips while under US and Singapore investigation for possible smuggling. She explains the legal gray area of remote cloud access, Nvidia's role in allocation and due diligence, and the widening fallout including Jensen Huang's affair and DOJ probe. Huawei's limited capacity and export attempts are also examined as a geopolitical counterweight.

## Key points
- Megaspeed is the single largest Southeast Asian buyer of NVIDIA chips and a major supplier of AI compute to Alibaba, having purchased $4.6 billion worth of NVIDIA hardware after being spun off from a Chinese gaming company, passing through a BVI shell, and incorporating in Singapore just four days before becoming an official NVIDIA partner.
- The U.S. and Singapore governments have been investigating Megaspeed for over a year on suspicion that the company may have smuggled physical NVIDIA chips into mainland China, which Megaspeed has categorically denied, though U.S. investigators have also examined whether its ownership structure violates export controls.
- NVIDIA does not sell chips directly to end users; instead, chips flow from NVIDIA to server makers like Supermicro, Dell, and HP, then through distributors before reaching data centers, but NVIDIA's allocation decisions make it the 'kingmaker' in the industry.
- Chip smuggling often involves false declarations of destination and content; for example, the Supermicro co-founder was indicted for allegedly selling $2.5 billion of hardware to a Thai intermediary that lacked data center capacity, and restricted chips were declared as 'adapter modules' to evade customs.
- Bloomberg's investigation found that NVIDIA's reported count of chips at Megaspeed's data centers left about 50,000 chips unaccounted for when compared with Malaysian and Indonesian trade records; NVIDIA later claimed to have located 'substantially all' of the chips in warehouses, but has not answered repeated questions about subsequent checks.
- Megaspeed's investor presentation included a rendering of a data center in Shanghai, which raised red flags because one of its largest data centers dropped Megaspeed as a tenant and two other data center operators contradicted NVIDIA's account of where the hardware was located.
- Remote access cloud computing—where Chinese companies rent NVIDIA compute from data centers in Southeast Asia—is legal in the vast majority of cases; the Biden administration's AI diffusion rule would have imposed licensing requirements but the Trump administration is not enforcing it.
- NVIDIA's general counsel Tim Teeter called the head of the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) to ask about the detention of Alice, who later admitted an affair with Jensen Huang; subsequent restrictions required BIS agents to get approval from Jeffrey Kessler before contacting NVIDIA, and the DOJ eventually asked BIS to stop its investigation, leaving the case with the DOJ.
- Huawei is the primary Chinese competitor to NVIDIA but has severely limited production capacity; its senior executives say it will not satisfy domestic demand until 2030, and DeepSeek placed an order for 160,000 Huawei 950DT chips for an Inner Mongolia data center, though those chips are planned for inference rather than training.
- Huawei has begun making small export attempts, including a bid to supply 2,000 chips to Egypt for defense-related AI compute, prompting the U.S. government to organize American companies to counterbid; Huawei's capacity constraints mean every export comes at the expense of domestic customers.
- The Trump administration has sent mixed signals on export controls to Southeast Asia, scrapping proposed restrictions on Malaysia and Thailand after diplomatic pressure and withdrawing a global export control rule, creating intense uncertainty for companies building AI infrastructure in the region.
