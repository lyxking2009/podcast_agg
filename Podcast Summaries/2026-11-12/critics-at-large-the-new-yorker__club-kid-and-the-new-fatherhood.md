# “Club Kid” and the New Fatherhood — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
In this episode of Critics at Large, the hosts explore the evolving depiction of fatherhood in culture, prompted by Jordan Firstman's new film Club Kid, about a gay club promoter who unexpectedly becomes a parent to a 10-year-old son. They trace a history from idealized 1950s patriarchs and 1980s sitcom dads to the dead-father trope and today's more engaged, emotionally present fathers. The discussion highlights the film's strengths and flaws, while noting broader demographic shifts—such as increased paternal involvement and the pandemic's impact—that are reshaping what it means to be a dad.

## Key points
- The episode was prompted by repeated listener requests for examples of fatherhood in art and culture, leading the hosts to examine classic and contemporary father figures from Tony Soprano and Heathcliff Huxtable to Atticus Finch and Leon the Professional.
- Homer Simpson is framed as a parody of the American TV dad: lazy, self-absorbed, and leaving domestic labor to Marge, representing a post-idealized sitcom archetype that emerged after the 1950s Leave It to Beaver father and before the henpecked, incompetent dads of Everybody Loves Raymond and King of Queens.
- Bronson Alcott, father of Louisa May Alcott, exemplifies the utopian absent father: while his wife kept the household running, he pursued the transcendental commune Fruitlands with strict rules like eating only downward-growing plants, highlighting a pattern where paternal idealism displaces practical care.
- The dead father is a recurrent trope (Mufasa, King Hamlet, Donald Barthelme's 1975 novel The Dead Father) that turns paternal absence into a psychological burden for children, allowing them to define themselves in opposition to loss rather than through direct relationship.
- Mr. Bennet of Pride and Prejudice is cited as a loving but ineffectual father; he supports Elizabeth's refusal of Mr. Collins with a line that values her happiness over economic security, yet he fails to secure the family's financial future, reflecting a lineage from literary dads to 1980s sitcom fathers.
- Films like Mr. Mom (1983) and Three Men and a Baby (1987) used comedy to dramatize anxieties about men taking on maternal roles, depicting a tender arc from diaper-stapling incompetence to competence as women entered the workforce.
- Who's the Boss? (1984) presented Tony Danza as a single father working as a housekeeper for a wealthy career woman, inverting gender roles and modeling an ideal dad who is emotionally available and capable, though the premise is played for both comedy and sexual tension.
- Club Kid, written, directed by, and starring Jordan Firstman, follows Peter, a 30-something gay nightlife promoter who learns he has a 10-year-old son, Arlo, after the mother's death; the film uses the child as a catalyst for Peter's belated maturation.
- The hosts praised Club Kid's nightlife sequences for their sensory richness and depiction of queer community, but criticized its fairy-tale approach: the child is unrealistically agreeable despite recent trauma, and the film glosses over practical concerns like schooling, insurance, and the legal implications of an informal custody transfer.
- Vinson Cunningham argues the film would have been stronger without the child, as its early scenes capture a love of New York nightlife; he notes Peter's climactic speech about wanting the child is entirely self-focused, revealing the film's limited understanding of parental responsibility.
- The film's representation of a wider queer care network—including trans women known as 'the dolls' and a friend teaching the child to DJ—gestures toward family abolition ideas, but the ending's turn toward monogamy and domesticity may read as a conservative retreat for some viewers.
- Alex Schwartz cites data showing millennial fathers spend about four times as much time with their children as fathers in the mid-1960s, and the pandemic further accelerated this trend; Jessica Grose's New York Times column and charts highlight a 15-minute increase in daily child care among millennial dads from 2015 to 2023.
- Vinson Cunningham, a father with daughters nearly 20 years apart, has observed a marked increase in physically engaged dads on playgrounds compared to two decades ago, attributing this to demographic shifts like remote work and women's workforce participation.
- Emerging cultural markers of the new engaged father include Instagram's 'bird dad' who bikes with his son, hair-braiding workshops like Brews and Dos / Pints and Ponytails, and a Slate article by Luke Winkie noting that cooking has become a stylish, creative domain for dads, reversing its earlier association with drudgery.
- Alex Schwartz points to the novel Transcription (as discussed earlier in the season) for a haunting portrayal of fatherhood's transfer of anxiety: when fathers take on maternal roles, they also inherit the unfixable problems, not just the rewarding tasks.
- Vinson Cunningham reflects personally that fatherhood is a role nobody can fully inhabit; he lost his father at 10 and became a father at 20, and sees the father figure as simultaneously the person who ruins you and the person who saves you, a duality he tries to integrate.
