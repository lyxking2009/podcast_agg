# #216 - Ross Clark - Britain is Bust & The Coming Sovereign Debt Crisis — The Peter McCormack Show

## TL;DR
Britain's public finances are in crisis: national debt equals GDP (~£3.4 trillion) with a £133 billion annual deficit and £109 billion interest bill, and no government has balanced the books since 2001. Ross Clark argues that without immediate cuts to welfare (including the triple-locked state pension), disability benefits, and NHS inefficiencies—plus a halt to tax rises that suppress growth—the UK risks a sovereign debt crisis, IMF-imposed austerity, or hyperinflation if it prints money. Public opinion is slowly shifting toward fiscal restraint, but no major party currently offers a credible plan to close the gap.

## Key points
- The UK's accumulated public debt is now equivalent to GDP, around £3.4 trillion; last year the government spent £109 billion on debt interest alone, more than the £60 billion defense budget and nearly as much as the £120 billion education budget.
- The annual budget deficit was £133 billion last year, and no UK government has run a surplus since 2000-01, when Gordon Brown delivered a small surplus; successive governments have relied on borrowing in both good and bad times.
- UK government bond yields have risen a full percentage point above the levels seen during Liz Truss's 2022 premiership, signaling declining investor confidence and making debt servicing even more expensive.
- A quarter of UK government debt is index-linked to inflation, meaning any inflation spike immediately raises borrowing costs—unlike most developed countries—making the UK uniquely vulnerable to a fiscal crisis.
- To balance the books, the first major cut must be welfare, especially the state pension: the triple lock has added £15 billion per year versus earnings-indexed increases, and Ross Clark proposes cutting the basic state pension by 10% to its 2011 level.
- Disability benefits like Personal Independence Payments have ballooned, with 100,000 claims for ADHD (many self-diagnosed) and provision of Motability vehicles; Clark argues these must be subject to cost control like any private sector spending.
- NHS productivity has declined since 2019, costing an extra £22 billion annually just to maintain the same service; finding and replicating best practice across GP surgeries and hospitals could deliver savings without dismantling the NHS.
- No political party currently has a credible plan to eliminate the deficit: Conservative proposals would cut only £23 billion of the £133 billion gap, and Reform UK's £80 billion welfare cuts are offset by tax cuts, leaving the deficit unresolved.
- The Labour government under Keir Starmer, Rachel Reeves, and John Healey is likely to raise taxes rather than cut spending, targeting wealth, capital gains, and property investors, but such taxes often reduce revenue by discouraging asset sales and work.
- Historic crises show the danger: Greece's 2010 debt crisis required eight years and 15 austerity packages; the UK's 1976 IMF loan forced an emergency budget cutting £2.5 billion per year; a similar crisis would remove control from UK politicians.
- If the government monetizes debt by printing money, hyperinflation would follow—as seen in Zimbabwe, Argentina, and Weimar Germany—eroding living standards far more than the unpopular spending cuts needed now.
- Public attitudes are shifting: the British Social Attitudes Survey shows the proportion wanting lower taxes is the highest since 1983, and the gap between those wanting higher vs lower taxes has narrowed to an all-time low, suggesting growing appetite for fiscal restraint.
- Public sector productivity has stagnated since 1997 while civil service numbers have risen; burdens on private businesses like regulatory compliance and employer national insurance increases suppress growth, making debt reduction even harder.
