# Quantum Anomalies with Lara Anderson — StarTalk with Neil deGrasse Tyson

## TL;DR
A Cosmic Queries edition with string theorist Lara Anderson (Virginia Tech), who walks Neil deGrasse Tyson and Jordan Klepper through what string theory has and has not delivered: the Higgs is “thoroughly discovered,” extra dimensions may be smaller than 10^-19 m, gravity is 40 orders of magnitude weaker than electromagnetism and might be leaking into dimensions we cannot see, and string theory could yet be proven wrong rather than right.

## Key points
- On the Higgs boson, Anderson says it is “thoroughly discovered” — theory predicted it roughly 50 years early, the 2012-era detection came with high statistical confidence and a Nobel Prize, and its role in giving mass to the Standard Model particles has held up in every experiment since. What is open is whether there is any physics beyond it that today's accelerators can reach: “the jury is still out,” which she reframes as a statement about energy scales rather than about theory.
- Her central epistemics point is the energy/resolution ladder: probing smaller distances requires shorter-wavelength, higher-energy probes, the way x-ray diffraction works at 10^-10 m. The LHC reaches roughly 1 to 13 TeV, corresponding to about 10^-19 m, so everything below that scale is “blind” to direct experiment — theorists can only predict signatures and look for energy deficits.
- Tyson opens with a direct challenge — Einstein produced general relativity alone in about a decade, while dozens of string theorists have worked for 40 years without a unified theory. Anderson accepts it as “a very fair question” and argues the comparison is unfair because string theory is trying to describe quantum gravity, spanning the smallest distance scales up to the large-scale structure and history of the universe; she notes we had ample solar-system data ready to test general relativity, which string theory lacks.
- She argues string theory has already delivered something real: it is a demonstrably consistent theory of quantum gravity, valuable in the way idealized models are valuable in physics, even if it may not describe our universe. She says it is very possible the theory will be proven wrong on the basis of predictions about things we already observe, and that no-go results are themselves useful.
- On “what is a string”: Anderson calls the one-dimensional description a cheat. Strings are really described by a two-dimensional world sheet as they sweep through spacetime, and the endpoints of open strings are themselves dynamical — strings can split, merge, dissolve into their endpoints, and open strings can become closed loops, which changes their allowed vibrational modes.
- The black-hole question from a community-college class — could a higher-dimensional object intersecting our spacetime look like a black hole? — gets a qualified yes: singularities that appear in three dimensions can be “resolved” or smoothed out when extra dimensions exist to separate the crossing lines, so what looks singular to us may not be singular in the full theory.
- On large extra dimensions, she explains why we can bound their size: if extra directions were as large as our own, energy and mass would visibly escape into them in LHC collisions. Physicists nonetheless search for “large” extra dimensions in the non-intuitive sense of about 10^-19 m, and one motivation is the hierarchy problem — if gravity leaks into extra dimensions while other forces do not, that could explain why gravity is so weak.
- The gravity-is-weak riff is the episode's set piece: 40 orders of magnitude weaker than the other forces, demonstrated by Tyson's thought experiment in which stripping the electrons from one cubic centimeter at the top of a rocket would generate enough electrostatic attraction to the launch pad to keep the rocket from launching.
- Asked whether dark matter could be ordinary matter in an adjacent universe whose gravity spills into ours, Anderson says all bets are on the table because we still do not know what dark matter is, and that the productive move is always to ask what else a given explanation would predict and then go test that.
- On the ten-dimensions question (D = 10 in string theory, 11 in M-theory), she notes nobody originally wanted extra dimensions — researchers would have been delighted to keep three space and one time dimension; the extra dimensions are forced by the math, and the conjecture that they are curled up too small to access is why we cannot step into them.
- The holographic-principle question gets a nuanced answer: whether we live in a holographic universe is unsettled, but the underlying idea that gravity and gauge theories are deeply related is genuinely exciting, and the “celestial holography” program explores whether gravitational information in our universe can be encoded on a boundary at infinity — a simplification tool that might reveal an organizing principle.
- The closing time question — does time exist if atoms stop moving? — is answered jointly: time requires repeated phenomena to be measured (days, years, atomic vibrations), a totally static universe makes the question meaningless, and special relativity tells us there is no universal clock anyway, only combinations of space and time all observers agree on.
- Throughout, Klepper's comedy keeps the audience in the room (his “Lost in Space” and “Tessaract” riffs), and Tyson's willingness to argue with a working physicist on camera — rather than accept a deferential answer — is what makes this episode more than a physics explainer.
