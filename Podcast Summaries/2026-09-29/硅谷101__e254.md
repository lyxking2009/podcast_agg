# E254｜超级厄尔尼诺来了，我们的日常所需真会因它涨价吗？ — 硅谷101

## TL;DR
This episode features commodity researcher Zhang Siqi discussing the ongoing super El Niño, with the Niño 3.4 index exceeding 2.5°C and a high likelihood of persisting into Q1 next year. She explains that El Niño itself is not a blanket price increase, but affects specific crops and regions during critical growth stages—palm oil, rubber, sugar, wheat, coffee, fishmeal, and copper—when combined with low inventories, export concentration, and logistics disruptions. The conversation covers downstream impacts on food processing, pet food, beverages, clothing retail, and power, alongside risk management tools like futures, options, and improved breeding, concluding that while inflationary pressures exist, end-consumer price transmission is often buffered by inventory, brand, and hedging.

## Key points
- The current super El Niño has seen the Niño 3.4 regional sea surface temperature anomaly exceed 2.5°C in September; since 1950, only four previous super El Niño events have occurred (1972–73, 1982–83, 1997, and 2016). This event is expected to persist through at least Q1 of the following year, causing east Pacific floods and west Pacific droughts, a pattern often described as 'east wet west dry'.
- El Niño does not automatically raise all commodity prices. It alters rainfall, temperature, and water resources in specific regions. Price transmission requires the anomaly to hit critical crop growth stages, low global inventories, concentrated exporters, logistics disruptions, or export policies. There is no fixed percentage of weather impact on price.
- Regional exposure varies: marine Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines) faces drought, impacting palm oil, Robusta coffee, rice, and some cocoa. South Asia and continental Southeast Asia (India, Thailand) suffer delayed monsoons affecting sugarcane, rice, cotton, and maize. Eastern Australia's wheat, canola, cotton, and sugarcane are at risk. South America has mixed effects—Argentina and southern Brazil may benefit from wetter conditions, while central Brazil drought could delay soybean planting and second-crop corn.
- Palm oil is especially vulnerable because oil palm is monoecious and drought shifts sex ratios toward more male flowers, reducing fruit bunches. Indonesia and Malaysia account for over 80% of global supply. Historical El Niño periods have pushed palm oil prices up 10–20%, with impacts lagging several months after drought.
- Natural rubber supply is concentrated in Thailand and Malaysia (70–80% of global output). Drought slows photosynthesis and reduces latex flow from 3–4 hours to about 1 hour per day. Over 40% of Thai rubber trees are older than 35 years, compounding supply constraints.
- India and Thailand are significant sugar exporters; drought or late monsoon reduces sugarcane yields and can trigger export restrictions, tightening global sugar supplies. Brazil remains the largest producer, but marginal changes in India and Thailand often drive price risk.
- Peru has banned anchovy fishing due to El Niño. Anchovy is the single most important fishmeal raw material, and fishmeal is 70–80% used in aquaculture feeds and also in pig and premium pet foods. Price increases may transmit to salmon, sea bass, shrimp, and cat/dog food, though feed producers can reformulate with soy/rapeseed meal within scientific limits.
- Australia is a marginal wheat exporter, important as a supply pressure indicator. El Niño combined with a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) amplifies drought in Australia and Indonesia. Global wheat concentration is moderate—even counting the EU as a whole, top-five concentration is about 70%.
- Panama Canal rainfall from May to August was 34% below historical average, reducing daily ship transits from 36 to 34 on September 3 and further to 32 on September 15. This affects Asia–US East Coast and US intercoastal trade, raising shipping costs and causing rerouting, but it is a temporary capacity constraint, not a loss of global shipping capacity.
- Nitrogen fertilizer prices have risen due to Hormuz Strait tensions tightening natural gas supply. China's nitrogen fertilizer is coal-based, but international price linkages prevent isolation. Soybeans are less affected because they fix their own nitrogen.
- El Niño is not the main driver of copper supply. Primary factors are long-term ore grade decline (copper ore grade has fallen 40% since 1991) and long mine development times (average 17 years). Weather can cause point risks like floods, landslides, and water scarcity, but not uniform production cuts. About 80% of Chilean mines are in water-scarce areas, requiring desalination investment.
- Clothing retail can be hit by warm winters affecting down jacket sales; companies like Uniqlo have cited weather impacts on revenue. Power generation faces risks from water scarcity and heat wave demand. Food processing, beverages, and packaging require stable water and energy. Insurance and reinsurance may see higher claims due to increased extreme weather events.
- Governments and farmers use early warning systems, improved breeding for drought and heat tolerance, drip irrigation, reservoirs, and road infrastructure. Financial tools include futures, options, basis contracts, and weather index insurance. Large farms and cooperatives actively hedge; USDA monthly balance sheets and meteorological agencies are key monitoring resources.

## Notable quotes
> "厄尔尼诺首先它不是一个所有商品都会涨价的按钮，它改变的是不同地区的降雨、气温、海洋环境和水资源。只有当这种变化刚好打在了某个商品关键的一些生长期，又恰好碰到这个品种它的库存偏低、出口国比较集中、物流受阻或者政策收紧，才会真正传导到价格的波动上面。" — 张思齐
> "市场其实关心的是减产相对于全球的库存和可出口的余量到底有多大的影响，占比是多少，而不是只看某一个百分比。在一个全球分布集中度比较分散、库存很高的一个品种，它其实就不容易出现统一的减产……但是一个占全球出口占比可能50%以上的国家，如果它边际减个3%到5%，有可能价格都会表现得非常地极端。" — 张思齐
> "因为油棕它是一个雌雄同株的植物，它的性别比例是受遗传和环境的双重影响。水分是一个非常关键的环境因素，如果是干旱增加，它会让油棕开出更多的雄花，减少了雌花的数量，就减少了果串的数量，所以它会对印尼还有马来西亚的棕榈油的产量有非常大的影响。" — 张思齐
