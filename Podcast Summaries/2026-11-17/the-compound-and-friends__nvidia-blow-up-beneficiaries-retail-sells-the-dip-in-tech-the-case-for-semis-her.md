# Nvidia Blow-up Beneficiaries, Retail Sells the Dip in Tech, the Case for Semis Here, Anthropic IPO Watch — The Compound and Friends

## TL;DR
Josh Brown and Michael Batnick discuss NVIDIA's blow-up following CEO Jensen Huang's resignation amid an SEC investigation and scandals. Despite a massive earnings beat and $150 billion buyback, NVIDIA faces a leadership vacuum, eroding competitive moat, and a 36% drawdown. They analyze beneficiaries like AMD, HPE, Astera Labs, Apple, and Anthropic, while contrasting retail dip-buying with institutional selling in tech and debating whether the semiconductor selloff is over.

## Key points
- NVIDIA reported a monster fiscal Q3 2027 with data center revenue up 97% year-over-year to $8.5 billion, Q4 guidance midpoint of $17 billion (66% growth), and year-to-date revenue of $293 billion versus $27 billion in fiscal 2023. The company authorized an additional $150 billion share repurchase, and the stock trades around 11x forward earnings after a 36% drawdown from its peak.
- NVIDIA's competitive moat is eroding: its share of announced Frontier Lab compute agreements fell from 80-90% two years ago to 28% now, with Broadcom at 23% and AMD at 18%. Customers like Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet are building custom chips, and AMD's Helios rack systems are now a viable alternative to NVIDIA's Vera Rubin.
- CEO Jensen Huang (transcribed as Jensen Wang) resigned amid an SEC investigation and scandals involving relationships with employees and possible espionage concerns. His departure exposes key man risk; NVIDIA had 60 direct reports, no succession plan, and was organized as an exoskeleton around Huang. Market odds of his return by end 2026/2027 are about 12.5%.
- Josh Brown argues the only scenario where Huang returns is if the business and stock price collapse, as corporate governance standards may be waived if fiduciary duty demands it. He also notes the potential for political intervention, though that becomes less likely after the midterm elections.
- Beneficiaries of NVIDIA's turmoil include AMD, which has the only other rack-scale AI system (Helios) and saw forward EPS estimates rise 3.7% in the last week; HPE, which builds the racks and raised its fiscal 2027 outlook; Astera Labs, whose Q3 revenue grew 154% YoY to $585 million and Q4 guidance is $730-750 million, but 70% of revenue comes from one customer.
- Apple benefits as a clean, cash-flow-rich large-cap alternative; its CFO said memory prices account for 100% of sequential margin pressure, and NVIDIA's aggressive memory purchasing may have inflated costs. With NVIDIA potentially less dominant, memory costs could ease, improving Apple margins. Apple stock is up 30% in 2026 while NVIDIA is down 16%.
- The balance of power may shift from chip suppliers to compute buyers like OpenAI and Anthropic, especially with Jensen Huang's voice in Washington diminished. Anthropic's IPO price talk is $70-78, and Michael Batnick expects it to open at $90-100 on the first day.
- There is a stark divergence in investor behavior: Bank of America's Global Fund Manager Survey showed the largest cut in tech overweight since 2006 (25 percentage point drop), while Robinhood traders bought record amounts of single stocks last week, led by NVIDIA. Retail is buying the dip while institutions are selling.
- Adam Parker's analysis shows that cheap semiconductor stocks that miss earnings get punished more severely than expensive ones, with the penalty for missing estimates harsher for cheap stocks than in 25 years. However, he believes this cycle is different and does not expect the typical 20% reduction in net income growth three months later.
- The multi-manager pod shop model (e.g., Millennium, Citadel) exacerbates selling pressure because when a trader's edge expires after sector rotations, they are fired and their positions liquidated, regardless of fundamentals. This can wreck semiconductor stocks even if earnings grow 20%.
- Hosts reject JC's call for a housing boom, citing mortgage rates above 7%, existing home sales at 16-year lows, Floor & Decor's 20 consecutive quarters of comp declines, and poor price action in homebuilder stocks. They argue a boom requires mortgage rates at 3-4%, which won't happen without a recession.
- Berkshire Hathaway broke out from $500 to $600 since October 1, a significant technical move that was largely ignored amid macro distractions, illustrating how investors often miss breakouts when focused on other news.
