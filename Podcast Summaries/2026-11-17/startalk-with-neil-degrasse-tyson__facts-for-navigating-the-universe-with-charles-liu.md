# Facts for Navigating the Universe with Charles Liu — StarTalk with Neil deGrasse Tyson

## TL;DR
In this StarTalk special edition, Neil deGrasse Tyson, Gary O'Reilly, and Chuck Nice welcome astrophysicist Charles Liu for a Patreon Q&A on quantum physics, qubits, time dilation, and alien civilizations. Liu explains that higher dimensions might underlie quantum entanglement, qubits are quantum bits of information in superposition, and there is no mass limit except black hole formation. The group also explores counterfactual physics—such as infinite light speed erasing cosmic history—and the grabby aliens hypothesis, which Neil argues is self-limiting due to finite resources.

## Key points
- Neil deGrasse Tyson uses the analogy of a 3D sphere passing through a 2D plane to explain how particles that appear separate in 3D could be connected in higher dimensions; Charles Liu says this is essentially the idea behind string theory, where extra dimensions may account for quantum weirdness, though testability remains a major challenge.
- Charles Liu defines quantum entanglement as two particles that behave as one even when separated in space and time, like stretching quantum taffy, and emphasizes that no classical force or energy exchange is required, which conflicts with our classical bias.
- On free will, Charles Liu notes that quantum probability and wavefunction collapse do not imply conscious decision-making; he cites psychological experiments where brain signals prompting an action occur before a person fabricates a reason for acting, suggesting free will may be an after-the-fact narrative.
- Charles Liu explains that a qubit is not a physical object but a quantum bit of information; unlike classical bits that are strictly 0 or 1, a qubit can exist in a superposition of states until measured, enabling quantum computers to perform certain calculations much faster than classical computers.
- In answer to a question about a mass limit for time dilation, Charles Liu states that there is no maximum mass for an object, but if too much mass is packed into a given volume, it forms a black hole; the singularity has zero volume and infinite density, which is mathematically undefined.
- Neil deGrasse Tyson and Charles Liu discuss the consequences of an infinite speed of light: we would lose all ability to see back in time and thus know nothing about the universe's history; as Ken Croswell noted, the night sky would be permanently bright because every line of sight would hit a star.
- Neil deGrasse Tyson mentions the fine-structure constant, historically measured as roughly 1/137, which depends on the speed of light; an infinite light speed would alter atomic energy levels and break the framework of relativity.
- Charles Liu and Neil deGrasse Tyson explain that both gravitational and velocity time dilation affect spacetime itself, not the object; GPS satellites must correct for both effects, with the gravitational effect dominating, or navigation errors would accumulate.
- Charles Liu describes the grabby aliens hypothesis as expansionist aliens that take resources without moral or ethical considerations, distinct from the dark forest scenario in Liu Cixin's Three-Body Problem, where civilizations preemptively destroy any detected others.
- The group notes that a full-scale quantum computer could break current encryption methods by exploring many possibilities simultaneously, leading to a race between quantum encryption and code-breaking.
- Neil deGrasse Tyson argues that grabby aliens would be self-limiting because if all aliens are grabby and resources are finite, they would fight each other, similar to how European colonial powers clashed over land; thus the grabby scenario may be unstable.
