# The $300 Billion Structured Notes Market Is Booming — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Odd Lots hosts Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal speak with Alex Michelizzi, Nomura's international head of equity structured notes, about the booming $300 billion U.S. registered structured notes market, up from roughly $200 billion last year. Michelizzi explains how products like auto callables and worst-of structures allow high-net-worth investors to enhance yield by selling embedded puts, while dealers hedge delta and vega exposure, creating feedback loops that can amplify volatility and gamma effects in turbulent markets. The conversation highlights the growing role of these instruments in the AI trade, with single-stock notes on semiconductor and hyperscaler names reaching yields as high as 40% in Asia.

## Key points
- Structured notes are bank-issued products where investors give $100, forgo interest, take issuer credit risk, and receive customized equity exposure via participation (call spreads) or yield (selling puts), primarily sold to high-net-worth individuals through private banks.
- U.S. SEC registered structured note issuance is on track to hit $300 billion in 2026, up from roughly $200 billion in 2025, driven largely by worst-of auto callables tied to S&P 500, Nasdaq, and Russell indices.
- Auto callables periodically observe whether the underlying is above its initial level; if so, the product 'knocks out,' returning principal plus coupon early. This feature increases yield because the embedded put loses value as markets rise, but creates reinvestment risk for investors if volatility falls.
- Worst-of structures base payoff on the worst-performing asset among a basket (e.g., S&P, Nasdaq, Russell), generating higher premiums due to correlation risk; if one index lags, the investor may face losses despite other indices rallying.
- Dealers hedging these products first neutralize delta, then manage vega and binary knockout exposures (digitized as call spreads) by selling options to listed or broker markets, but the hedge is imperfect because actual trades are forward-starting and local vol models underestimate forward risk.
- Market turbulence causes auto callables to extend maturity, increasing dealer vega; simultaneously, market makers become less willing to absorb vega, leading to a 'spot up, vol up' regime where implied volatility rises even as spot prices remain near highs.
- On the single-stock side, structures with daily observations and knockout features create highly binary outcomes: if a stock rallies even slightly, the product disappears, but if it drifts lower, dealers are left with large vega positions that can vanish on a small rebound.
- Popular single-stock structured notes are concentrated in semiconductor and hyperscaler names, with some CoreWeave notes in Asia offering annualized yields of 40%, as reported by Bloomberg's Shuli Ren.
- The ideal scenario for an investor is high initial volatility (to lock in a high coupon) followed by a quiet, gradual drift lower with low volatility, maximizing the number of coupon payments without triggering downside exposure.
- If the AI trade reprices sharply, many structured products could approach their downside barriers, causing dealers to become short gamma near those levels, which would exacerbate market moves as they sell into declines and buy into rallies.
- Modeling correlation is challenging; dealers use stochastic volatility models to account for vol-of-vol, which is unobservable for single stocks, and products with knock-out features on individual names create massive vol-of-vol positions that can disappear rapidly.
- A creative structure gaining popularity is a 'knock-out auto call' where if any one of several stocks rallies sufficiently, the entire product knocks out, even if other stocks are deeply down, leaving dealers with deeply in-the-money puts that can be destroyed by a small rally in the remaining stock.
