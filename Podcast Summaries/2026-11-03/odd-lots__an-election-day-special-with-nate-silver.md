# An Election Day Special With Nate Silver — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Nate Silver joins Odd Lots for an Election Day special to discuss the midterm landscape. He notes Trump's approval is around 37%, historically bad, and conditions strongly favor Democrats, though Senate control requires wins in very red states. Silver's model gives Democrats roughly a 70% chance to win the Senate and highlights polling error risks, prediction markets, and the role of AI in his forecasting work.

## Key points
- Trump's approval rating sits at about 37%, which Nate Silver says is the worst for a president at a midterm since Harry Truman in 1946 by some measures. He attributes the unpopularity to broken promises on cheaper gas and no more wars, fatigue with Trump, and the Iran war; only 19% strong approval among that 37%.
- Silver describes the election as a 'rock versus the hard place' contest: background conditions (midterm history, voter anxiety about the economy, Iran war, Trump's lame-duck status, $4.30 gas) are extremely favorable to Democrats, but winning the Senate requires victories in deep-red states like Texas, Kansas, Alaska, and South Carolina, making it a difficult probabilistic leap.
- Polling errors are correlated across states, but less so in midterms because each state has different candidates; Trump-era polls underestimated Trump when he was on the ballot (2016, 2020, 2024), but elections without Trump on the ballot (like New Jersey and Virginia 2025) saw Democrats underestimated, suggesting the 'missing Trump voter' may not turn out for other Republicans.
- Pollsters appear to be self-correcting against a perceived Democratic bias; Silver cites a Marist poll of Maine where Democrat Troy Jackson led by 9 points among registered voters but the likely-voter model cut the lead to 2, a shift he says 'the math literally doesn't add up,' implying pollsters are rational actors avoiding another miss.
- Early voting and turnout data show strong Democratic enthusiasm: high turnout among Black voters and unlikely voters (those who voted in at most one of the past four elections), with little crossover from Democrats to Republicans, reinforcing the polling consensus that Democrats are having a good year.
- Trump remains the central character of the midterms despite Republican attempts to distance themselves; his endorsement of Ken Paxton in Texas and the decision to go to war with Iran are cited as factors that may have cost Republicans, with Democrat James Tallarico holding a slight lead in Texas and the Senate race leaning Democratic.
- Prediction markets like Polymarket and Kalshi are highly correlated with Silver's Flipper model (0.995 on individual states) and largely reflect polling plus sentiment; Silver notes they have a slightly Republican-leaning clientele and can move on news faster than polls, but he believes they would be 'extremely terrible' if they couldn't look at polls.
- Gerrymandering can backfire in wave elections—Silver calls this a 'dummymander'; Florida's map drawn by Ron DeSantis for a presidential-year baseline could yield more Democratic seats in a D+9 environment, and Texas's map based on Hispanic realignment may fail if those voters swing back, though the House is still only about 95% likely Democratic.
- Governors' races are undercovered but important; Silver highlights the Texas governor's race where Greg Abbott is ahead by only a couple points, noting Abbott's pro-data-center stance as a key policy issue, and suggests AI regulation may be one area of bipartisan action in a gridlocked Congress.
- Campaign advertising has sharply diminishing returns; Silver cites the case of Sam Bankman-Fried spending $10 million on an Oregon primary candidate, which backfired as voters were turned off by the flood of mail and crypto money, suggesting better investment in turnout and voter awareness.
- AI has dramatically improved Silver's model-building; he says LLMs can code complex systems like NFL playoff tiebreakers in 15 minutes versus two and a half days, though they require oversight because they can introduce hidden bugs, and he feels like he's getting two days of work done in ten hours.
- The internal future of the Democratic Party will be tested in Michigan, where progressive Abdul El-Sayed faces moderate Haley Stevens; El-Sayed's margin will be parsed as evidence for progressive vs. moderate strategy, while Adam Hamilton's race in Kansas tests whether a moderate white pastor can win in a red state.
