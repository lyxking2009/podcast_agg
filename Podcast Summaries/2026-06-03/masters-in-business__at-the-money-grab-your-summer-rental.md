## Key Points
- The summer vacation rental market is "normalizing" after pandemic-era frenzy — rents are still elevated but returning toward pre-pandemic levels, and no longer selling out instantly.
- Consumer behavior has been "Amazonified": renters now book last-minute rather than months in advance, with noticeable traffic upticks even after Memorial Day and expected again after July 4th.
- The market is K-shaped — demand is skewed toward upper-income renters and buyers, while more modest-priced second-home markets are softer due to mortgage rate pressure on middle-class consumers.
- Higher interest rates push rental prices up indirectly: would-be second-home buyers stay on the sidelines and lock in repeat renters, reducing available inventory and keeping rents firm.
- A boom in construction is visible across the Hamptons and other vacation markets, driven largely by Wall Street bonus cycles; contractors are fully booked and workers commute long distances since they cannot afford to live in the areas they serve.
- Renting before buying remains a common pathway into second-home ownership — people test a market for a season or two before purchasing nearby.
- COVID has structurally extended second-home use (remote work via Zoom), but also made demand patterns less predictable and more last-minute.
- Inventory exists but prime spots are already taken; if you want a summer rental, act now rather than waiting.

## Overview
Barry Ritholtz interviews Jonathan Miller, director of markets for Street Matrix and co-founder of Miller Samuel, about the state of the 2026 summer vacation rental market. The market has moved away from the pandemic-era frenzy and is best described as "normalizing" — rents remain high but are trending back toward pre-pandemic levels rather than hitting new records. A key structural shift is the "Amazonification" of renter behavior: consumers now book vacation rentals at the last minute, just as they order everyday goods on demand, which has made traditional early-season booking patterns obsolete. The conversation covers the Hamptons, the Jersey Shore, the Berkshires, mountain destinations, and Sun Belt escape markets, finding a consistent theme: activity is skewed toward higher-income consumers who are less sensitive to elevated mortgage rates, while more modest-priced second-home markets show relative softness. Miller emphasizes that the summer rental market is not a standalone economic indicator but rather an extension of where broader demand is already coming from — primarily well-compensated financial-sector workers.

## Implications
The "Amazonification" of vacation rentals represents a durable behavioral shift that will keep late-season inventory available longer than historically expected, reducing urgency for early bookings — but prime properties still get claimed first. The K-shaped nature of demand means that softness in the broader economy will disproportionately hit mid-market vacation destinations while luxury beach markets remain relatively insulated, as long as Wall Street bonus pools hold up. Rising interest rates create a self-reinforcing dynamic in rental markets: they deter second-home purchases, keep more properties in the rental pool under long-term agreements, and ultimately sustain or push up rental prices even as the overall frenzy subsides.
