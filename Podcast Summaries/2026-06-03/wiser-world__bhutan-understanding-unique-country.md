## Key Points
- Bhutan is one of the few nations never conquered or colonized by an outside power, preserving a cultural and political identity shaped almost entirely by Tibetan Buddhism since the 8th century
- The country remained almost entirely isolated from the outside world until the 1970s when it opened to limited tourism, and did not allow television until 1999 — the last country on earth to do so
- King Jigme Singye Wangchuck (the 4th king) coined the concept of Gross National Happiness (GNH) in the 1970s, establishing a governing philosophy that prioritizes well-being over GDP
- Bhutan is constitutionally required to maintain at least 60% forest cover, is carbon-negative, and runs almost entirely on hydroelectric power — making it one of the world's most environmentally progressive countries
- Democracy was voluntarily introduced by the 4th king in 2008, transitioning from absolute monarchy to constitutional monarchy without political pressure or revolution
- The darker side of the 4th king's reign includes the forced expulsion of over 100,000 Lhotshampa (ethnic Nepali) citizens in the late 1980s and early 1990s, widely characterized by human rights organizations as ethnic cleansing
- Bhutan faces a modern existential crisis: approximately 9% of its population — mostly young, educated workers — have emigrated, primarily to Australia, threatening the country's future workforce
- The current 5th king has responded to the emigration crisis by launching the Gelephu Mindfulness City, an ambitious sustainable development project designed by Danish architect Bjarke Ingels to create economic opportunity without sacrificing cultural values
- Special input for this episode came from Kinley Dorji (Druk Asia) and Chencho Lham (soultrips.co), both experts in Bhutanese tourism and culture

## Overview
Bhutan is a small Himalayan kingdom sandwiched between China and India that has charted one of the world's most distinctive development paths. The country's identity has been shaped by centuries of Buddhist tradition, deliberate isolation, and a governing philosophy that measures national success not by economic growth but by the happiness and well-being of its citizens. Host Alli Roper walks through Bhutan's history from its founding myths and the unifying arrival of the Zhabdrung from Tibet in the 1600s, through the establishment of the Wangchuck dynasty in 1907 and the reign of the visionary 4th king, to the contemporary challenges of emigration and modernization. The episode balances Bhutan's celebrated achievements — carbon negativity, free healthcare and education, voluntary democratization — against its less-discussed failures, including the ethnic cleansing of Lhotshampa citizens. The result is a nuanced portrait of a country that is genuinely unique but also genuinely complicated.

## Implications
Bhutan's experiment with Gross National Happiness raises fundamental questions about what development should mean for any nation: whether economic metrics like GDP are sufficient measures of societal progress, and whether a country can deliberately opt out of globalization's race to the bottom on wages, environment, and culture. The Gelephu Mindfulness City project represents a crucial test — if Bhutan can create good jobs at home without sacrificing the values that make it distinctive, it may offer a replicable model for small nations navigating the tension between modernization and cultural preservation. The emigration crisis, however, reveals a hard truth: even a country that has prioritized happiness may struggle to compete with the raw economic pull of wealthier nations.
