## Key Points
- The hallmarks of financial crises — 2008 and 2023 alike — are unbridled growth, excessive leverage, and dependence on uninsured deposits; Silicon Valley Bank doubled in size over two years before collapsing
- Private credit is not a systemic threat to the broader economy, but the rapid expansion has saddled many investors with worse liquidity and returns than expected; a sorting and consolidation of private credit managers is coming
- The US banking industry is in its best shape in decades — Citigroup's tangible common equity ratio went from ~1.75% at the GFC to over 6% today — driven by two decades of post-Dodd-Frank regulatory perimeter enforcement
- The current regulatory pivot under the Trump administration (Bessant) is not deregulation but a "regulatory reset" back to originally intended supervision, aiming for a level playing field between banks and non-banks
- Scale advantages in technology spend are widening — JP Morgan spends ~$19.8 billion on tech annually, more than most tech companies globally; community banks (97% of US banks by count) must rely on fintech providers to stay relevant
- The 2026 IPO market is already running stronger than 2021, with massive demand from passive index funds that must buy shares once companies list; but individual stock selection matters and not every offering will succeed
- The pending Clarity Act on crypto stablecoins is a key risk: if stablecoins are allowed to pay interest and act like deposits, it could disintermediate smaller banks and reduce the credit multiplier effect for Main Street
- Credit quality remains extraordinarily benign — KBW's median US bank provision estimate is ~26 basis points, below a typical credit cycle — but a normalization is inevitable and will be the biggest "torpedo in the ocean" for banks
- M&A in financial services is surging under a more permissive regulatory environment; during the Biden administration approvals took 18 months vs. 6 months historically — executives are rushing deals through now before a potential policy reversal
- Tom Michaud's three investment rules: (1) buy the best-in-class companies when they are on sale; (2) use the pendulum — be greedy when others are fearful; (3) maintain liquidity so you can buy more when prices fall further

## Overview
Tom Michaud, 40-year veteran and CEO of Keefe, Bruyette & Woods (KBW) — the leading financial services investment bank — joins Wilfred Frost in London to dissect the current state of US banking, the IPO wave, and the hidden risks lurking beneath a healthy-looking market. Michaud draws on his experience navigating the 2008 global financial crisis, the 2023 regional bank collapses, and KBW's devastating losses on 9/11 to frame what separates a routine market correction from a systemic catastrophe. He is broadly optimistic about the strength of the US banking sector — capital levels are at a two-decade high and credit metrics remain benign — but warns that a credit normalization is coming and that the frothy IPO market demands careful individual stock selection rather than indiscriminate buying. The episode takes a moving turn as Michaud recounts losing 67 colleagues on September 11, 2001, and how that experience forged the resilience — captured in the phrase "we've seen the end of times" — that has guided his leadership through every subsequent crisis.

## Implications
The episode underscores that the biggest near-term risk to financial markets is not a banking-system blowup but a creeping credit normalization that could catch investors off-guard after years of abnormally low provisions. The regulatory reset under the current US administration creates a window for bank M&A consolidation and innovation, but the incomplete legislative framework around stablecoins (the Clarity Act) represents a genuine long-term structural threat to deposit franchises, particularly for community banks. Michaud's pendulum analogy — markets are currently far closer to greed than fear, with tech at 36% of the S&P 500 — suggests that well-run, under-followed bank stocks trading at discounts to historical valuations may offer superior risk-adjusted returns compared to hyped AI IPOs.
