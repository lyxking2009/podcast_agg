# Paul Graham On Startups, Ambition, and Great Founders — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
Paul Graham reflects on YC's 47th batch and 21st year, arguing that today's YC startups are more serious—citing cancer research and intercontinental cargo drones—and that founder ambition and formidability remain the core drivers of massive success. He explains that founders are motivated daily by fear of failure, not wealth, and that AI has not changed the importance of shipping speed. He also discusses AGI as a 'smear' of capabilities rather than a clear finish line.

## Key points
- YC is now on its 47th batch and 21st year; Paul Graham recreates his startup talk from scratch each time, though core advice remains constant across technology shifts.
- YC startups today are more serious than in the 'supposedly good old days.' Examples include an intercontinental ballistic cargo startup and a cancer research startup offering on-demand research for cancer patients, which PG likens to 'death of a thousand cuts.'
- That cancer startup emerged from helping Sid Sijbrandij (GitLab co-founder) manage his own cancer; Sid approached it like a startup, wrote an essay 'founder mode on my cancer,' and the team became his de facto co-founders, now building a company to do for everyone what Sid did for himself.
- PG coined 'frighteningly ambitious' in 2012. He cites 'new Google' as an example that has been achieved by OpenAI, which was incubated at YC and began as one of Sam Altman's side projects. The key was waiting for the search model to become obsolete.
- Ambition is essential because startups are grueling; founders are driven day-to-day primarily by fear of failure (e.g., server crashing, disaster) rather than the distant prospect of wealth. Becoming a billionaire often comes as a surprise; PG has been first to tell founders they are billionaires based on valuation.
- Ambition is mostly inborn, not learned. Sam Altman was formidable from first meeting. Some founders have ambition but were trained not to show it, often due to obedience expectations from parents or school; YC helps them unleash it.
- Treating YC as a resume credential is misguided. Unlike Harvard where you can choose easy majors, startups have no easy major—it's brutally hard and inefficient for seeming cool.
- 'Formidable' (a term from Jessica Livingston and Maya's private language) means someone who gets what they want. Investors seek formidable founders because their interests align: if founders get what they want, investors get what they want.
- PG hasn't read The Lean Startup but argues startups can still start with little money because technology gets cheaper; even rocket startups can start with a design/simulation and raise incremental rounds. Philip from StarCloud wrote a white paper and booked a launch to raise funds.
- AI surprised PG by arriving as a 'full-on human but full of bullshit' (like a bullshitting undergraduate) rather than progressing from perfect fly to mouse to human. AGI is not a line but a 'smear' of capabilities; we are already on the smear, with jagged frontier (e.g., AI can solve math problems but fail restaurant hours).
- Shipping speed remains the best predictor of startup success, even with AI. Many startups still ship too slowly; the only new thing is giant AI/token bills (tens of thousands of dollars a day) replacing salaries as main cost.
- YC advantages include having colleagues, learning from other startups' solutions, and the 'YC GDP'—selling to other batch startups who are early adopters and decide quickly.
- YC originated from idea of an angel firm with standardized paperwork; to learn investing, they funded many startups at once, targeting undergrads as alternative to summer jobs. The batch length (summer) was accident but perfect. YC has changed little: bigger but divided into pods of ~70 startups preserving old feel.
- Next trillion-dollar company will come from formidable founders with good ideas, not a specific sector. Founders 20 years from now will look like they do today (not robots).
