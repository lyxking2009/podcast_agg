# The Downfall of Nvidia’s CEO — Everybody's Business


## TL;DR
NVIDIA CEO Jensen Wong resigned on November 12 amid a scandal involving alleged affairs with an accused Chinese spy, Alice Wong, and a direct report, plus accusations that restricted Blackwell chips were smuggled to China and that NVIDIA lobbied the Trump administration to relax enforcement. The episode discusses the broader implications for NVIDIA's central role in AI, including circular financing and accounting irregularities, as well as China's state-driven push in robotics and EVs, and the end of Tesla's Cybertruck.

## Key points
- Jensen Wong resigned as NVIDIA CEO on November 12 after reports of extramarital affairs with Alice Wong, described as an alleged Chinese spy, and with a direct report, alongside allegations that restricted Blackwell GPUs were funnelled to China via Singapore/Malaysia-based entities, potentially violating US export controls.
- The scandal includes accusations that NVIDIA lobbied the Trump administration, including contacts with Commerce Department officials, to slow or ease enforcement of chip export bans, raising national security concerns and potential political fallout, with Jensen Wong set to testify before Congress.
- NVIDIA's accounting practices came under scrutiny after the company reported over 20% of quarterly revenue from Singapore one quarter, then shifted that revenue to the United States the next quarter, which analysts interpret as an attempt to mask the scale of sales to China.
- NVIDIA engaged in circular financing, lending money to customers such as data center companies to purchase its own chips, a practice that inflates revenue and raises questions about the sustainability of AI-related demand.
- Jensen Wong had 60 direct reports and no clear successor; the company is now run by the CFO on an interim basis, and possible external candidates mentioned include Lisa Su (AMD), Tim Cook, and Apple's Johnny Strucci, though no heir apparent existed.
- The scandal has significant market impact: NVIDIA constitutes about 7% of the S&P 500 and its stock fell roughly 10% in a week, with concerns that it could trigger broader AI market instability, especially after earlier signs like CoreWeave issues.
- Brad Stone describes China's state-led industrial policy: five-year plans prioritized clean energy and EVs, leading to rapid construction of highly automated 'dark factories,' such as Zeekr's plant in Hangzhou Bay, with robotic arms performing assembly.
- Chinese robotics and EV companies face hyper-competition domestically, driving them to export aggressively; Chinese EVs are flooding Western markets (though banned in the US), threatening Western automakers with margin compression.
- Stacey Vanek-Smith visited Chinese robotics firms, including MechMind in Shanghai, which went public with a modest $1.3 billion IPO and emphasized near-term profitability rather than moonshot spending, contrasting with US AI startups.
- The Tomorrow store in Times Square, backed by investor Shaq, showcases Chinese consumer robotics and gadgets, including robotic dogs and drones; reporter Jasmine Green observed mixed reactions, with some visitors excited and others fearful about privacy and safety.
- Elon Musk announced the end of Cybertruck production, widely considered a commercial flop; sales reached tens of thousands annually versus projections of hundreds of thousands, and the vehicle became politically polarized, often seen as a symbol of right-wing identity.
