# Why a $2 Billion Law Firm Suddenly Collapsed — Odd Lots


## TL;DR
The episode examines the sudden collapse of Weil, Gotshal & Manges (referred to as Weill Gottschall in the transcript), a ~95-year-old elite bankruptcy law firm. John Morley, Yale Law School professor, explains that despite rising profits, Weil lagged rivals like Kirkland & Ellis in profits per partner growth, triggering rainmaker departures and a partner run. Morley details how partnership ownership creates fraudulent transfer liability and generational conflict, contrasting with investor-owned Slater & Gordon which survived a crisis.

## Key points
- John Morley's research predicted that a law firm can collapse even when profits are rising, if its relative profitability falls behind peers; Weil's profits per partner ranking dropped from 9th in 2020 to 20th in 2025, making partners more receptive to outside offers.
- Law firms are partnerships because they require little capital and because state ethics rules (Model Rules of Professional Conduct) generally prohibit non-lawyer ownership, except in Arizona and Utah; Henry Hansmann argued worker ownership works because lawyer productivity is measurable through billable hours and client origination.
- When a rainmaker leaves, revenues can fall faster than costs (leases, staff, pension obligations), immediately reducing profits for remaining partners; this makes lateral offers relatively more attractive, creating a 'partner run' analogous to a bank run.
- Partners are owners, not employees, so profit distributions can be clawed back as fraudulent transfers if the firm is later deemed insolvent; this risk causes partners to discount future compensation and leave preemptively to avoid clawback liability.
- Weil's strategic mistake was staying mid-sized while big law is bifurcating into very large or very small firms; clients demand large specialized platforms, and the departure of private equity rainmaker Mike Aiello to Cravath was the final straw.
- Non-compete agreements for lawyers are prohibited by ABA Model Rule 5.6, so partners can leave and take clients with them; this also deters outside investors because a purchased law firm's key assets could walk away, though forfeiture conditions on MSO equity are a workaround.
- Investor-owned law firm Slater & Gordon went public in Australia and later acquired a fraudulent business (Quindell), but survived insolvency because the lawyers were not owners and could not withdraw capital; equity was wiped out while operations continued, unlike partnership firms.
- Generational conflict arises because partner equity disappears at retirement, so older partners underinvest in long-term projects; Morley predicts law firms will rent AI rather than develop it, and only giant firms like Kirkland & Ellis and Latham can make modest AI investments spread over time.
- Law firm culture has eroded as lockstep compensation and familial norms give way to eat-what-you-kill and lateral movement; Morley compares this to broader modernity, citing Nick Christakis's 'mild hierarchy' concept and noting that Ramona Nee not becoming chair was a crippling loss of confidence.
- Weil's partners voted to dissolve the firm quickly to minimize fraudulent transfer liability; partners likely lose about 50% of their annual compensation (the January payout) to creditors, primarily landlords, and no bankruptcy is expected because there are few creditors.
