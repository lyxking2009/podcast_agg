# The avalanche that hit math is coming for AI development — Dwarkesh Podcast


## TL;DR
Dwarkesh argues that AI is on track to automate AI research within a few years—his median is 2028, with a 25% chance after 2032 and a possibility of 2027. He draws on explosive progress in mathematics, where models have gone from failing arithmetic to solving a Millennium Prize problem, and argues that AI research has similarly verifiable proxies (pre-training loss, RL environment scores, inference efficiency). Once automated researchers exceed top humans, speed, smartness, and parallelization could compress years of progress into months, leading either to a software-only singularity or a 'spark' scenario.

## Key points
- Anthropic's rate of AI progress on well-scoped tasks is now 2x what it was a year ago, according to a three-month embedded evaluation by Meter; a majority of that acceleration is attributed to AI use. Anthropic also reported that Claude now leads 26% of its AI R&D work, up from under 1% in February.
- Mathematical capabilities have exploded: as recently as 2023 models couldn't reliably add four-digit numbers, in 2024 they could barely solve high school math competition problems, in 2025 they won gold at the International Math Olympiad, and a month ago OpenAI solved a Millennium Prize problem in 88 hours.
- Current AI math progress is disproportionately good at verification-heavy tasks like finding counterexamples, but weak at asking profound questions or founding new fields; however, AI research prioritizes practical, empirically testable improvements over theory, making it a better fit for these AI strengths.
- The transformer architecture could have been invented earlier: it was a combination of known ideas (attention, residual networks) with many small tweaks; AIs excel at persistently trying many combinations and connecting adjacent domain ideas, which is exactly what transformer invention required.
- AI research has strong verifiable proxies: pre-training can be improved by minimizing held-out test loss, post-training by measuring sample efficiency and RL environment scores (e.g., Anthropic's CoBench with 500 real problems), and inference efficiency by metrics like GPU utilization, KV cache size, and latency.
- Dwarkesh's median estimate for when automated AI researchers will match the best human AI researchers is 2028, assuming labs go full speed without government slowdown; he gives a 25% chance it happens after 2032 and thinks it could be as early as 2027 based on straightforward takeoff models.
- Once automated researchers are as good as top humans, progress accelerates for three reasons: they work about 10x human speed and 24/7 (roughly 50x more work per week), every one is at top-researcher level (where ~80% of progress comes from ~50 people), and they can be massively parallelized across independent subproblems with verifiable checkpoints.
- Dwarkesh assigns roughly 50% probability to a software-only singularity (where recursive self-improvement continues) and 50% to a 'spark' scenario (3-6 years of current-rate progress compressed into one year, then slowdown). Even the spark implies a jump as large as GPT-3.5 to GPT-6 Astra in 12 months.
- Post-spark AIs would be superhuman in verifiable real-world domains: better than Nvidia at designing AI accelerators, more profitable than Citadel/Jane Street if given market access, and better at finding room-temp superconductors with lab access; they can ramp up quickly via RL-trained general skills and simulated environments.
- Future RL training will use tens of thousands of times more environments—large, open-ended, and covering economies, science history, politics—to teach general skills like planning, information seeking, and collaboration, enabling transfer to hard-to-verify real-world tasks.
- Bottlenecks include continual learning, finding verifiable proxies, data, and experiment compute. Dwarkesh argues continual learning may be solved via scaling in-context learning, proxies are paradigm-agnostic, data can be synthesized by AIs, and compute can be used far more efficiently via small-scale experiments and scaling laws (e.g., MuP).
