# Why Money Launderers Love $100 Bills — Odd Lots

## TL;DR

Oliver Bullough discusses the paradox of rising cash circulation dominated by $100 bills, revealing money laundering's scale of $2-5 trillion annually and varied methods including cash smuggling, trade-based laundering, carousel fraud, and crypto. He argues current anti-money-laundering regulations cost $200 billion yearly but fail to stop criminals, while the US profits from seigniorage and $100 bills enable global crime.

## Key points

- Global money laundering is estimated at 2-5% of global GDP, roughly $2-5 trillion annually, based on a late-1990s IMF estimate by Michel Camdessus; this share has not declined despite decades of anti-money-laundering efforts.
- AML compliance costs financial institutions about $200 billion per year (LexisNexis estimate), but the system is bureaucratic and ineffective, generating millions of suspicious activity reports that overwhelm law enforcement and rarely lead to prosecutions.
- The paradox of banknotes: cash demand for transactions is low (9% in UK, 13% in US), yet cash in circulation is at record highs—$2.5 trillion in US, €1.6 trillion in Eurozone, £100 billion in UK—because high-denomination bills like $100 are used for illicit activities.
- 85% of US currency value is in $100 bills; the average American holds about $430 cash while over $7,000 per person exists, indicating large amounts are held outside normal household use, likely for crime and tax evasion.
- The $100 bill is the preferred instrument for criminals due to its high value density and global acceptance; proposals to eliminate it face collective action problems because other countries still issue large notes (e.g., €200) and the US benefits from seigniorage.
- Trade-based money laundering is estimated at $1 trillion annually by Global Financial Integrity; it moves value through goods, e.g., Mexican cartels send drugs to the US and receive cash and goods like tractors, while Chinese networks use luxury goods to move money out of China.
- The 'Vancouver model' links Chinese demand for moving money out of China with drug cartel cash supply: cash is handed to Chinese gamblers who lose it in casinos, creating debts repaid via drug shipments, enabling value transfer without cross-border money movement.
- Carousel fraud exploits VAT zero-rating on intra-EU imports: import goods without paying VAT, sell to another shell company charging VAT, export and claim refund, while the importer never pays VAT—creating 20% of shipment value from thin air; it costs Europe €50 billion annually.
- Seigniorage is the profit from issuing currency; the US has $2.5 trillion in circulation essentially as an interest-free loan to the government, saving significant interest costs and creating incentive not to phase out $100 bills.
- Crypto, especially stablecoins, is increasingly used for money laundering, often combined with cash: street-level cash is converted to crypto for international movement, supercharging both methods and allowing instantaneous value transfer.
- The Medici banking system of moving value via trade rather than physically moving bullion is analogous to modern trade-based money laundering networks, showing the long history of value transfer through goods.
- Historical political will (e.g., Wright Patman's role in the 1970 Bank Secrecy Act) is lacking today; current system overburdens banks with compliance costs and fines while under-resourcing law enforcement, creating perverse incentives.
- Suspicious activity reports were intended as real-time intelligence but are mostly unread; reform should focus on fewer, well-analyzed reports and actual prosecution to take profit out of crime, not just generate paperwork.
- Central banks officially attribute rising cash to 'store of value' but surveys contradict this, and the true explanation is largely illicit use; eliminating large bills would require international cooperation that has so far been elusive.
