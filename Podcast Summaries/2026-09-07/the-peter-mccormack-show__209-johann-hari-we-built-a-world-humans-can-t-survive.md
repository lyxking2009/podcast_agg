# #209 - Johann Hari - We Built A World Humans Can’t Survive — The Peter McCormack Show

## TL;DR

Johann Hari and Peter McCormack discuss how modern life degrades attention, fuels negativity, and fails psychological needs, from AI's impact on jobs to social media algorithms that reward outrage. Hari argues the attention crisis stems from constant interruptions, with studies showing texts cause 23-minute focus recovery and interruptions lower IQ more than cannabis. They critique the factory-model education system and advocate for progressive learning, banning phones in schools, and replacing surveillance capitalism with subscription or public models. Peter shares his own struggle with podcast negativity and the pull of clickbait, while Hari offers solutions like social prescribing, free play, and intrinsic values to build a healthier, more connected world.

## Key points

- Peter McCormack opens reflecting at age 47 that humans are built for scarcity but now live in abundance; he feels more disconnected despite technology, and his son watching Mid90s called it a 'cool time to live,' prompting Peter's concern about what world they've built for kids.
- Johann Hari says rising depression, anxiety, addiction, and attention problems are primarily because modern culture fails to meet deep psychological needs: belonging, meaning, purpose, being seen and valued, and a future that makes sense.
- Hari recounts a Monaco pitch where an AI wedding planner reduces a task that took five people two weeks to one person one day, illustrating how AI could eliminate many jobs; Peter mentions predictions of 50% job loss within two years to AI working 24/7 for $20/day.
- Hari emphasizes focus as the foundation of human achievement, but attention is collapsing; AI will supercharge the tech assault on attention, with TikTok's algorithm already more addictive.
- Neuroscientist Earl Miller at MIT says humans can only consciously think about one or two things at a time; futile multitasking leads to the 'switch cost effect' with more mistakes, poorer memory, and less creativity.
- An HP study split workers: those interrupted by email and calls scored 10 IQ points lower than uninterrupted workers; short-term cannabis use lowers IQ by 5 points, so constant interruptions are twice as harmful to intelligence.
- Professor Michael Posner found a mere text message interruption costs on average 23 minutes to return to prior focus; most people never get 23 uninterrupted minutes, so they operate at permanently degraded cognitive levels.
- The education system was designed in the 1870s to produce factory workers, teaching obedience and tolerance of boredom; it now deadens curiosity and prepares kids to be robots at a time when actual robots excel at memorization.
- Hari criticizes South Korea's PISA-topping system where children study from 8am to 10pm, exhaust themselves, and still can't speak English despite years of rote learning; Peter's daughter spent weeks memorizing facts for GCSEs despite having a supercomputer in her pocket.
- Progressive models like Berlin's Evangela Zentrum have children choose curiosity questions each term and spend half their lessons applying subjects to answer them, boosting engagement; James Cameron's school lets each child pick one subject per term.
- Lenore Skenazy's Let Grow program restores free play and independence; a 14-year-old boy who had never been outside alone built a fort in the woods, showing that children naturally seek autonomy and challenge when given freedom.
- Social media operates on surveillance capitalism: apps are free but harvest attention and sell it to advertisers; algorithms optimize for time-on-screen, not user wellbeing, leading to negativity bias because outrage keeps people scrolling longer.
- Former Google designer James Williams told an audience of tech designers, 'If there's anyone here who wants to live in the world that we're creating, please put up your hand,' and nobody did; he quit to become an attention philosopher.
- Hari argues the solution is to ban surveillance capitalism like leaded petrol was banned; moving to subscription or public ownership models would align incentives with user satisfaction rather than advertiser profit.
- Leaded petrol analogy: British moms led by Jill Runette fought the lead industry and won, raising average child IQ by 3-5 points; similarly, citizens can force tech companies to change their business model.
- Facebook's own internal research leaked by Frances Haugen showed one-third of German neo-Nazi group joiners came via algorithm recommendations; a court found Meta responsible for harming a young woman, marking a 'big tobacco moment.'
- Peter confesses he feels trapped by podcast incentives: rage-bait titles get 1.3 million views while substantive topics might get 200,000; he questions whether he is a 'conflict entrepreneur' and wants to stop feeding the negativity machine.
- Johann introduces Tim Kasser's research: the more people value money, status, and appearance, the worse their mental health; he uses the term 'junk values' analogous to junk food, and Nathan Dungan's program showed materialism can drop by focusing on meaning and connection.
- Social prescribing, pioneered by GP Sam Everington, treats depression by connecting patients to meaningful group activities like gardening; a Norway study found it twice as effective as antidepressants for teen anxiety.
- Johann closes by citing historical progress—from gay marriage being unthinkable in 1994 to Supreme Court recognition—as evidence that collective action can overcome seemingly impossible odds; Peter resolves to post more authentic content and mentions his cat post got 19,000 likes, showing people crave kindness.
