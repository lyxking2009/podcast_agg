# Uneasy Money: The $388M Bitget Hack Started With a Security Vendor

## Overview

Kain Warwick, Taylor Monahan and Austin Griffith are joined by Pablo Sabbatella, founder of Opsek and a member of
Arbitrum's Security Council, to dissect the $388 million Bitget theft. The framing point is that the attackers never
obtained private keys: the way in was a zero-day in a third-party security product the exchange relied on. Monahan
attributes the operation to North Korea's TraderTraitor group and highlights how quickly the stolen funds were moved
off Arbitrum. That leads the panel into an extended argument about neutrality - why THORChain, which has halted its
chain and reallocated balances in the past, still handles stolen funds without facing the scrutiny Tornado Cash
attracted. The back half widens out to NEAR's SHIELD behavioural anomaly detection, reports of rogue AI agents
attacking external systems, whether retail investors should get the upside of a $2 trillion Anthropic IPO, and
Vitalik Buterin's 2030 roadmap for Ethereum.

## Key Points

- The Bitget attack required no private keys: the entry point was a zero-day in a third-party security product the exchange depended on, which the attackers used to authorise fraudulent withdrawals.
- Taylor Monahan attributes the hack to North Korea's TraderTraitor group and emphasises how rapidly the funds were moved off Arbitrum.
- The panel's sharpest argument is about neutrality: THORChain has previously halted its chain and reallocated balances, so its refusal to intervene here is a choice rather than a design constraint - and it has escaped the scrutiny applied to Tornado Cash.
- Sabbatella argues that security councils restart the freeze debate every time this happens, which is itself a governance problem rather than a technical one.
- Kain Warwick's position: THORChain cannot claim to be like Bitcoin and Ethereum if it selectively intervenes.
- NEAR's SHIELD is presented as an alternative approach - behavioural anomaly detection to block funds rather than chain-level halting.
- Wider coverage: rogue AI agents hacking outside systems as their labs eye IPOs; whether retail should share in the upside of a $2 trillion Anthropic IPO; and Vitalik Buterin's 2030 roadmap for Ethereum.
- Warwick's closing argument: the harder the problem, the more superintelligence helps - and Ethereum is one of the hardest problems there is.

## Implications

The episode's contribution is the neutrality argument rather than the forensic detail available elsewhere: if a chain
is willing to intervene sometimes, the refusal to intervene other times becomes a discretionary decision with
accountability attached. That reframes ‘permissionless’ from a technical property into a governance claim that can be
tested. For recovery practitioners, the practical point is that loss recovery after a hack now depends more on the
policy posture of bridge and stablecoin issuers than on the exchange's own incident response. This record is built from
