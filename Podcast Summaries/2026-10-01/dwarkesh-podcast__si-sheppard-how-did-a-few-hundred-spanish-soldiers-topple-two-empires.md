# Si Sheppard - How did a few hundred Spanish soldiers topple two empires?

## Overview

Dwarkesh Patel interviews military historian Si Sheppard about the Spanish conquests of the Aztec and Inca
empires - arguably the most shocking military and diplomatic events in human history. A few hundred conquistadors
arrived on a continent they knew almost nothing about; within about two and a half years Cortes had subjugated an
Aztec empire of some six million people, and a decade later Pizarro did the same to an Incan empire of roughly ten
million. In both cases the Spanish repeatedly won battles while outnumbered 100 to 1, sometimes 1000 to 1, often
without taking a single casualty - and with as much as 99% of their fighting forces made up of the very peoples they
were subjugating. Sheppard traces the arc from Portuguese navigation and Columbus through Cortes's political reading of
Totonac resentment, the Tlaxcalan alliance, and Pizarro's gamble, and asks why the Aztecs and Incas failed to learn
from each other. The episode closes on why Spain still lost in the long run, and Patel's explicit framing of the AI
takeover analogy.

## Key Points

- Scale of the conquests: Cortes conquered an Aztec empire of about six million people within roughly two and a half years, with a force numbering in the hundreds; a decade later Pizarro repeated the feat against an Incan empire of some ten million.
- The conquistadors consistently won while outnumbered by factors of 100 to 1, and in some cases 1000 to 1, often without casualties - and with up to 99% of their forces composed of native allies they had recruited along the way.
- Cortes's decisive asset was political intelligence rather than firepower: he recognised early that the Totonacs chafed under Mexica (Aztec) imperial authority and turned that resentment into an alliance.
- The Tlaxcalans initially fought Cortes to a standstill, then switched sides and became guides and reinforcements for the march inland - a pattern that repeated across the campaign.
- Explanations for Spanish military superiority in the episode include steel, horses, ships and above all diplomacy - playing subjugated peoples against imperial centres.
- The Aztecs and Incas did not learn from each other despite both being subject to the same alien force, which Sheppard attributes to closed epistemic worlds.
- Spain still lost in the long run: the demographic tide reversed the conquest, a dynamic repeated later in Australia and New Zealand as colonisers became the minority in their own countries.
- Sheppard stresses that diplomacy mattered as much as technology, and that the Mughal experience in India shows the same dynamic failing to be exploited.

## Implications

The episode is really about how a small, technologically superior but numerically trivial force can topple a much
larger and older order using local fracture lines - which is why Patel frames it against AI takeover scenarios. Three
transferable observations: superior technology alone was insufficient without local allies; closed systems that cannot
learn from each other's fate are structurally vulnerable; and short-run conquest tells you nothing about long-run
outcomes, because demographic and cultural tides run on a much slower clock than military ones. This record is built
