# Tom Cruise-a-Palooza — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
Critics at Large hosts Alex Schwartz, Naomi Fry, and Vinson Cunningham are joined by New Yorker editor Tyler Foggatt to examine Tom Cruise's 40-year career, from early heartthrob roles like Risky Business and Top Gun to acclaimed dramas like Jerry Maguire and Magnolia, and his current dominance as an action star. They discuss his new film Digger, directed by Alejandro Iñárritu, where Cruise gives a bravura performance in a divisive satire, and debate whether Cruise is the last true movie star in an era of IP-driven franchises and shifting Hollywood economics.

## Key points
- Tyler Foggatt, a senior editor at The New Yorker and self-described 'Cruisehead,' organizes Tom Cruise's filmography into four buckets: early heartthrob years (Risky Business, Top Gun, Days of Thunder), critically acclaimed dramatic roles (Born on the Fourth of July, Jerry Maguire, Magnolia), action franchise era (Mission: Impossible, Top Gun: Maverick), and a 'secret fourth bucket' of weird experiments like Interview with the Vampire and Tropic Thunder.
- Cruise's early appeal was built on a combination of sex appeal and aspirational masculinity: his Risky Business dance scene and Ray-Ban Aviators sold out, Top Gun boosted Navy enlistment, and roles like The Color of Money showcased a charismatic cockiness that resonated with both male and female audiences.
- Foggatt highlights Cruise's underrated comedic talent, citing Jerry Maguire's 'You complete me' line, which Cameron Crowe initially thought too corny but Cruise delivered with enough sincerity to move the crew to tears, and his turn as men's rights guru Frank T.J. Mackey in Magnolia, which anticipated later cultural discourse.
- Despite three Oscar nominations (Born on the Fourth of July, Jerry Maguire, Magnolia), Cruise has never won; after the biopic Valkyrie (2008) failed to earn a nomination, he shifted decisively into action films, collaborating with Christopher McQuarrie, whom he met on Valkyrie's set, to perfect stunt-driven blockbusters.
- The hosts trace Cruise's Scientology controversies in the mid-2000s—the Oprah couch jump, the 'You're Being Glib, Matt' interview with Matt Lauer where he called psychiatry a pseudoscience—and note that while War of the Worlds was a hit, Mission: Impossible III underperformed, leading Cruise to retreat into the heroic Ethan Hunt persona.
- Foggatt connects Cruise's Scientology beliefs to his on-screen savior complex, referencing a leaked interview where Cruise claimed he could heal car crash victims using a 'touch assist' technique, and argues he enjoys playing characters who save the world, which may have culminated in his claim to have saved the movie industry with Top Gun: Maverick.
- In Digger, directed by Alejandro Iñárritu, Cruise plays Digger Rockwell, an oil tycoon whose Greenland rig explodes, causing a massive ice shelf to threaten Europe; the film reveals it is actually a play, with Cruise playing actor Curtis Markson, and the hosts praise Cruise's physical transformation and performance but criticize the satire as heavy-handed and outdated.
- Vinson Cunningham appreciated Digger's Brechtian layers, noting the film's deliberate falseness gave him a 'cathartic conceptual release' when the play-within-a-film twist was revealed, while Nomi Fry found it audacious but imperfect, and Alex Schwartz called it a 'bad, bad movie' despite Cruise's excellent work.
- The discussion turns to whether Cruise is the last movie star; the hosts agree that structural changes—streaming, IP franchises, the decline of mid-budget movies—have made it harder for new stars to emerge, though they note exceptions like Zendaya, Glen Powell, and Robert Pattinson.
- Cruise himself is invested in mentoring successors: he reportedly sent Timothée Chalamet an email offering contacts for stunt coordinators and helicopter pilots after Dune, indicating he does not want to be the last of his kind.
- The hosts reflect on the anxiety surrounding the death of the movie star, suggesting audiences crave a 'handhold' of a reliable star, but also point to the success of Gen Z horror films with unknown casts as evidence that Hollywood can thrive without traditional star power.
