# Everything in Markets Is Now Moving Incredibly Fast

## Overview

Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway record a state-of-the-markets episode with Luke Kawa, head of markets at
Sherwood News and a former Bloomberg colleague. The organising observation is velocity: a week of war headlines,
model releases and bond-market moves now get absorbed in hours, and personnel news that would once have dominated a
trading day barely breaks through. Kawa supplies the numbers behind the unease - a market half a percent from a record
with barely half its stocks above their 200-day moving average - and the three debate whether the market is genuinely
inured to higher rates or simply betting everything on one factor. Consumer names and cyclicals are the casualty;
dispersion, not direction, is the defining feature of 2026.

## Key Points

- Breadth is the tell: coming into the week the S&P 500 was 0.5% off a record high while only 51.2% of stocks were above their 200-day moving average - the closest-to-high, fewest-above-the-200-day combination since the day after the dot-com bubble peak.
- Kawa immediately qualifies it: breadth was also poor in 1998 and the market rallied for another two years, so breadth is a warning, not a signal.
- The market is not uniformly inert to higher rates - equal-weight S&P 500 and the Russell 2000 certainly are not. What is holding the index up is a concentrated bet that the AI impulse continues to swallow the economy until credit conditions, lending capacity or regulation stops it.
- Hyperscaler credit spreads are not at their year wides at the long end, following a September with essentially zero hyperscaler USIG issuance - after the market pushed back on capex.
- Dispersion is the defining market structure of 2026: Helene Meisler's “either-or market”; Kawa's “Jerry's friends, only one can do well at the right time.” Early in the year it was semis versus software; more recently it is inertia stocks versus AI beneficiaries, with multiple layers of the AI stack benefiting and others getting hit.
- Consumer-facing names are persistently weak - Nike cited from around $180 in 2021 to roughly $35 now; McDonald's peaked in early March and has fallen since.
- The meta-point: so much is happening that nothing specific moves the market any more. Even a major Wall Street personnel move barely registered.

## Implications

This is a compact statement of the market's current regime: narrow leadership, high dispersion, and a single
dominant factor absorbing all the macro noise. The practical read for practitioners is that index-level calm is being
purchased with concentration risk, and that breadth measures deserve attention precisely because they are diverging
from price. The 1998 caveat is important and is stated on the episode - breadth was similarly poor then and the rally
continued for two years, so this is a risk indicator rather than a timing tool. Built from the full Omny transcript.
