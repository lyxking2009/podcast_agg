# These Are the Sharps Actually Making Money on Prediction Markets

## Key Points

- A private Discord called the "Maga Kiwi Club" (ironically named) operates as a collaborative community of specialized prediction market traders, each with distinct domain expertise, sharing information in exchange for reciprocal help
- Brian Golden built an Excel model replicating the BLS inflation calculation formula from scratch and consistently outperforms Bloomberg consensus inflation forecasts as a single individual with a drama undergraduate degree
- The group conducts original on-the-ground research including phone polling and door-to-door canvassing for election markets, outperforming institutional forecasters in several notable races
- 67% of profits on Polymarket flow to 0.1% of accounts — the structure mirrors poker's "soft tables" dynamic, with minnows eventually leaving once they realize the edge differential
- Election markets remain persistently mispriced due to siloed media ecosystems and emotional attachment to outcomes; the 2025 LA mayor's race (Spencer Pratt priced at 27% to win a D+42 city) is a prime example
- The Romanian 2025 election was a notable loss for the group — domestic Romanian traders had information advantages the international sharps lacked
- AI tools are useful for international market research and language translation but asking LLMs to predict outcomes provides no edge since their training data reflects the same beatable expert consensus
- Resolution mechanisms differ critically: Polymarket's decentralized oracle has been undermined, while Kalshi's centralized resolution is generally reliable for binary events like elections and cabinet confirmations

## Overview

Joe Weisenthal and Tracy Alloway host a special episode featuring NYT journalist Adam alongside two sharp prediction market traders — Brian Golden (inflation specialist) and Daniel Reichman, who goes by "Carnitas Taco" (elections and politics specialist). The conversation grew from Adam's New York Times piece "The Average Guys Out-Smarting Wall Street on Prediction Markets." The episode examines how a small community of rigorous researchers consistently generate positive expected value in markets that most participants treat as gambling. Brian rebuilt the BLS formula in Excel over three months, calling public servants at the Bureau of Labor Statistics to understand the math, and now produces inflation forecasts that beat major investment bank consensus. Daniel's elections team builds precinct-level models that can call winners in real time as vote batches arrive. The conversation covers market efficiency, insider trading, the decline of "soft money" as professionals enter, and the structural differences between prediction markets and traditional financial markets.

## Implications

- The edge in prediction markets mirrors the edge in poker: domain expertise, original research, emotional discipline, and community information-sharing — not AI or financial sophistication
- Expert consensus (Wall Street inflation forecasts, media election coverage) is systematically beatable because it carries no financial consequence for being wrong — an insight that should trouble anyone who relies on professional forecasts
- Prediction market integrity depends heavily on resolution mechanism design; Polymarket's oracle problems and Kalshi's centralized approach represent two different risk profiles for traders
- The "soft money" dynamic from online poker likely applies: as more professionals enter prediction markets, returns will compress and the accessible edge will migrate to new, less liquid markets
- The Romanian election loss illustrates a systematic blind spot: international sharps lack the local knowledge that domestic participants have in foreign elections
