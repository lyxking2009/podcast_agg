# Critics' Cult Classics: Hanif Abdurraqib on Camp Lo's "Uptown Saturday Night" — Critics at Large | The New Yorker

## TL;DR
In the first entry of a new "Critics' Cult Classics" interview series, host Vinson Cunningham talks with New Yorker contributing writer and diehard Camp Lo fan Hanif Abdurraqib about the duo's 1997 album "Uptown Saturday Night." They dig into why the record — which leaned hard on Blaxploitation-movie style to paint a cinematic vision of New York — thrilled some listeners while confounding others and never quite broke through commercially. Abdurraqib frames the album as "essentially a heist film" in musical form, and traces its lukewarm reception to a late-'90s hip-hop marketplace that demanded artists pick a lane between underground authenticity and mainstream opulence, a binary Camp Lo refused to honor.

## Key points
- The episode launches a new recurring format for Critics at Large: interview episodes devoted to resurfacing beloved cult-classic albums, films, and books, with Camp Lo's "Uptown Saturday Night" as the inaugural pick.
- Abdurraqib, a longtime and vocal fan of the Camp Lo project, walks through why the January 1997 album landed as a strange, singular object at the time of its release — too stylistically idiosyncratic to fit neatly into either the underground-conscious or hyper-commercial camps that were hardening in hip-hop during that era.
- He describes the record as "essentially a heist film" unto itself, pointing to its debt to 1970s Blaxploitation cinema — citing touchstones like "Uptown Saturday Night" (1974), "Midnight Express" (1978), and "The Black Connection" (1974) — for its slang-dense, cinematic sense of place and its vision of New York City as stage and getaway car at once.
- Cunningham and Abdurraqib discuss the album's "lukewarm reception" on release and argue that its ambiguous positioning — refusing to commit to either underground grit or unapologetic mainstream shine — is precisely what caused it to "get lost" commercially even as it built a devoted cult following over time.
- The conversation situates "Uptown Saturday Night" alongside adjacent touchstones discussed on the show, including the Fugees' "The Score," Dynasty's "Adventures in the Land of Music," Slum Village's "Fan-Tas-Tic (Vol. 1)," Puff Daddy's "No Way Out," and the later Pete Rock and Camp Lo collaboration "80 Blocks from Tiffany's," tracing a lineage of records that blurred underground and mainstream hip-hop identities.
- They also touch on Camp Lo's later catalog, including "Black Hollywood," and draw a contrast with acts like Jay-Z ("American Gangster") and Wyclef Jean ("The Carnival") who more explicitly straddled or crossed into commercial hip-hop, versus Camp Lo's refusal to change who they were even as the industry pushed toward hyper-commercialization.
- Elliott Wilson's contemporaneous review of the album in The Source is referenced as part of the critical record documenting how the project was received (and often misunderstood) at the time.
