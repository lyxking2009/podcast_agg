# Garry Tan: Own Your Intelligence — Y Combinator Startup Podcast

## TL;DR
In this Startup School 2026 keynote, YC President & CEO Garry Tan argues that AGI isn't arriving as a single dramatic event but is already here in diffuse form as "personal AGI" — an AI agent that runs on infrastructure you own, reads from a memory library you control, and executes procedures ("skill files") you wrote, compounding daily instead of resetting like a rented chatbot subscription. He walks through his own system (a ~220,000-page personal markdown knowledge base called GBrain plus an agentic coding harness), gives a five-step how-to for building a personal version, and warns that whoever controls your skill files controls your compounded judgment — so founders and workers alike should own their own repos rather than let employers extract them.

## Key points
- Opens with the story of philosopher Baruch Spinoza's 1656 excommunication to frame his central metaphor: institutions try to control intelligence/thought, and the same dynamic now applies to AI.
- Defines "personal AGI" as an agent running on your own infrastructure, reading a memory you own, and executing procedures you wrote — contrasted with "corporate AGI" (a rented chatbot subscription) that resets when you close the tab and never truly compounds.
- Claims his own coding output is roughly 400x his 2013 baseline as a YC partner (and still at least ~8x even under the most skeptical discounting), and argues the same multiplier applies to design, product management, growth, and other knowledge work, not just code.
- Cites YC portfolio data: a quarter of the Winter '25 batch had codebases that were 95% AI-generated, and singles out two portfolio companies (Emergent, Retell) that reached unusually high revenue-per-headcount by building "natively" on agent workflows from day one.
- Describes his personal system, GBrain: a ~220,000-page personal markdown knowledge wiki documenting ~25 years of his life (email, meetings, notes), used by agents to triage his inbox overnight, prep meeting briefs, and run overnight research.
- Explains "skill files" — plain-English markdown instructions that an agent can execute — coining the phrase "markdown is code" and "fat skills, thin harness."
- Frames the overall stack as three layers: a rented, commoditized frontier model + an owned personal context library + a harness (e.g., OpenClaw, Hermes Agent, Claude Code, Codex) wiring them together.
- Lays out a five-step how-to for listeners: (1) pick an agent harness and run it locally, (2) start a personal markdown library of notes/projects/people, (3) write your first skill file for a hated recurring task, (4) turn it into a scheduled/recurring job, (5) "skillify" every task afterward so nothing is ever done just once.
- Warns about who controls skill files using a hypothetical support-engineer example: if an employee's skill files live in a company repo, the company can keep running that judgment after the employee leaves; if they live in the employee's own repo, the judgment (and the potential business) stays with them.
- Addresses three anticipated objections: rapid model improvement won't obsolete personal context (better models extract more value from the same library); it's not "just RAG" (retrieval is the easy part — curation, provenance, and contradiction-checking are the hard part); and consolidating one's life into one system isn't a new privacy risk but a way to take custody of data already scattered across corporate clouds.
- Explains why he open-sourced his entire personal-AI stack (GBrain, harness, skills): a belief that "tools of the powerful should be given away" to avoid AI becoming controlled by a narrow "priesthood."
- Closes with an anecdote about a friend who built an 80,000-file personal knowledge system to track every detail of his son's rare epilepsy, using it as proof that personal AGI is already usable for high-stakes, unfunded, individual problems.
