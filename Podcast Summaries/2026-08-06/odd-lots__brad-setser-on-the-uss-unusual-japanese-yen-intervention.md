# Brad Setser on the US's Unusual Japanese Yen Intervention — Odd Lots

## TL;DR
Brad Setser (Council on Foreign Relations, former Treasury official) joins Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal to unpack the US Treasury's joint intervention with Japan's Ministry of Finance to support a weakening yen — an operation notable for the Fed's FIMA repo facility and Treasury's odd choice to sell euros rather than dollars. Setser explains why the BOJ has been slow to raise rates, why record East Asian current-account surpluses have coincided with historically weak currencies, and why he believes the intervention can work if the Bank of Japan follows through with rate hikes. He also argues Japan's underlying fiscal position — a primary balance near zero and a massive foreign asset cushion — is in better shape than conventional "fiscal dominance" narratives suggest, and notably better than the deteriorating US fiscal trajectory.

## Key points
- The episode opens with hosts discussing a viral photo of Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's to-do list reading "buy Japanese yen, 5-10 billion (JPY)," which confirmed the US had jointly intervened with Japan to support the yen.
- The Bank of Japan has been slow to raise rates (policy rate ~1%, below inflation) partly to avoid snapping back into a zero-rate/zero-inflation mindset, and possibly due to concern about raising funding costs for banks holding low-yielding yen assets and pressure on government fiscal interest costs — though Setser thinks that fiscal concern is somewhat overstated.
- 2026 has seen record current-account surpluses across East Asia (Korea's roughly tripling toward $300-400B, Taiwan's potentially doubling to 25-30% of GDP) driven by AI/chip capex, yet currencies have weakened — a counterintuitive dynamic Setser attributes to different mechanisms per country (Taiwan's central bank managing TSMC's conversions; Korea's concentration-limit-driven equity outflows and pension/ETF dynamics).
- Japan's own fundamentals look "fundamentally okay": a ~5% of GDP current account surplus (driven by investment income, not trade), roughly $1.2 trillion in government reserves, $900B+ in GPIF foreign pension assets, and a foreign asset position near 50% of GDP.
- Speculative yen carry trades existed but weren't the dominant driver, per Setser; more significant were hedging dynamics among "real money" investors (life insurers, GPIF) that had grown progressively less hedged on foreign holdings.
- The US Treasury's stated concern (per Bessent) wasn't the classic "weak yen hurts Detroit" trade-competitiveness argument but rather that yen weakness and Ministry of Finance reserve sales could add pressure to the US Treasury market; joint intervention with Japan was framed partly as reducing that pressure and "helping a friend" (per the President).
- Treasury sold euros instead of dollars partly because Bessent (a former currency trader) wanted to signal this was a yen-specific view rather than a broader anti-dollar stance, and because Treasury's own dollar firepower is limited (ESF has only ~$20B in liquid dollar cash, plus ~$160-170B in SDRs).
- The Fed's FIMA repo facility (Foreign and International Monetary Authorities Repo Facility) lets foreign central banks repo Treasuries for dollars rather than selling them outright; Setser (who pushed for it while at Treasury in 2020) is a fan — it's zero risk to the Fed and gives Japan's MOF flexibility to intervene without immediately dumping Treasuries, though it carries an above-market rate premium and is capped around $60 billion.
- Classical currency valuation models (Big Mac Index/PPP, behavioral equilibrium exchange rate models, current-account-based models) all suggest Asian currencies — including the yen, Korean won, and Chinese yuan — are undervalued, though financial flows have pulled currencies far from these fundamental anchors.
- The Ministry of Finance has effectively drawn a defensive line around 160 yen/dollar (having previously intervened near that level); whether the intervention holds depends heavily on whether the BOJ actually raises rates, especially at its September meeting — a failure to hike would signal a lack of full domestic commitment to defending the currency.
- Setser pushes back on "fiscal dominance" narratives around Japan: Japan's primary balance (excluding interest) is now roughly flat/near-surplus, better than the US, UK, France, and arguably Germany; the government's massive foreign asset holdings mean net interest payments have been close to zero even as debt-to-GDP is high, making Japan's debt dynamics more sustainable than commonly portrayed.
- Setser contrasts this with the US fiscal trajectory, noting the US primary deficit is around 5-6% of GDP and rising (before new defense spending), calling US fiscal policy "way worse than Japanese fiscal policy."
- In the wrap-up, hosts note the lingering influence of Japan's "lost decades" on market psychology, and highlight the tension between Treasury's push to ease pressure on long-term Treasury yields and a more independent-minded Fed under recent leadership commentary.
