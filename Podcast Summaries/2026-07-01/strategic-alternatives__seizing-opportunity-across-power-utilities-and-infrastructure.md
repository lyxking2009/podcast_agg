## Key Points

- Energy security is now the primary driver of investment decisions globally, accelerating an "all of the above" approach to power generation and infrastructure across every form of generation and delivery.
- The war in Iran has amplified energy supply constraint concerns beyond fuel prices to everyday goods — from groceries to plastics, rubber, and solvents — underscoring how deeply energy is embedded in daily life.
- Companies are navigating the "energy trilemma": balancing energy security, energy affordability, and energy transition (clean energy) simultaneously.
- Big tech platforms and hyperscalers are on track to become the largest electricity consumers on the planet, with some operating across country borders — giving them unprecedented power to drive demand growth and deliver demand-side management services for utilities.
- Regulators and governments face pressure to accommodate these "platform nations" or risk losing AI investments and global competitiveness.
- North American utilities are allocating significant capital toward resilience, including modular grids, microgrids, distributed generation, and behind-the-meter solutions to build redundancy against volatility.
- Synthetic technology offers wide-ranging benefits for energy infrastructure efficiency and supply chain security, particularly for rare earth minerals in geographically challenging locations.
- The National Energy Technology Laboratory has developed a self-healing coating to prevent pipeline corrosion, with potential to improve safety of both new and existing pipelines.
- Canada is well-positioned as a long-term winner in global energy supply, with significant conventional energy resources and a democratic, high-standard-of-living society.
- Utilities are expected to form partnerships with high-electricity consumers (such as tech companies) and integrate with domestic mining companies to secure raw materials closer to home.

## Overview

This episode of RBC Capital Markets' Strategic Alternatives podcast examines how rising power demand is reshaping energy development and creating investment opportunities across the power, utilities, and infrastructure sectors. Host Joseph Coletti is joined by Robert Kwan, Head of Global Power, Utilities and Infrastructure Research, and Maurice Choy, Canadian Energy Infrastructure Analyst, both at RBC Capital Markets.

The conversation begins with how geopolitical events — particularly the war in Ukraine and now a war in Iran — have fundamentally shifted energy policy from a rush toward zero-carbon resources to a more balanced "all of the above" approach. Kwan explains that energy supply constraints now have highly visible downstream effects on everyday consumer goods, making energy security a mainstream concern rather than a policy abstraction. Choy introduces the concept of the "energy trilemma," framing the challenge for infrastructure companies as finding equilibrium between energy security, affordability, and the clean energy transition.

A major theme of the discussion is the emergence of "platform nations" — a concept from RBC's Imagine report — referring to hyperscale digital platforms that may soon rival countries in electricity consumption and geopolitical influence. Choy argues that governments and regulators must create policy environments hospitable to these tech giants or risk losing both investment capital and their position in the global AI race. The episode closes with discussion of practical investment responses: utilities investing in decentralized, resilient energy systems; the role of synthetic technology and self-healing pipeline coatings; and Canada's strategic positioning as a reliable conventional energy supplier in an increasingly volatile world.

## Implications

The episode's central message is that energy infrastructure is entering a period of sustained, broad-based investment driven by structural demand growth rather than policy cycles alone. The convergence of AI-driven electricity demand, geopolitical energy security concerns, and aging infrastructure creates durable tailwinds for power generation, transmission, and midstream assets. For investors, the "all of the above" framing means no single generation type is out of favor — conventional, renewable, and nuclear all benefit.

The rise of platform nations as electricity consumers introduces a new category of offtake partner for utilities, potentially enabling more bankable demand contracts and accelerating capital deployment. However, it also raises affordability and regulatory risks as large tech consumers may receive preferential terms at the expense of residential ratepayers. The emphasis on decentralized energy systems and domestic supply chain integration (mining partnerships) signals that capital allocation in this sector will increasingly prioritize resilience and optionality over pure cost efficiency.
