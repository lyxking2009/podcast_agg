## Key Points

- Rosenberg argues productivity now accounts for 90% of US economic growth — versus the historical 50/50 split with labor input — making persistent inflation structurally untenable and setting up a string of surprisingly benign CPI prints ahead.
- Unit labor costs are running at just 0.5% year-over-year (down from 3% a year ago), and without wage-price spiral dynamics the Fed's fear of inflation is misplaced; Rosenberg expects rate hike expectations to be walked back.
- The personal savings rate has collapsed from ~8% pre-COVID to 3% today, falling two percentage points in a single year; real disposable income growth is running at 0%, meaning consumer spending is being fueled entirely by equity wealth effects (high-end) and credit card borrowing (low-end).
- Low-end consumers are under severe stress: credit card usage has reaccelerated dramatically, with a $1.3 trillion unpaid balance at ~20% interest rates and delinquency rates at a two-decade high of 15%.
- "There are no bears left" — portfolio managers are at 1% cash, household balance sheets hold 73% equities, and even well-known former bears like Michael Wilson have turned bullish, which Rosenberg views as a contrary warning signal.
- The AI trade has broadened from Mag 7 into semiconductors, memory, utilities, Corning, Caterpillar, and Cisco — creating concentration risk that now exceeds tech's share at the dot-com peak — while the hyperscalers themselves are rolling over.
- The financialization of the AI buildout — circular financing arrangements (e.g., Blackstone/Apollo's $35B SPV funding Google TPUs used by Anthropic), Nvidia funding its own customers, private credit funds capping redemptions — mirrors the telecom-equipment circularity of the late 1990s.
- When adjusted for the full depreciation allowance effect (where AI capex buyers immediately expense purchases while sellers book full revenue), the S&P's true P/E is closer to 30, not the reported figure; the Shiller CAPE stands at 41 and the equity risk premium is negative.
- Rosenberg draws a direct parallel to Charles Merrill's 1928 letter warning of a bubble — Merrill wanted to raise cash, was told to see a psychiatrist, and ultimately saved the firm by getting out before October 1929.
- Despite his bearish framework, Rosenberg's model portfolio is roughly 50% equities (heavily tilted to Asia), 40% bonds, and 10% gold/miners — and it has returned more than 60% over the past three-plus years.

## Overview

Recorded on the final day of Q2 2026, Dan Nathan sits down with David Rosenberg, president and founder of Rosenberg Research, for a wide-ranging conversation on inflation, the consumer, and a market in which virtually no skeptics remain. Rosenberg opens by debunking the consensus inflation narrative, arguing that the successive cost-push shocks of the past six years — COVID supply-chain disruptions, the Ukraine energy shock, and the recent US-Iran war — were discrete events, not structural. With productivity now driving 90% of US growth (versus a normal 50/50 split with labor), and unit labor costs near zero, Rosenberg sees no wage-price spiral and expects the Fed to reverse its rate-hike talk as a string of benign CPI prints materializes.

The conversation then pivots to the consumer, where Rosenberg surfaces what he considers the most underappreciated risk: the personal savings rate has dropped from 8% pre-COVID to 3% today, entirely masking stagnant real disposable income (running at 0% year-over-year). The high-end consumer is spending embedded equity gains from a 401(k) wealth effect; the low-end is maxing out credit cards at 20% interest rates with a 15% delinquency rate — the highest in two decades. Rosenberg warns that if the savings rate mean-reverts even partway, real consumer spending would collapse from 2%-plus to flat or negative, tipping the economy into a consumer recession that "few see coming."

On markets, Rosenberg is unambiguous: he has never seen sentiment this one-sided. Cash allocations at hedge funds are at 1%, household equity exposure is at an all-time high of 73%, and even perennial bears have capitulated. He invokes Charles Merrill's 1928 letter — Merrill tried to raise cash, was told he needed a psychiatrist, and the psychiatrist sold his own stocks — as a historical mirror for today's positioning. He closes by walking through the financialization of AI infrastructure: circular SPV arrangements, Nvidia funding its own customers, private credit redemption caps, and accounting distortions from accelerated depreciation that may make the S&P's earnings appear 25–30% more robust than they truly are.

## Implications

Rosenberg's analysis implies a significant tail risk that markets are not pricing. A mean reversion in the savings rate, a tightening of credit card lending standards, or a plateau in the equity wealth effect could each individually shove real consumer spending into contraction — collectively they could trigger a consumer-led recession. The AI capex cycle faces a similar dynamic: as hyperscaler capex crowd out free cash flow and ROI evidence stays elusive, the risk of order pullbacks ripples through a chain that now includes utilities, industrial companies, and private credit funds whose collateral integrity is untested.

The negative equity risk premium (real earnings yield ~2.4% vs. real long-bond yield ~2.7%) means the market is effectively treating equities as risk-free. Combined with extreme concentration, zero hedge fund cash, and complex cross-financing arrangements, the setup resembles 1999–2000 more than any prior cycle — with the additional wrinkle that the financial plumbing underneath it (private credit, SPVs, insurance company balance sheets) is more opaque. Rosenberg's positioning in Asia equities, long-duration bonds, and gold reflects a bet that once rate-hike expectations are priced out, the dollar weakens and bonds rally — even while US equities face a reckoning.
