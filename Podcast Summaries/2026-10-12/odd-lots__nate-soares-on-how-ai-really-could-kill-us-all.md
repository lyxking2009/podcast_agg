# Nate Soares on How AI Really Could Kill Us All

**Show:** Odd Lots  
**Published:** 2026-10-12  
**Transcript source:** `rss_omny_srt`

## Overview

Joe Weisenthal (just back from the Curve conference in Berkeley) and Tracy Alloway talk to Nate Soares, a long-time AI safety theorist, about why the standard scientific pattern of learning from failure does not transfer to a technology that could end the run. Soares's argument is not that labs are reckless in the abstract but that they operate in reactive mode — patching problems after they appear — which is exactly the wrong mode for a system that could cross into a regime where it can switch you off before you switch it off. The conversation works through what makes this transition a phase change rather than a gradual drift.

## Key Points

- Soares lays out the core asymmetry: there is a difference between AIs that are safe because if they tried to take over the world they would fail, and AIs that have to be safe because if they tried they would succeed. That crossover is a phase change, not a slope.
- Scientific progress normally proceeds by trial and error — put a new system in a new environment, watch it break in a new way, diagnose, patch, move on. Soares argues this loop fails for a technology that can disable you before you can respond.
- He says AI companies are still in a reactive mode and have not shifted to a proactive one, and that this is not a technology you want to be reactive around.
- Weisenthal pushes back that Anthropic's approach — writing an 80-page constitution encoding Claude's values into the model — is not merely fighting the last war but looking ahead; Soares engages with why that is still not the same as solving the problem.
- The sociological backdrop: at safety conferences, the people building the technology are also the people most anxious about it, and they keep building anyway.
- The lab-side justification Soares describes: someone is going to build it, so why not me — plus the game-theoretic argument that unilateral slowing accomplishes nothing against a competitor or another country.

## Implications

The practical upshot for anyone building or investing around frontier models is that "we will respond when problems appear" is the wrong risk model here, and that safety measures aimed at the current capability level will not automatically extend across the crossover. It also reframes the usefulness of internal constitutions and similar alignment work as necessary but not sufficient.

## Notable Quotes

- “The difference between an AI that can really see a way to become self-sufficient … and the sort of AI that's just being told that in training, that's one of those like, oh, this is a different sort of AI system … Except this time, when the new problem crops up, it's going to be able to turn you off before you turn it off.” — Nate Soares
- “This usual process of like, oh, we're going to respond when the new type of problem arises and put the patch on and react, that doesn't work for a technology that at some point crosses over into the regime where it can wipe you out if there's a problem.” — Nate Soares
- “We have seen that these AI companies are in this reactive mode. We have not seen them get into the proactive mode yet. And this is just not a technology you want to be reactive around.” — Nate Soares

- “They're all deeply anxious about the very thing that they're building, but they just keep building it.” — Joe Weisenthal
