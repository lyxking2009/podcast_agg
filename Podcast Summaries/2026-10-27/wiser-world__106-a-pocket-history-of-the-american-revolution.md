# 106. A Pocket History of the American Revolution — Wiser World

## TL;DR
This episode provides a concise overview of the American Revolution, covering its global context, causes, key events, and consequences. It explains how the 13 British colonies, accustomed to self-government, resisted new taxes after the Seven Years' War, leading to the Declaration of Independence in 1776 and an eight-year war won with crucial French assistance. The episode also highlights the revolution's contradictions—such as the continuation of slavery—and its global impact as a model for independence movements, written constitutions, and large republics.

## Key points
- In the mid-1700s, most of the world was governed by monarchies or empires, but Britain's 13 American colonies had developed strong traditions of local self-government through elected assemblies, though the right to vote was limited.
- Britain's victory in the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) left it with massive debt and a larger empire to administer, prompting Parliament to seek direct taxes from the colonies, starting with the Stamp Act of 1765.
- Colonists objected to direct taxes not primarily because of cost but because they lacked elected representatives in Parliament, crystallizing in the slogan 'No taxation without representation' and leading to the Stamp Act's repeal in 1766.
- Escalating conflicts included the Boston Massacre (1770), when British soldiers killed five protesters; the Boston Tea Party (1773), when colonists dumped 342 chests of tea; and the Intolerable Acts, which closed Boston's port and prompted the First Continental Congress in 1774.
- Armed conflict began in April 1775 at Lexington and Concord, where British troops sent to seize colonial munitions clashed with militiamen, but independence was not yet the universal goal and colonists remained divided between patriots and loyalists.
- Thomas Paine's pamphlet Common Sense (January 1776) shifted opinion by arguing for complete separation from Britain and challenging monarchy itself, using plain language that reached ordinary colonists.
- On July 2, 1776, the Continental Congress voted for independence, and on July 4 it adopted the Declaration of Independence, which asserted natural rights and the principle that governments derive power from the consent of the governed.
- The Declaration's ideals contradicted reality: its principal author Thomas Jefferson and others were enslavers, and the rights proclaimed did not extend to enslaved people, women, or indigenous peoples.
- The Americans initially struggled militarily, but the 1777 victory at Saratoga convinced France to enter an alliance in 1778, providing soldiers, weapons, money, and naval support; Spain and the Dutch Republic also joined against Britain, turning the war into a global conflict.
- The decisive 1781 siege of Yorktown, where American and French forces trapped the British on land and sea, led to surrender, and the 1783 Treaty of Paris formally recognized American independence.
- The first national government under the Articles of Confederation proved too weak: Congress could not tax directly, states retained most power, and Shays' Rebellion (1786) alarmed leaders, leading to the Constitutional Convention of 1787.
- The new Constitution created a stronger national government with separation of powers and checks and balances, and the Bill of Rights was added in 1791 to protect individual liberties; George Washington became the first president in 1789.
- Despite revolutionary ideals, slavery remained legal until 1865, Native Americans faced displacement as settlers expanded westward, and voting rights were limited; however, later movements for abolition, women's rights, and civil rights drew on the founding principles.
- The American Revolution had global impact by providing a model for declarations of independence, popularizing written constitutions, and demonstrating that a large republic could function without a monarch.
