# Brendan Greeley on the Real 500-Year History of the Dollar

## Key Points
- The word "dollar" derives from a 16th-century Bohemian silver coin called the Joachimsthaler, minted from an illegal mine in a valley (German: Thal) and copied across northern Europe.
- When Alexander Hamilton called it the "ancient dollar," the currency was already roughly 250 years old, rooted in Spanish Empire silver from Bolivia and Mexico — not in any act of American monetary sovereignty.
- The Spanish piece-of-eight was essentially a copy of the Joachimsthaler, and this coin circulated as legal currency in America until the 1850s, long after independence.
- There is no single dollar — Greeley counts up to 15 distinct types, including cash, bank dollars, eurodollars, Fed reserves, stablecoins, money market dollars, and check-casher dollars that trade at a discount.
- The value of a dollar rests on assets held on the opposite side of commercial bank balance sheets, not on a vague "fiat" declaration; FDIC insurance and bank regulation are what make dollars reliable.
- Foreign banks have manufactured dollar-denominated claims since the 1950s (eurodollars), meaning the dollar supply has never been fully under US control.
- Fed swap lines — not aircraft carriers — are the most important diplomatic tool the US possesses, backstopping the global dollar system when foreign-created dollars face a panic.
- The "fiat money" framing is intellectually corrosive because it hides the real mechanics of money creation and strips away the analytical importance of bank regulation.
- Colonial and state paper currencies worked reasonably well before the Constitution; Hamilton's solution (handing money creation to banks) was one choice, not an inevitable necessity.
- Financial ledgers are the most reliable historical records because they cannot be falsified the way letters can, and they reveal commercial life in granular detail.

## Overview
Tracy Alloway and Joe Weisenthal welcome Brendan Greeley, contributing editor at the Financial Times and author of *The Almighty Dollar: Five Hundred Years of the World's Most Powerful Money*, who is also completing a PhD in financial history at Princeton. The conversation begins with a deep historical account of how the dollar came to be. The story starts in 1520 in a Bohemian silver valley where an illegal mining operation financed by Saxon investors produced large silver dividend coins rather than the full spectrum of big and small coins required by existing minting laws. This exception produced a coin that became a pan-European trade standard, was copied endlessly, and whose name (Joachimsthaler → taler → dollar) traveled into English, Dutch, and eventually American usage.

Greeley traces the dollar through the Spanish Empire's silver production in Latin America, the role of pieces-of-eight in global trade from China to the Caribbean, and the eventual adoption of the word "dollar" in Shakespeare and in colonial American newspapers. He argues that the founding of the United States involved no monetary sovereignty — the new nation simply pegged itself to an existing global coin it could not control. The silver Spanish dollar continued to circulate in America until the 1850s.

The second half turns to what makes contemporary dollars valuable. Greeley challenges both the "fiat" framing and MMT's tax-drives-currency claim, arguing instead that dollars have value because of the assets on the other side of commercial bank balance sheets, the regulatory infrastructure built after 19th-century banking panics through the FDIC, and the unique depth of the US Treasury market. He describes the eurodollar system as proof that anyone can create a dollar, and frames Fed swap lines as the real foundation of dollar hegemony: the promise that in a crisis, the Fed will make foreign-created dollars whole.

## Implications
Greeley's historical account has direct relevance to contemporary debates about dollar dominance. If the dollar's global status is the product of 500 years of decentralized manufacturing governed by commercial trust and evolving regulation — rather than US government fiat or military power — then that status is both more robust and more fragile than commonly assumed. It is more robust because it does not depend on any single institution; it is more fragile because it depends on regulatory quality, balance-sheet integrity, and the Fed's willingness to act as global lender of last resort, all of which can be degraded.

The episode provides a powerful corrective to magical thinking about money. Whether it is gold-standard nostalgia, MMT, or cryptocurrency maximalism, most popular monetary frameworks skip over the mundane but essential mechanics of bank regulation and asset quality. Greeley's framework implies that financial deregulation is not just a domestic risk but a threat to the entire global dollar architecture, and that debates about "the future of the dollar" cannot be settled without first understanding the 500-year history that got us here.
