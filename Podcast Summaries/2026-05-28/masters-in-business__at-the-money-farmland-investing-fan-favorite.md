# At The Money: Farmland Investing (Fan Favorite)

## Key Points
- Farmland offers a combination of income yield, capital appreciation, and inflation correlation that few other asset classes can match.
- Long-term farmland appreciation has averaged roughly 6% annually over 70 years, driven by inflation plus productivity gains from improving crop technology.
- Only about 3% of US farmland is institutionally owned, indicating a large untapped opportunity for sophisticated investors.
- Sourcing farmland is almost entirely off-market — there is no Zillow for agriculture; deals flow through farmer-tenant networks and private transactions with estates and non-farming heirs.
- The Great Lakes region (Indiana, Michigan, Illinois, Wisconsin, Kentucky, Ohio, western New York) is preferred for strong soils, abundant water, and proximity to East Coast population centers.
- Ancillary income streams — hunting leases, timber, oil/gas royalties, wind turbines, solar, easements, and data center land sales — can multiply base farmland returns dramatically.
- Data center development in the Midwest is currently paying 8 to 20 times farmland value for well-sited parcels with power, water, and fiber access.
- California farmland is less attractive due to water scarcity, regulatory burden, and misaligned incentives around water use for commodity crops.
- Multi-year lease structures (negotiated roughly every three years) provide income stability even when commodity prices decline.
- Climate change is expected to make the Great Lakes region more, not less, attractive for farming relative to drier parts of the country.

## Overview
Host Barry Ritholtz speaks with Brandon Zick, Chief Investment Officer of Sara's (Saratoga) Farmland Fund, which manages approximately $2 billion in agricultural assets. The conversation covers why farmland has emerged as a compelling alternative asset class and how a professional farmland investment operation actually works.

Zick explains that farmland's investment appeal rests on three pillars: stable income from crop rents, long-term capital appreciation driven by inflation and agricultural productivity improvements, and low correlation with traditional financial assets. Rather than operating farms themselves, funds like Sara's lease land to active family farmers. Sourcing deals requires cultivating a farmer-tenant network, since most farmland changes hands through private transactions among estates, trusts, and non-farming heirs — not public listings. The fund attends 200-300 auctions a year but does the majority of its business off-market.

The fund's geographic focus on the Great Lakes states reflects deliberate risk management around water availability and soil quality. Zick discusses the wide spectrum of ancillary value drivers that can sit atop farmland, from modest income sources like hunting leases and timber to transformative opportunities such as solar projects generating 15-20% annual gross yield on cost, or data center developments paying many multiples of agricultural land value. He contrasts this with California, where water scarcity, regulatory constraints, and labor costs make commodity crop farming increasingly unattractive as an investment.

## Implications
Farmland represents an underpenetrated institutional asset class that combines characteristics of real estate, commodities, and infrastructure in a single vehicle. The emerging optionality around data center siting, renewable energy, and pipeline easements means the total return potential extends well beyond traditional agricultural yields — a fact that is only beginning to attract infrastructure capital. For investors, the key takeaway is that farmland can serve simultaneously as an inflation hedge, income generator, and long-duration capital appreciation play with relatively low volatility.

The water dimension of the analysis is particularly forward-looking. As climate change intensifies and aquifer depletion accelerates in the Southwest and Great Plains, regions with natural rainfall and abundant freshwater reserves — particularly around the Great Lakes — stand to appreciate both in agricultural and optionality value. The supply of arable land with reliable water is genuinely finite, and institutional demand for it is just beginning to grow.
