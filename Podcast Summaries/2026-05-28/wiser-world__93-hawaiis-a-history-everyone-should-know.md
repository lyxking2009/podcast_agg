# 93. Hawai'i: A History Everyone Should Know

## Key Points
- Hawai'i's history spans more than 1,500 years of Polynesian settlement, with the islands first populated by voyagers from the Marquesas Islands around 300–600 CE and a second wave from Tahiti around 1000 CE
- Captain James Cook became the first documented European to make contact with the Hawaiian Islands in 1778, an encounter that would ultimately prove fatal for Cook and transformative for Hawaiian society
- King Kamehameha I unified the Hawaiian Islands under a single monarchy between 1795 and 1810 through military conquest, establishing the Kingdom of Hawai'i that would persist for nearly a century
- The introduction of Western diseases devastated the native Hawaiian population, which fell from an estimated 300,000–800,000 at first European contact to fewer than 40,000 by the late 19th century
- American and European missionaries arrived beginning in 1820, converting much of the population and establishing a written form of the Hawaiian language while simultaneously suppressing traditional practices
- Sugar plantation agriculture transformed the Hawaiian economy and demographics from the mid-19th century, driving the importation of contract laborers from China, Japan, Portugal, Korea, and the Philippines
- American business interests, primarily sugar planters, orchestrated the overthrow of Queen Lili'uokalani in 1893 with the support of U.S. Marines, ending the Hawaiian Kingdom
- President Grover Cleveland opposed the annexation as an illegal act and attempted to restore the monarchy, but Congress refused; Hawai'i was formally annexed in 1898 during the Spanish-American War
- The attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, drew the United States into World War II and led to the incarceration of Japanese Americans in Hawai'i and the continental U.S., despite the loyalty demonstrated by the 442nd Infantry Regiment
- Hawai'i became the 50th U.S. state in 1959, but ongoing sovereignty movements among native Hawaiians reflect unresolved questions about the legality and legitimacy of annexation

## Overview
Host Alli Roper guides listeners through the layered and often contested history of the Hawaiian Islands, from the remarkable Polynesian navigation achievements that first settled the archipelago to the complex politics of statehood and sovereignty in the modern era. The episode draws on the show's signature approach — treating history as stories everyone deserves to know, not just travelers preparing for a visit — to examine how a remote island chain became the site of competing colonial, imperial, and indigenous narratives.

The episode traces the arc of the Hawaiian Kingdom's rise and fall in considerable detail. King Kamehameha I's unification drew on both traditional Hawaiian martial culture and newly acquired Western weapons, creating a centralized monarchy that managed to maintain a degree of independence even as European and American influence deepened throughout the 19th century. The reign of Kamehameha's successors saw increasing tensions between the planter class and the Hawaiian throne, culminating in the Bayonet Constitution of 1887, which stripped the monarchy of most executive powers, and the eventual overthrow of Queen Lili'uokalani in 1893.

Roper gives particular attention to the demographic catastrophe that accompanied Western contact: the collapse of the native Hawaiian population due to introduced diseases represents one of the most severe demographic declines in recorded history. The episode contextualizes this alongside the labor importation that made the sugar economy function, creating the multiethnic society that defines contemporary Hawai'i while fundamentally displacing the indigenous population from economic and political power. The final sections examine the path to statehood and the continuing Native Hawaiian sovereignty movement, which argues that annexation was illegal under international law and that the 1993 Apology Resolution by Congress acknowledges this without providing remedy.

## Implications
The history of Hawai'i offers a particularly clear case study in how economic interests — in this instance, sugar — can drive geopolitical outcomes that outlast their original justifications by centuries. The infrastructure of U.S. Pacific military power, including Pearl Harbor, was built on the foundation of an annexation that a sitting U.S. president described as illegal at the time. The episode implicitly raises questions about how the U.S. processes historical injustice when the beneficiary of that injustice is the federal government itself.

For contemporary listeners, the Native Hawaiian sovereignty movement is not historical footnote but active political reality: debates about the Akaka Bill, the Mauna Kea telescope protests, and federal recognition of Native Hawaiians as an indigenous people continue to play out within the framework this history created. Understanding that framework changes how one reads current headlines from the fiftieth state.
