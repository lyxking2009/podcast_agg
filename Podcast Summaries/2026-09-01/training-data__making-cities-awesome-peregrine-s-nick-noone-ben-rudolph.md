# Making Cities Awesome: Peregrine’s Nick Noone & Ben Rudolph — Training Data

## TL;DR
Peregrine cofounders Nick Noone and Ben Rudolph discuss their company's mission to make cities awesome by building AI that integrates data cities already own, rather than collecting new data. They share their background in forward deployed engineering and humanitarian work, how they got started by cold-calling the San Pablo Police Department after more than two dozen rejections, and their philosophy of data sovereignty and privacy-first approach. The conversation covers specific use cases like semantic search for investigations, a cold case agent that exonerated a wrongly convicted man, and long-horizon agents for data integration.

## Key points
- Peregrine is fundamentally different from data collection companies like Flock or Axon: it does not collect new data via sensors or manual input, but instead integrates and joins the data cities already own, providing secure, governed access to improve decision-making.
- Nick Noone previously ran Palantir's SOCOM unit, deploying to high-stakes intelligence operations in the Middle East; Ben Rudolph worked for the UN Refugee Agency (UNHCR) and later Dimagi on healthcare solutions in under-resourced areas. Their combined experiences shaped Peregrine's focus on serving cities.
- The company was founded on February 26, 2018, when the San Pablo Police Department finally granted them access after more than two dozen rejections. They initially connected with Commander Brian Bubar, who led Operation Red Reach, a major cross-jurisdictional narcotics investigation in Northern California.
- Forward deployed engineering, as practiced by Peregrine, means owning or co-owning the customer's problem, delivering outcomes 3-5 times faster than typical teams, and taking pride in the customer's win rather than claiming credit. This requires suspending ego and practicing high-empathy, patient work.
- Peregrine's data governance philosophy is that each customer institution owns its own data, which is ultimately the community's data. The company provides permission controls and robust sharing capabilities so that data is not overshared, and organizations can securely collaborate when needed.
- One notable use case involved a county in Florida that performed over 100 water rescues. Using Peregrine's AI, they discovered that a specific weather pattern occurring for three consecutive days created sand channels and rip currents, leading to the rescues—a pattern that had never happened before.
- Another use case was a detective investigating threats against a synagogue. Using semantic search, the detective was able to find other antisemitic threats against the synagogues by understanding the meaning of the data, which would have been nearly impossible with keyword searches alone.
- Peregrine built a cold case agent that can analyze 200-300 GB of evidence (videos, audio, PDFs) and reproduce the results of a case where a man was wrongly convicted and later exonerated. In a Wisconsin county, the agent identified a suspect by finding a cell tower ping that placed him at the scene and where the body was found.
- Approximately 90% of Peregrine's data integration Python notebooks are now written by agents, supervised by deployment strategists. These long-horizon agents run for hours, analyze databases, understand ontologies, and spawn subagents to complete integration tasks.
- On facial recognition, Peregrine does not impose a general-purpose policy. Instead, they help customers understand context, legal constraints, and community norms so that each city can make its own informed decision. Most public safety agencies and communities in America choose not to implement facial recognition.
- Peregrine's deployment strategists are seen as R&D and growth drivers, not a cost center. The company trusts them to innovate at the edges, using an analogy of sending someone into a dark cave with tools and a rope, where innovation happens at the farthest fringes of the organization.
- Nick Noone believes the path to serving 10,000 cities requires a fundamental infrastructure operating model that preserves each city's uniqueness, with a moral compass and integrity. They have lowered the price point to below $1 million per year, which was previously impossible for similar solutions, enabling them to serve underdog organizations.
