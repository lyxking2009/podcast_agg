# John Deere CEO: Farming's Future, Autonomous Tractors and AI in the Field — In Good Company with Nicolai Tangen

## TL;DR
John Deere CEO John May discusses the company's transformation from an equipment manufacturer to a technology-driven provider of precision agriculture solutions. He details AI-powered computer vision that cuts herbicide use by 60% and nitrogen costs by two-thirds, autonomous tractors with a goal of fully autonomous corn production by 2030, and a shift to recurring revenue models. May also addresses farm income pressures, right-to-repair resolutions, and the role of data, culture, and climate volatility in shaping the future of farming.

## Key points
- John May became CEO of John Deere in 2019 and immediately launched the 'Smart Industrial' strategy, shifting the company's focus from selling the best equipment to helping customers achieve better planting, spraying, and harvesting outcomes through technology and production-system alignment.
- Deere acquired Blue River, an AI company, and applied its computer vision technology to agriculture, enabling machines to distinguish between healthy plants and weeds and spray only weeds, reducing herbicide application by 60%.
- Using computer vision, Deere's planting technology can target nitrogen application directly to each seed, cutting nitrogen fertilizer costs by approximately two-thirds and reducing environmental impact.
- Deere launched fully autonomous battery-electric equipment and a commercial autonomous tillage solution, with plans to roll autonomy across all production steps, and has set a goal of fully autonomous corn production in the US by 2030, covering tillage, planting, spraying, and harvesting without an operator.
- Autonomy allows equipment to run 24 hours a day and frees farmers from the cab to focus on higher-value tasks such as planning, monitoring multiple machines, and field-level optimization.
- The company is shifting toward recurring revenue models, including pay-as-you-go and SaaS offerings, which allow farmers to pay per acre or application; take rates for this technology doubled year over year.
- May attributes current farm income challenges to global trade disruptions, including Russia's exit from certain markets, the Ukraine war, and the Iran situation affecting fertilizer and fuel flows, but notes supply and demand are balanced and predicts 2026 as the bottom of the cycle.
- To stabilize the equipment market, Deere intentionally underproduces retail demand to reduce used-equipment inventories and bring market balance.
- After right-to-repair disputes, Deere introduced an AI-backed diagnostic tool that lets customers plug into their equipment like a dealer technician, diagnose failures, order parts, and choose between self-repair or dealer service.
- Since 2012, Deere has collected machine and agronomic data via telematics into its cloud-based Operations Center, now with over one million connected machines and 15 years of data; customers can share data with third parties and use AI tools to answer questions like 'What if I planted a different hybrid?'
- Deere connects crop protection and seed companies, such as Bayer and Syngenta, to farmer data (with consent) so they can write prescriptions that are sent directly to the planter, automating hybrid selection, seed volume, and spacing.
- To accelerate innovation, May restructured the company from a traditional matrix with product lines and regions to production systems (corn, soybean, wheat, cotton, sugar) and reduced management layers from 11 to 7, enabling faster decision-making and deeper customer focus.
- May emphasizes that climate volatility is the biggest challenge farmers face today, with more extreme weather patterns and shifting crop regions; Deere's precision technologies help farmers be better stewards of soil and water while cutting costs.
- Having grown up in Caribou, Maine, and paying for college via Pell Grants, May advises young people to be willing to relocate and take on new responsibilities, as such experiences build better leaders.
