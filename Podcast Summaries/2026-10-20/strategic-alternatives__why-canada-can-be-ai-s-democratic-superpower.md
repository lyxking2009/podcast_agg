# Why Canada can be AI’s democratic superpower — Strategic Alternatives

## TL;DR
Cohere co-founder Aidan Gomez, Canada's Minister of AI Evan Solomon, and RBC CEO Dave McKay discuss Canada's potential to become a democratic AI superpower amid US-China competition. They argue Canada must retain sovereign control of frontier AI, with Cohere as a strategic national champion, and that the world needs a second democratic AI supplier. Key themes include AI safety, selective access to advanced cyber tools, and workforce disruption, with RBC noting product cycles compressing from years to months.

## Key points
- Aidan Gomez argues that AI regulation should include pre-deployment testing by independent third parties not tied to any single country or industry player, and warns that large incumbents may use regulation as a moat to entrench dominance and stifle competitors.
- RBC CEO Dave McKay states that current guardrails are insufficient, noting that advanced cyber-defense tools like Mythos are released selectively outside the US, creating sovereign risk and an unfair playing field where US firms get preferential access to the most powerful tools, heightening cyber vulnerability for other nations.
- Canada is one of only four countries with frontier large language models: the US, China, France (Mistral), and Canada (Cohere). The Canadian government has declared Cohere a strategic asset through an MOU and contracts, with RBC also contracting with Cohere; the government aims to 'champion our champions.'
- Canada is the birthplace of generative AI: Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio won the Nobel Prize for work underpinning modern AI, and Canadians co-founded OpenAI, Anthropic, and Cohere. Aidan Gomez recalls his first day at Google Brain hearing Quebecois French from Samy Bengio and others, underscoring Canada's foundational role.
- The world is diversifying AI supply chains away from a single source, but currently risks sleepwalking into a duopoly where only the US and China build AI. Aidan Gomez argues that a second democratic rail must come from Canada (Cohere) or France (Mistral), and Canada should fight to retain control over its AI development and deployment.
- Dave McKay highlights that AI development cycles are compressing dramatically: what once took two years to build now takes two to three months, and a new product expected for late 2027/2028 could be in market by early 2027. This acceleration raises concerns about job displacement and societal adaptation.
- On workforce impact, Dave McKay rejects the claim that 50% of white-collar jobs could be erased in a year or two, instead describing a massive pivot where jobs will change, workers will need to be flexible, and tasks will be reimagined. He suggests a safety net and pacing to bring society along, noting that if society opts out, benefits are lost.
- Minister Evan Solomon says Canada's AI strategy rests on trust, opportunity, and sovereign control, and the framework is 'build right or build wrong' rather than 'build or not build.' He emphasizes safety as the number one concern and the need for social license to build data centers.
- The panel collectively stresses that Canada has all ingredients—talent, market, capital, natural resources—to build a global AI champion despite being one-tenth the US size; they argue it's a critical moment and Canada must move quickly to capitalize.
