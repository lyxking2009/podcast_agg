# This CPO regrets that product management exists | Tom Verrilli (CPO of Whatnot) — Lenny's Podcast: Product | Career | Growth

## TL;DR
Tom Verrilli, CPO of Whatnot, shares his contrarian view that product management is often over-hired, leading to infantilized engineers and designers. At Whatnot, they 'regret that product management exists' and only hire when a specific need arises, with only one external PM hire from over 31,000 applicants in two years. He advocates for senior leaders doing IC work, top-down alignment, and leveraging AI for data analysis and codebase understanding. The conversation covers how PM skills are shifting away from politics toward deep customer and technical insight, and why fewer, more senior PMs can drive better outcomes.

## Key points
- Whatnot’s product team operates on the premise that they 'regret that product management exists,' meaning PMs are hired only for specific, high-leverage needs rather than being assigned to every team. This avoids infantilizing engineers and designers, who are capable of making product decisions but lose that muscle when a PM always babysits them.
- Hiring ratios based on pods (e.g., one PM per six engineers) led to an oversupply of PMs, many of whom specialized in politics and alignment rather than building. Tom notes a anti-pattern: candidates who emphasize stakeholder management over technical or customer insight.
- At Whatnot, PMs are assigned to problems or outcomes during biannual planning, not permanently to teams. This allows PMs to move where needed and keeps them from becoming stale, while also giving engineering and design teams chances to own product decisions.
- Tom sees a shift toward IC work for PMs: even directors and VPs spend most of their time on hands-on product work (e.g., writing specs, analyzing data, talking to customers). This keeps them connected to ground truth and avoids the 'promoted out of doing things' trap. He compares it to wanting Messi on the field rather than coaching the academy.
- AI has dramatically increased PM leverage: tools like Hex Threads enable rapid data science without waiting for data scientists; querying the codebase via Claude replaces bothering engineers; and real-time bug identification during user sessions accelerates feedback loops. This allows fewer, more senior PMs to cover more ground.
- The most valuable PM skills are the classic ones: understanding customers, business, and technology, and translating between them. However, many PMs have spent recent years developing 'product theater'—storytelling, framework presentations, and alignment—rather than these core skills, making them less effective in a world where building is cheaper and faster.
- Tom advocates for 'verify then trust' leadership, where executives clear their calendar to dive deep into problems alongside ICs. This top-down, detail-oriented approach works when leaders have good judgment and stay connected to ground truth, but can become micromanagement if they lack real understanding.
- A key failure pattern Tom has experienced is relying on averages without understanding individual user impacts. Removing a feature used by only 3% of users can destroy the core workflow for a crucial subset, causing cascading negative network effects. He learned to always look beneath averages.
- Tom's 'play the accordion' mental model describes the rhythm of zooming out to define the big picture (pulling the accordion out) and then zooming in to build and ship the smallest version to learn (pushing it in), repeating based on data. This balances long-term vision with iterative experimentation.
- Working with a strong founder-CEO like Grant requires understanding that it's the founder's company, not competing for vision. Tom advises being curious, asking 'Are you open to feedback?' and bringing data to debates. He often steps back from areas where the founder is deeply involved to avoid the 'two dads problem.'
